TEL AVIV—Hamas handed over the bodies of four more hostages to Israel on Tuesday, after both sides accused each other of violating the cease-fire that was part of the deal that released all 20 living detainees from Gaza.

Israel said it was cutting the humanitarian aid promised under the agreement to increase pressure on Hamas to return more deceased detainees. Later the Israeli military and the internal security agency said that coffins containing the remains of four hostages had been transferred to the Red Cross.

Mediators were still negotiating with the U.S.-designated terrorist group over releasing a few more bodies on Wednesday. The latest transfer means that the bodies of eight of the 28 dead hostages are now back in Israel.

Israelis celebrated the return of the living hostages on Monday, in what for many marked an end to the two-year Gaza war. But families of deceased hostages who are supposed to be returned to Israel as part of President Trump’s 20-point plan for peace said they were angered that only four bodies had initially been returned. 

In separate incidents on Tuesday, Hamas said several Gaza residents were killed by the Israeli military, calling it a violation of the cease-fire agreement.

COGAT, the Israeli military unit in charge of aid and other civilian affairs in Gaza and the West Bank, told humanitarian agencies on Tuesday that the number of trucks carrying humanitarian aid into the enclave would be reduced after it said Hamas had violated the cease-fire deal regarding the release of the bodies.

Quick Summary





China believes the U.S. economy can’t sustain a prolonged trade conflict, aiming to exploit President Trump’s focus on the stock market.

In its trade standoff with Washington, Beijing thinks it has found America’s Achilles’ heel: President Trump’s fixation on the stock market.

China’s leader, Xi Jinping, is betting that the U.S. economy can’t absorb a prolonged trade conflict with the world’s second-largest economy, according to people close to Beijing’s decision-making. China is holding a firm line because of its conviction, the people said, that an escalating trade war will tank markets, as it did in April after Trump announced his Liberation Day tariffs, prompting Beijing to hit back.

China expects that the prospect of another market meltdown ultimately will force Trump to negotiate at an expected summit with Xi late this month, the people said.

Beijing continued playing hardball this week, escalating the trade fight Monday by sanctioning the U.S. units of South Korean shipping company Hanwha Ocean. The move whipsawed U.S. markets on Tuesday, triggering a sharp early selloff as hopes for easing tensions faded, before major indexes partially rebounded and steadied in the afternoon.

Last week, Beijing imposed restrictions on the export of rare-earth minerals, which are vital to consumer electronics and the tech industry. Trump then threatened additional 100% tariffs on China starting Nov. 1.

Both sides have vacillated between tough talk and de-escalation in recent days, but the rhetoric took a harder turn on Tuesday. China’s Commerce Ministry accused the U.S. of “double standards” regarding tariff threats and it vowed that China would “fight to the end” in the trade dispute.

=== # The Disarmament Ultimatum ===

# The Disarmament Ultimatum

The briefing came at dawn from Jerusalem, delivered with the matter-of-fact tone that crisis reporters adopt when describing the intolerable. Three more bodies had been released overnight by Hamas. Egyptian teams would soon enter Gaza to help locate additional remains. Among the dead was Evgenia Shirabi, whose family spoke of sad closure and the consolation of having a grave to visit after two years of agonized waiting.

President Trump's message to Hamas was unambiguous in its brutality: "We have told them we want disarmed and they will disarm or we will disarm them and it will happen quickly and perhaps violently." He claimed to have spoken directly with Hamas representatives, extracting what he characterized as their agreement. Whether this conversation occurred as described, and whether any such commitment would hold, remained open questions.

The reporter on the ground noted a dangerous pattern emerging in Gaza itself. Hamas had begun executing Palestinians accused of stealing aid or collaborating with Israel. The terrorist organization was reasserting control in the streets even as the ceasefire held, a troubling indicator for anyone hoping the group might voluntarily relinquish power.

The proposed peace plan outlined ambitious goals: Hamas leadership would leave Gaza, weapons would be surrendered, amnesty might follow for those who complied. International forces would deploy to stabilize the territory. Aid would flow in substantial quantities. A moderate technocratic government would emerge, focused on reconstruction rather than conflict. Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu made clear his country would resume military operations if Hamas failed to comply.

But the timeline remained undefined, and the images of Hamas militants in the streets suggested the terrorist organization had no intention of simply fading away.

Twenty living hostages had been released, reuniting with families after two years in captivity. In synagogues, congregants stood and cheered. Videos showed emotional reunions at hospitals. The joy was real and profound, tempered only by the knowledge that recovery from such trauma would require far more than a few months. One former hostage's relative, speaking from experience, warned that severe psychological wounds don't heal in days.

The same person offered an unsurprising observation about Hamas's recent executions of rivals: Israelis had long understood this about the organization. It would do anything to maintain control of Gaza, respecting no boundaries in pursuit of that goal. The question wasn't whether Hamas wanted to disarm—clearly they did not—but whether anyone possessed the will and capability to force the issue.

And therein lay the essential problem. Reconstruction requires stability. Humanitarian aid requires security. Neither is possible when a terrorist organization controls the streets and executes its opponents. International forces might deploy, moderate governments might be proposed, but absent the genuine disarmament of Hamas, all such plans existed only as aspirational documents. The gap between what the peace plan envisioned and what Hamas's behavior suggested remained vast, perhaps unbridgeable without the violent confrontation Trump had threatened.

The celebration of returned hostages coexisted uneasily with this reality. Families had their loved ones back, a mercy after two years of captivity. But the larger question—whether this ceasefire represented a genuine path toward peace or merely a temporary pause before the next eruption of violence—remained unanswered in the predawn briefings from Jerusalem.

=== # The Dietary Guidelines War ===

# The Dietary Guidelines War

**How Big Food and Big Pharma Are Keeping America Sick—And What We Can Do About It**

I'm watching Nina Teicholz join the video call, and I'm thinking about how insane this all is. Here's a woman who wrote *The Big Fat Surprise*, a massive bestseller that opened millions of people's eyes to how we've been lied to about nutrition for decades. And now she's working behind the scenes in Washington, trying to convince government officials to do something that should be obvious: give sick people a dietary option that actually works.

The U.S. Dietary Guidelines for Americans. Maybe you've seen them—the food pyramid, then "MyPlate," all those colorful graphics telling you to eat six to seven servings of grains a day, including three servings of refined grains. Every day. Oh, and five and a half teaspoons of seed oils. And up to 10 percent of your calories as sugar. For kids? There's literally no limit on sugar in school lunches.

"Welcome, Nina," I say as she gets situated on screen. "Tell us what's going on with the current administration and the dietary guidelines."

She doesn't waste time. "The U.S. dietary guidelines are the most influential policy on nutrition," she explains. "It's the key lever affecting what people think is a healthy diet and what they're fed in school lunches, feeding programs for the elderly, what the military is taught to eat."

Let that sink in. The military has an obesity problem almost equal to the general population. Our fighting forces are metabolically sick because of the food they're being told to eat.

## The System Is Rigged

Nina lays out how the process works. Every five years, a committee revisits the guidelines. This committee—and here's where it gets good—has significant ties to the big food industry. She should know. Her organization did the first-ever systematic review of conflicts of interest on the 2020 dietary guidelines committee.

The results? Ninety-five percent of the twenty-person committee had at least one tie to food or pharmaceutical companies. Half had thirty or more such ties. One member had 152 connections to food and pharma companies.

One hundred and fifty-two.

But it gets better. There was literally an employee of the company that makes medical formula supplements serving on the committee. If you believe that supplement-based formula is the solution for obesity, you're not exactly invested in a food-based solution, are you?

"So how much impact do the big food lobbyists have?" I ask, though I already know the answer is going to make me angry.

Nina tells me about the "stakeholders"—the polite Washington term for lobbyists. The soy farmers, wheat farmers, corn farmers. The ultra-processed food manufacturers: Nestlé, Unilever, Danone, Kellogg's, General Mills. These companies show up again and again in virtually every nutrition policy process in Washington.

## The Secret They Buried

Then Nina drops a bomb. She filed Freedom of Information Act requests to get emails from the 2015 dietary guideline process. What she found was stunning: they did a secret review of low-carb diets. The overwhelming evidence showed that low-carb outperformed low-fat for weight loss.

A Harvard professor on the committee said it was important, powerful data. People would accuse them of ignoring it.

So what did the USDA officials do?

They buried it. Literally buried the evidence.

In 2020, they tried again. Despite tens of thousands of public comments demanding a review of low-carbohydrate diets—the most commented-upon topic in the public comment period—they somehow couldn't find a single study on low-carb. Not one. Even though there were over a thousand studies available.

In the Biden administration's review, they did computer modeling instead of looking at actual clinical trials on humans. They removed all the carbohydrates from a diet model and didn't replace them with anything. Then they declared the resulting starvation diet "nutritionally deficient."

No kidding. You'd be starving to death.

## The Real Cost

Here's what forty-five years of these dietary guidelines have accomplished:

When the guidelines launched in 1980, obesity rates were below 12 percent. Type 2 diabetes was rare. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease was uncommon. The population was generally healthy.

Now? Eighty-eight percent of American adults have at least one marker of metabolic disease. Type 2 diabetes in children—which didn't exist forty-five years ago—is now common. Same with fatty liver disease in kids.

We're spending $1 trillion on healthcare, with 85 to 90 percent going to treat and manage chronic diseases. A third of military recruits don't qualify, mainly due to obesity and being on medications.

And what are kids getting for breakfast in schools? Individual serving sizes of Froot Loops. Lucky Charms. Dora the Explorer cereal. Fruit juice that's basically sugar water.

All USDA-approved.

## The Propaganda Machine

When evidence for low-carb diets gets too strong to ignore, the propaganda kicks in. The American College of Cardiology—largely funded by drug companies—launched a press campaign claiming low-carb diets increase your risk of death. Their evidence? A slide presentation at their annual conference. No paper, no peer review, nothing. Just headlines around the world about how keto kills you.

The actual paper didn't come out for another year and a half.

Just a few weeks ago, every science website ran headlines about how keto makes you age prematurely. Read past the headline and you find it's a mouse study checking markers we don't even use in humans.

Meanwhile, there are over a thousand research studies in actual human beings showing low-carb and ketogenic diets work for metabolic disease. More research than the Mediterranean diet, which everyone endorses.

## The Opportunity

But here's why Nina agreed to come on: there's hope. For the first time in decades, there's an administration willing to consider changing the guidelines. Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s team is taking the lead, and they've rejected the Biden administration's expert report.

FDA Commissioner Marty Makary has publicly stated he doesn't believe we got it right on saturated fat. The scientific consensus has evolved over the last fifteen years showing the data never supported limiting saturated fats—which is what keeps red meat, whole milk, and eggs off school breakfast tables.

Nina's been working behind the scenes, and she tells me that at the highest levels—the very highest—there's consideration of including language that allows for a low-carbohydrate diet for people with metabolic diseases.

Just a sentence or two. An option. Not mandatory, just an option.

It sounds small, but it would change everything. If the U.S. government says low-carb is a safe and effective option, it breaks the dam worldwide. Every country that copy-pastes U.S. guidelines will have to deal with it.

## What You Can Do

"This is our moment," Nina says. "I've been pushing for this for a decade, and I've never seen an administration so open to non-status quo science. This is it. We ought not to waste it."

She's created a website: lowcarbmaha.org. There are downloadable graphics, talking points, and names of people to tag on social media—especially X (formerly Twitter), where this administration lives.

"The lobbyists are in D.C. every day," Nina explains. "They're the permanent residents. They're taking officials out to dinner and lunch, having meetings. We have to do what we can, and that's getting loud on social media."

If they hear from two or three hundred people, nothing happens. But if half a million Americans speak up? That makes a difference.

## The Bottom Line

This isn't partisan. This is about the health of every American child being fed Froot Loops for breakfast in school. Every teenager developing type 2 diabetes. Every adult suffering from preventable, reversible chronic disease. Every soldier who can't serve because they're metabolically sick.

Big Food profits on the front end by selling ultra-processed garbage. Big Pharma profits on the back end with pills and injections for the diseases that garbage causes. Meanwhile, our kids, our military, our families get sicker and sicker.

The poorest children in America don't have a lobbyist at the table in Washington. You have to be their voice. Download those graphics. Tag those officials. Share your story about how low-carb, keto, or carnivore changed your health.

Be loud. Be consistent. Be respectful, but be relentless.

Because if we don't speak up now, we're looking at another forty-five years of the same guidelines that created this disaster.

And I don't know about you, but I'm not okay with that.

Belichick’s hand-picked general manager, long-time National Football League executive Mike Lombardi, spent $13 million of Carolina’s revenue-sharing money but ultimately failed to deliver much (if any) NFL-caliber talent, a fact that was obvious to some observers even before the 2025 season started.

Belichick’s choice for offensive coordinator — perhaps the most important hire for a head coach with a defensive background — also has been a disaster. Freddie Kitchens, a Mack Brown holdover with minimal experience as a coordinator or play-caller during his 27-year coaching career, has directed the Atlantic Coast Conference’s most anemic unit, including an inept and seemingly directionless passing game.

At his weekly press conference, which had an extremely unusual, star-studded list of attendees on Monday, Belichick both reinforced his personal commitment to UNC and reminded fans of his process- and culture-oriented approach to building the Tar Heels’ program.

“Everybody’s most interested in the final score, and I’m at the top of that list, but it’s a process,” Belichick said. “You build a culture, you build a program, and eventually the results will come. When will that happen? Hopefully, as soon as possible. We’re working hard to get there.”

In theory, an unusual schedule quirk that included two open dates in the past three weeks should enable the Tar Heels to improve enough to have at least a chance against most of the teams in this latter portion of their fairly manageable schedule: at Cal, Virginia, at Syracuse, Stanford, at Wake Forest, Duke, at NC State.

The Bears, while 4-2, were blown out by their two most accomplished opponents, San Diego State (34-0) and Duke (45-21).

In the Blue Devils’ victory over Cal, during a game played in Berkeley, they trailed 21-7 early in the second quarter. But Duke’s players never panicked, never pointed fingers, never let their attitude or effort wane, and the Devils scored the next 38 points in a row against a Cal team with mediocre line play and a talented but error-prone true freshman at quarterback.

That kind of poise, togetherness and ultimate success through adverse circumstances has a chance of happening only if there’s a rock-solid trust factor between players and coaches and an underlying pride and mutual respect among the players themselves.

Is this UNC squad, with so many first-year coaches and 70 new players, and amidst credible reports of locker-room division, capable of such things?

It’s a fundamental part of Belichick’s job description to make sure that’s the case, even in a year when the Tar Heels obviously have a below-average talent base and now a losing record.


UNC receiver Jordan Shipp is one of the team’s few returning players from 2024 and has emerged not only as one of Carolina’s better offensive options, but as a vocal leader of how the team needs to be playing. (Photo via UNC Athletic Communications/Jeffrey A. Camarati.)

In the professional ranks, most multi-million-dollar athletes will continue to play hard, even for a losing team, because they know their future paychecks directly or indirectly depend on the perception of their effort level and productivity. Video doesn’t lie, and half-hearted attempts rank among the ultimate sins in the eyes of coaches and general managers.

At the college level, things are inherently different in that regard, among many others. Most seniors simply aren’t good enough to play professionally, and most realize that fact. Underclassmen, especially in the transfer portal era, can be vulnerable to checking out mentally and emotionally and looking forward to a fresh start somewhere else next season.

Carolina’s players are staring in the face of true tests of their attitude, effort and competitive spirit, and for most of those players there’s a completely different incentive structure than the one Belichick observed for his almost five decades in the NFL.

Regardless of the final score at Cal or even their ultimate record this season, the 2025 Tar Heels will serve as an intriguing litmus test for Belichick.

If they stay engaged and show improvement, it will reflect well on their coach.

If things get worse, and their attitude and effort levels decline, it will reflect very poorly on their coach.

In the latter scenario, either Belichick will quickly grasp the unique nature of the college game and make the necessary adjustments with his general manager, his coaching staff and his youthful locker room, or it would be best for all involved if he found something else to do.
