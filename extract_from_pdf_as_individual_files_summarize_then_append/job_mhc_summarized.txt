# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job: Introduction Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Job as **"a monument of primitive theology,"** emphasizing that the book demonstrates fundamental truths of natural religion through divine inspiration. He argues that Job represents authentic history rather than mere allegory, noting that "we are sure that it is, for the substance of it, a true history, and not a romance, though the dialogues are poetical."

The commentary establishes Job's **antiquity as a key theological point**, placing him in the patriarchal age contemporary with Isaac and Jacob. Henry notes that God is called "Shaddai—the Almighty, above thirty times in this book," indicating the pre-Mosaic era when divine knowledge was "conveyed, not by writing, but by tradition."

**Central to Henry's interpretation is Job as a type of Christ.** He writes that "Job was a great sufferer, was emptied and humbled, but in order to his greater glory. So Christ abased himself, that we might be exalted." This typological reading sees Job's suffering and restoration as prefiguring Christ's passion and glorification.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry identifies **five practical benefits** from studying Job:

1. **Understanding divine attributes**: The book showcases God's "unsearchable wisdom, irresistible power, inconceivable glory, inflexible justice, and incontestable sovereignty" through sustained theological discourse.

2. **Recognizing universal access to God**: Job demonstrates **"Gentile piety"** - that "in every nation he that fears God and works righteousness is accepted of him" (Acts 10:35), proving God's acceptance extends beyond ethnic Israel.

3. **Solving providential mysteries**: Henry calls Job **"an exposition of the book of Providence,"** particularly addressing why the righteous suffer while the wicked prosper.

4. **Modeling patient endurance**: Job exemplifies "passive fortitude" and "close adherence to God in the midst of the sorest calamities."

## Structure and Cross-References

Henry outlines the book's structure: Job's initial sufferings (chapters 1-3), the debate with three friends, Elihu's moderation (chapters 32-37), God's direct intervention (chapters 38-41), and Job's restoration (chapter 42).

**Key cross-references** include:
- **Ezekiel 14:14** (confirming Job's historical existence alongside Noah and Daniel)
- **James 5:11** and **1 Corinthians 3:19** (apostolic references validating canonical authority)
- **Genesis 22:21** (connecting Job to Nahor's lineage through Uz)

## Notable Insights

Henry suggests **Elihu as the likely author** of the dialogues, while Moses possibly wrote the narrative framework, based on the distribution of divine names throughout the text. He emphasizes that despite difficult passages requiring critical scholarship, "enough is plain to make the whole profitable, and it was all written for our learning."

The commentary concludes that Job teaches "many are the afflictions of the righteous, but when the Lord delivers them out of them all the trial of their faith will be found to praise, and honour, and glory."

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Job Chapter 1

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry establishes Job as a profound example of godly suffering, emphasizing that **"no prosperity can secure us"** from affliction, **"but through which integrity and uprightness will preserve us."** He presents Job's "gospel perfection" as sincerity rather than sinlessness, noting that Job was "perfect" not in being without sin, but in having **"a respect to all God's commandments, aiming at perfection."**

The commentary highlights God's universal providence, observing that **"God has his remnant in all places, sealed ones out of every nation"** - demonstrating that divine grace extends beyond Israel. Henry interprets Job's prosperity as compatible with true piety, arguing that godly character can actually enhance worldly success: **"piety and charity are ordinarily the surest ways of thriving."**

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living. He emphasizes the importance of **family spiritual leadership**, showing Job's **"pious concern for the spiritual welfare of his children"** through regular intercession and sacrifices. Parents should maintain **"godly jealousy"** over their families' spiritual condition.

Regarding wealth, Henry teaches that material prosperity should enhance rather than hinder spiritual devotion, noting that Job's abundance gave him **"so much greater opportunity of doing good."** He warns against the spiritual dangers of feasting and prosperity, observing that **"nothing alienates the mind more from God than the indulgence of the flesh."**

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry defines Job's perfection as sincerity with **"his heart sound and his eye single"** - emphasizing authenticity over sinlessness. The "fear of God" becomes **"the principle that governed his whole conversation."**

**Verses 2-3**: Henry notes the blessing of children in proper proportion (**"more of the more noble sex"**) and explains why livestock rather than land measured wealth in that era - representing **"service and present use more than...show and state."**

**Verses 4-5**: Henry praises the siblings' unity (**"Behold, how good and how pleasant it is!"**) while highlighting Job's wisdom in allowing young people appropriate liberty while maintaining spiritual oversight through **"sanctification"** rituals.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout: **Revelation 7:9** (God's universal remnant), **Isaiah 33:15** (uprightness), **Proverbs 8:13** and **16:6** (fear of the Lord departing from evil), **Psalm 127:3** (children as God's heritage), **Ecclesiastes 5:11** (vanity of multiplied possessions), **Psalm 133:1** (brotherly unity), and **Proverbs 30:9** (dangers of prosperity leading to spiritual forgetfulness).

These references demonstrate Henry's theological method of interpreting Job within the broader biblical narrative of God's dealings with humanity across all nations and circumstances.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job Chapter 2 as a profound study in divine sovereignty and Satan's persistent malice. The central theological tension revolves around God's permissive will allowing Satan to intensify Job's trials while maintaining ultimate control. Henry emphasizes that "malice is restless" - Satan, despite his previous failure, returns with renewed accusations, demonstrating the relentless nature of spiritual opposition.

The commentary highlights God's role as both judge and advocate, with the Lord himself defending Job's character: "Still he holds fast his integrity." Henry sees this as evidence of divine grace's power, noting that Job "gets ground" rather than losing it through temptation. This reinforces the theme that true faith strengthens under pressure.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers. He encourages patience during physical suffering, noting that "while God continues to us our life and health...we should the more patiently bear the loss of other comforts." He warns that physical pain is particularly challenging to faith: "nothing is more likely to ruffle the thoughts and put the mind into disorder than acute pain."

The commentary emphasizes constancy as faith's crown, showing how Job's unwavering integrity becomes his "weapon" and "treasure" that Satan cannot steal. Henry applies this to believers facing false accusations, noting how "the ways and people of God [are] often misrepresented by the devil and his agents."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry notes the irony that Satan appears unrepentant, coming "as if he had been doing no harm." God's rhetorical question about Job serves as divine testimony to human faithfulness.

**Verse 4**: Satan's "Skin for skin" argument reveals twisted logic - Henry acknowledges the truth that self-preservation is powerful, but shows how Satan uses this to falsely portray Job as selfish and "unnatural" toward his family.

**Verses 7-8**: The physical description emphasizes Job's complete isolation - "no help afforded him" with only a potsherd for comfort, highlighting how former friends abandoned him due to fear or disgust.

**Verse 10**: Job's response to his wife becomes the chapter's theological climax: "shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?"

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his analysis:

- **Revelation 12:10** - Satan as the persistent accuser "day and night"
- **2 Corinthians 4:9** - "cast down, are not destroyed" 
- **1 Peter 1:7** - trials leading to "praise and honour"
- **Genesis 3:15** - Job as a type of Christ, with Satan bruising his heel
- **Matthew 4:6** - Satan's similar temptation of Christ's identity
- **Luke 13:16** - Satan's role in binding through disease
- **Psalm 76:10** - God restraining wrath while using it for His praise

Henry concludes that Job's suffering prefigures Christ's greater victory, where Satan received permission to touch Christ's "bone and flesh without exception of his life" so that through dying, Christ could "destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 3 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job 3 as a stark contrast between human patience and impatience. He notes the apostle James speaks of "the patience of Job," yet here we witness Job's profound impatience. Henry emphasizes that **"Job sinned with his lips"** and this chapter serves **"not for our imitation, but our admonition."** 

The central theological tension Henry identifies is that while Job cursed his day, **"he did not curse his God"**—remaining weary of life but not of religion. This distinction proves Job was no hypocrite, as Satan had claimed, but rather a genuine believer experiencing human frailty.

Henry acknowledges Job's behavior as sinful yet contextualizes it within God's grace: Job **"condemned himself for it, and therefore God did not condemn him."** This demonstrates that even exemplary believers can fall into serious sin while maintaining their fundamental faith.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Impatience**: He warns that **"he who thinks he stands may take heed lest he fall,"** showing how even the most patient can become overwhelmed by suffering.

**On Perspective**: Henry argues that no earthly condition should make us wish we'd never been born, since in any circumstance we can **"honour God, and work out his own salvation, and make sure a happiness for himself in a better world."**

**On Spiritual vs. Natural Life**: He notes that while many curse their natural birth, **"never any cursed the day of their new-birth, nor wished they never had had grace."** Spiritual gifts are **"above life and being itself, and which will never be a burden."**

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry describes Job's poetic curses as **"full of poetical fancy, flame, and rapture."** Job wishes:
- Earth might forget his birthday (v. 3,6)  
- Heaven might frown upon it: **"Let not God regard it from above"** (v. 4)
- All joy might forsake it (v. 7)
- All curses might follow it (v. 8)

Henry critiques Job's reasoning in v. 10, calling it **"the folly and madness of a passionate discontent"** that talks **"absurdly and extravagantly."** He points out Job's logical inconsistencies, including wishing something barbarous toward his mother and despising God's gift of noble existence.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Job's experience to other biblical passages:

- **James 5:11** - "the patience of Job"
- **Psalm 39:3-4** - David's better response after reflection
- **Jeremiah 15:10, 20:14** - Similar laments from Jeremiah
- **Romans 6:7** - Death freeing us from sin
- **Ecclesiastes 7:1** - Death being better than birth
- **Matthew 26:24** - Judas having reason to wish he'd never been born
- **Luke 23:29** - Christ's words about blessing the barren

Henry uses these references to show both the human tendency toward such despair and the better responses available to believers through Scripture and grace.

The commentary ultimately presents Job 3 as a cautionary example of how even the godly can fall into sinful despair, while maintaining that true faith endures even through such failures.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry frames Job 4 as the beginning of a theological dispute that mirrors the cosmic debate between God and Satan from earlier chapters. The central question remains: "whether Job was an honest man or no." While Satan had conceded Job's integrity, his friends now take up the adversary's cause, arguing that Job's severe afflictions prove his hypocrisy.

Henry identifies the core theological error of Job's friends: their rigid retribution theology that assumes all suffering indicates divine judgment for sin. They maintain "that man's wickedness is that which always brings God's judgments," creating a false equivalency between righteousness and prosperity, wickedness and affliction.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Reproving Friends:** Christians should reprove even afflicted friends when necessary, but with wisdom, meekness, and tenderness. "It is foolish pity not to reprove our friends...only for fear of offending them." However, we must be careful not to "add affliction to the afflicted."

**On Pastoral Care:** Henry praises Job's former ministry of "instructing many" and strengthening "weak hands" and "feeble knees," noting that "a good word, well and wisely spoken, may do more good than perhaps we think of." This sets a standard for spiritual charity and encouragement.

**Against Harsh Judgment:** Henry warns that "those that pass rash and uncharitable censures upon their brethren, and condemn them as hypocrites, do Satan's work, and serve his interest." This cautions against the very mistake Eliphaz makes.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-4:** Henry commends Eliphaz's apparent humility in approaching Job carefully, though noting his underlying harshness. He highlights Job's previous ministry of strengthening others with "the tongue of the learned."

**Verse 5:** Henry criticizes Eliphaz for making "too light of Job's afflictions" (using Satan's own word "touches") while making "too much of Job's resentments."

**Verse 6:** This verse appears differently in various Bible editions, but Henry sees it as Eliphaz suggesting Job's religion was merely "mercenary" - echoing Satan's original accusation.

**Verses 7-11:** Henry identifies Eliphaz's flawed principle that "good men were never thus ruined," noting it's only true regarding eternal destruction, not temporal suffering.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout his commentary:

- **Psalm 69:26** - warning against adding affliction to the afflicted
- **Lamentations 3:33** - God "does not afflict willingly"
- **Hebrews 12:12** - lifting up hands that hang down
- **Isaiah 35:3-4** - strengthening fearful hearts
- **1 Corinthians 11:2** - Paul's pattern of praise before reproof
- **John 16:8** - the Comforter reproving
- **Romans 2:21** - the inconsistency of teaching others but not oneself
- **2 Thessalonians 2:3** - only the man of sin is a son of perdition
- **Proverbs 24:10** - fainting in adversity showing small strength

Henry's commentary reveals Eliphaz as unwittingly serving Satan's agenda while appearing religiously zealous, making this chapter a cautionary tale about theological presumption and pastoral insensitivity.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 5 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Eliphaz's discourse as addressing four key theological principles: that sin leads to ruin, affliction is mankind's common lot, believers should turn to God in trouble, and patient endurance leads to divine blessing. Henry notes that Eliphaz "concludes his discourse in somewhat a better humour than he began it," moving from harsh accusation toward encouragement.

A central theme is divine sovereignty in suffering. Henry emphasizes that "no affliction comes by chance" - troubles don't "come forth of the dust, neither doth trouble spring out of the ground" but according to God's will. He distinguishes between the source of affliction (God) and sin (ourselves), noting "we must own that God sends it upon us and we procure it to ourselves."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Eliphaz's counsel. Regarding verse 8's call to "seek unto God," he provides both reproof and advice: Job should not quarrel with God but "apply to God" through prayer and patience. Henry emphasizes two responses: "(1.) We must by prayer fetch in mercy and grace from God" and "(2.) We must by patience refer ourselves and our cause to him."

On dealing with adversity, Henry notes that since "man is born unto trouble," believers shouldn't be "surprised at our afflictions as strange, or quarrel with them as hard, when they are but what we are born to." This reflects the doctrine of original sin's consequences.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry interprets Eliphaz's appeal to "the saints" as claiming unanimous support from faithful witnesses. He observes that "good people are called saints even in the Old Testament" and notes that true saints "will turn to those that are so."

**Verses 2-5**: On sin's self-destructive nature, Henry explains that "wrath kills the foolish man, his own wrath" - it becomes "a fire in his bones." He sees Eliphaz drawing parallels between Job's situation and typical patterns of wicked people's temporary prosperity followed by certain destruction.

**Verses 6-7**: The famous phrase about man being "born unto trouble, as the sparks fly upward" receives Henry's explanation that this results from our fallen state: "Man is born in sin, and therefore born to trouble."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Job's text to other biblical passages. He references **Psalm 119:79** regarding saints choosing fellow believers as judges, and **1 Corinthians 6:1-2** on saints judging the world. For the temporary prosperity of the wicked, he cites **Psalm 73:17-18** about understanding their end in God's sanctuary.

The concept of divine judgment appears through references to **Proverbs 3:33** (curse in the house of the wicked) and **Zechariah 5:4** (curse consuming houses). Henry also connects the "born unto trouble" theme to **Romans 5:12** regarding sin's entry into the world and **Isaiah 48:8** about being "transgressors from the womb."

For practical application, he references **Galatians 4:20** about changing one's voice when counseling others, demonstrating how Eliphaz shifts from accusation to encouragement.

Henry's commentary reveals careful attention to both the theological implications of suffering and practical pastoral concerns for those experiencing trials.

Chapter 6
----------------------------------------
## Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 6 Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry interprets Job's response to Eliphaz as revealing the profound depth of suffering that extends beyond physical affliction to spiritual anguish. The central theological theme is that **mental and spiritual distress constitutes "the sorest trouble"** - Job's primary complaint isn't his material losses but feeling that "the arrows of the Almighty are within me." Henry sees Job as a **type of Christ** in his soul's suffering, referencing John 12:27 and Matthew 26:38.

The commentary emphasizes the **complexity of righteous suffering**, noting that while Job "spoke much that was reasonable," it came "with a mixture of passion and human infirmity." Henry presents this as instructive about the reality that even faithful believers can struggle with ungoverned emotions during trials.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Compassionate Ministry**: He criticizes those who "undertook to administer spiritual physic before they thoroughly understood his case," warning that **"it is seldom that those who are at ease themselves rightly weigh the afflictions of the afflicted."**

**On Dietary Contentment**: From Job's reference to unsavory food, Henry advises: **"It is wisdom not to use ourselves or our children to be nice and dainty about meat and drink, because we know not how we or they may be reduced."**

**On Suicide Prevention**: Though Job desperately wanted death, **"he did not offer to destroy himself"** but waited for God's timing. Henry strongly condemns self-murder as violating "the law of God and nature."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Job's desire to have his grief "thoroughly weighed" reveals his feeling of being misunderstood. His calamity being "heavier than the sand of the sea" explains why "my words are swallowed up" - his passionate expressions result from extraordinary circumstances.

**Verse 4**: The "arrows of the Almighty" represent God's unique ability to wound the soul: **"He that made the soul can make his sword to approach to it."**

**Verses 5-7**: Job's metaphors about animals not complaining when fed show his responses aren't characteristic complaining but natural reactions to genuine deprivation.

**Verses 8-13**: Henry sees Job's death wish as his worst error, calling these **"passionate prayers are the worst of passionate expressions."** Yet he notes Job's confidence in post-death comfort reveals "the hope which the righteous have in their death."

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Job's experience to Christ's passion (John 12:27, Matthew 26:38, 27:46). He references 1 Corinthians 10:22 regarding presuming against God's strength, and contrasts Job's attitude with David's plea to "spare me a little" from Psalm 39:13.

The commentary also alludes to the parable of Lazarus and the rich man, showing how **"death turns the tables"** between present suffering and future comfort for the righteous.

Henry concludes that this passage serves as **"admonition, not imitation"** - demonstrating both the reality of human weakness in suffering and the danger of allowing passion to override patient faith.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 7 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Job's discourse as an impassioned plea excusing his "inordinate desire of death." The commentary explores several key theological themes:

**Human Frailty and Divine Sovereignty**: Henry emphasizes that man's time on earth represents "a warfare" with an "appointed time" determined by God's "wise, holy, and sovereign counsel," not "blind fortune." Life is portrayed as both temporary placement ("upon earth, not under it; on earth, not in hell") and divine mercy.

**The Nature of Suffering**: Job's condition illustrates universal human experience - Henry notes that "every man has some reason (more or less) to desire to die shortly." Suffering serves to remind believers of life's brevity and eternity's reality.

**Death as Release**: Rather than condemning Job's death wish, Henry presents it as understandable given that death provides "perpetual ease" from earthly calamities, though emphasizing death's finality - "dying is work that is to be done but once."

## Key Practical Applications

**For the Afflicted**: Henry offers comfort to sufferers by noting that "when we are disabled to work for God, if we will but sit still quietly for him, it is all one; we shall be accepted." Even in uselessness, quiet submission has value.

**Perspective on Life's Purpose**: He warns against placing happiness in "visible good things" since "the eye of the body must be closed." Instead, believers should "live by that faith which is the substance and evidence of things not seen."

**View of Human Condition**: Henry draws practical lessons about seeing ourselves as "soldiers, exposed to hardship" and "day-labourers" who must "make up their account at night."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6**: Henry interprets Job's comparison to a hireling as legitimate - just as laborers desire evening rest, Job reasonably desires death's relief. The phrase "months of vanity" particularly troubles Job because of forced uselessness rather than mere discomfort.

**Verse 6**: The famous "weaver's shuttle" metaphor receives extended treatment: "every day, like the shuttle, leaves a thread behind it." Henry warns some "weave the spider's web, which will fail them," while others weave "holy garments and robes of righteousness."

**Verses 7-10**: Henry finds Job's plea "O remember that my life is wind" as proper appeal to divine compassion, noting God had similar mercy on Israel "remembering that they were but flesh."

**Verses 11-16**: While acknowledging "a great mixture of passion and corruption" in Job's words, Henry counsels readers not to learn prayer techniques from this passage, yet shows understanding of Job's desperation.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:
- **Psalm 115:16** on earth as given to men
- **Psalm 78:38-39** on God's compassion for human frailty  
- **Ecclesiastes 5:12** on the laborer's sweet sleep
- **Isaiah 38:12** on cutting off life like a weaver
- **Galatians 6:8** on sowing and reaping principles
- **Psalm 88:18** on friends removed into darkness

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic approach of finding theological depth and practical wisdom even in Scripture's most difficult passages, while maintaining pastoral sensitivity to human suffering.

# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 8

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Bildad as representing a flawed but partially correct theological position. **The central theme is divine justice** - Bildad correctly affirms that "God doth not pervert judgment" and never acts contrary to justice (v.3). Henry emphasizes that God's omnipotence ensures He cannot be unjust, unlike humans who pervert justice through weakness or fear.

However, Henry critiques Bildad's **rigid retribution theology** - the assumption that extraordinary afflictions always indicate extraordinary sins. Henry notes that "extraordinary afflictions are not always the punishment of extraordinary sins, but sometimes the trial of extraordinary graces." This represents a key theological insight: **suffering can be for testing rather than punishment**.

The commentary also explores **the doctrine of divine providence**, showing how God ordinarily works gradually ("per gradum--step by step") rather than suddenly, with "dawning light growing to noonday."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On handling reproofs**: "Reproofs are often grounded upon mistakes" - we must ensure we understand others correctly before rebuking them.

**On prayer and repentance**: Sincere seeking of God requires being "early in seeking God" with "humility and faith." Only "the prayer of the upright" is acceptable and prevailing.

**On small beginnings**: "Let us not therefore despise the day of small things, but hope for the day of great things." God blesses gradual growth over sudden leaps.

**On judging others' suffering**: We should "take the more favourable side" when judging others' afflictions, following Christ's example in Luke 13:2-4.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry defends Job's previous words as "good sense" while noting Bildad's passionate response demonstrates how "eager angry disputants" dismiss valid arguments.

**Verse 4**: Henry strongly criticizes Bildad's assumption about Job's children, calling it unfair to conclude they were "cast-aways" based solely on their tragic deaths.

**Verses 8-10**: The appeal to "former ages" receives nuanced treatment - Henry notes that while ancient wisdom has value, "now that we have the word of God in writing...we need not enquire of the former age."

**Verses 11-19**: The metaphors of plants without water and spider's webs effectively illustrate how "the hypocrite's hope shall perish," though Henry emphasizes this applies ultimately if not immediately.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Job to other biblical passages:

- **1 Corinthians 14:30-31** - on taking turns in conversation
- **Genesis 18:25** - "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?"
- **Romans 3:5-6** and **Romans 10:8** - on God's justice and the accessibility of His word
- **Luke 13:2-4** - Christ's teaching against assuming suffering indicates special guilt
- **Psalm 66:18** and **Proverbs 15:8** - on acceptable prayer
- **Psalm 119:99-100** - on understanding more than ancients through God's precepts

The commentary demonstrates Henry's method of interpreting difficult passages through the lens of clearer biblical revelation, showing how Job's narrative fits within broader scriptural themes of divine justice, human suffering, and spiritual growth.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Job's response to Bildad as a profound meditation on **God's absolute sovereignty and human frailty**. The central theological theme is expressed in Job's question: "How should man be just with God?" Henry interprets this not as rebellious complaint, but as "a pious confession of man's sinfulness" - acknowledging that if God dealt with humanity according to justice alone, all would be condemned.

The commentary emphasizes **God's incomprehensible wisdom and unlimited power**, demonstrating that humans are "an unequal match for his Maker, either in dispute or combat." Henry notes that despite Job's suffering, rather than cursing God, "he sets himself to honour God and to speak highly of him."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

1. **Humility in theological disputes**: "We should be ready to own how far we agree with those with whom we dispute" and receive truth "though it may have been misapplied."

2. **Recognition of human limitations**: We are "incompetent judges of God's proceedings, because we know not what he does or what he designs."

3. **Submission to divine sovereignty**: God "is not obliged to give us a reason of what he does" - His actions may seem mysterious now but will prove wise in eternity.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 2**: Job agrees with Bildad's principle while maintaining his position about human sinfulness before God.

**Verses 3-4**: Henry explains that God can ask "a thousand puzzling questions" and "lay to our charge a thousand offences" that humans cannot answer or defend against.

**Verses 5-10**: These verses showcase God's power over nature - moving mountains, shaking earth, commanding celestial bodies, and making constellations. Henry notes this demonstrates both "continued creation" and God's ability to "alter the course of nature."

**Verses 11-13**: God's invisible operations and "incontestable sovereignty" - He acts beyond human perception and accountability.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical references throughout:
- Psalm 143:2 on human justification before God
- Isaiah 27:4 and Ezekiel 28:24 on futile opposition to God
- 2 Peter 2:4 and Revelation 12:9 on fallen angels' failed rebellion
- Daniel 4:35 on God's unquestionable authority
- Acts 17:23 on God's invisible presence

The commentary connects Job's cosmic descriptions to both Noah's flood and the future judgment by fire, showing continuity in God's sovereign power throughout biblical history.

Henry masterfully presents Job as maintaining orthodox theology while wrestling with profound personal suffering, offering a model of faithful questioning that honors rather than challenges divine authority.

## Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 10: Summary

### **Main Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Henry presents Job as a man in spiritual confusion, torn between faith and overwhelming grief. The central tension revolves around **God's sovereignty versus apparent injustice**. Henry emphasizes that Job's discourse reflects his inner turmoil—"he knew not what to say, and perhaps sometimes scarcely knew what he said."

The commentary explores the **doctrine of divine omniscience** versus human limitations. Henry stresses that God "has not eyes of flesh" and "sees not as man sees," highlighting the infinite gap between divine and human perspective. A key theme is **God as Creator and Sustainer**, with Henry noting how Job appeals to his relationship with God as his Maker to ground his complaints and hopes.

### **Key Practical Applications**

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers facing trials:

**On Handling Despair**: When Job declares "My soul is weary of my life," Henry warns that "Faith and patience would keep us from being weary of our lives...even when Providence has made them most wearisome to us." He advocates for spiritual disciplines over emotional venting.

**On Prayer in Affliction**: Henry commends Job's petition "Do not condemn me" as proper prioritizing, noting: "This therefore we should deprecate above any thing else, when we are in affliction"—fearing spiritual condemnation more than physical suffering.

**On Divine Submission**: Henry concludes that **"it is to no purpose to contend with Omnipotence"** while finding comfort in being able to appeal: "Lord, thou knowest that I am not wicked."

### **Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights**

**Verses 1-2**: Henry sees Job's resolution to complain as understandable but dangerous, noting that "grace puts in a word" even amid corruption. Job's prayer reflects proper priorities—seeking to avoid condemnation while understanding God's purpose.

**Verses 3-7**: Henry interprets Job's questioning not as accusation but as honest perplexity about reconciling God's justice with apparent oppression. The commentary emphasizes God's perfect knowledge making human-like investigation unnecessary.

**Verses 8-13**: Henry provides rich exposition on human creation, describing the body as "fearfully and wonderfully made" through elegant metaphors of pottery and cheese-making. He emphasizes both human dignity (divine craftsmanship) and humility (clay material).

### **Cross-References to Other Scripture**

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:

- **Romans 8:1** and **1 Corinthians 11:32** on the distinction between chastening and condemnation
- **Genesis 42:21** on conscience's role in understanding God's controversy
- **Lamentations 3:34-36** on God's character regarding oppression  
- **Psalm 139:1-13** on God's complete knowledge of His creatures
- **Psalm 18:21** regarding not wickedly departing from God

Henry's commentary balances sympathy for Job's suffering with gentle correction of his theological missteps, consistently pointing readers toward faith, patience, and proper understanding of God's character and ways.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Job Chapter 11

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as Zophar's harsh attack on Job, emphasizing three major theological themes. **God's incomprehensible nature** dominates the commentary - Henry notes that "God is unsearchable" and that "we may apprehend him, but we cannot comprehend him." The **sovereignty of God** appears as an uncontestable truth, with God possessing "incontrollable power" over all creation. Finally, **divine justice** emerges as Henry argues that "whatever punishment is inflicted upon us in this world we must own that it is less than our iniquities deserve."

Henry characterizes Zophar as representing misguided religious zeal, showing how "even wise and good men are sometimes betrayed into intemperate passions by the heat of disputation." This serves as a warning about controversy's corrupting influence on Christian discourse.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from this difficult passage. He warns that "those who engage in controversy will find it very hard to keep their temper," using Zophar as a cautionary example of how theological disputes can lead to uncharitable behavior. 

He emphasizes the importance of charitable interpretation: "We ought to put the best construction on the words and actions of our brethren that they will bear; but contenders are tempted to put the worst."

Most significantly, Henry calls for humble submission to God's providence: "By employing ourselves in adoring the depth of those divine counsels of which we cannot find the bottom we shall very much tranquilize our minds under the afflicting hand of God."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry sharply criticizes Zophar's opening attack (verses 2-3), noting that calling Job a liar and mocker "is not the way to comfort Job... nor to convince him neither." He observes that Zophar misrepresents Job's position, charging him with claiming sinless perfection when Job had actually said "I have sinned."

On verses 7-9, Henry provides memorable exposition on God's incomprehensibility: "The eye can see the ocean but not see over it." He explains that while we "may, by searching find God... we cannot find him out to perfection."

Regarding verse 10's description of God's sovereignty, Henry notes: "If he that made all out of nothing think fit to reduce all to nothing... who can turn him away, alter his mind or stay his hand?"

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry enriches his commentary with extensive biblical connections. He references **Matthew 6:7** regarding God not accepting "much speaking," **Acts 17:27** on finding God through searching, and **1 Corinthians 13:9-10** concerning our partial knowledge.

He connects God's incomprehensible love to **Ephesians 3:18-19**, comparing Paul's "immeasurable dimensions to the divine love" with Zophar's description of divine wisdom. **Psalm 139:6** supports the theme of knowledge being "too wonderful for us," while **Lamentations 3:39** reinforces the principle that our sufferings are less than we deserve.

Henry's treatment reveals both the dangers of self-righteous judgment and the proper response of humble submission to God's mysterious but just ways.

## Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Job Chapter 12

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents three central theological themes in this chapter. First, he addresses the danger of spiritual pride and self-righteousness, using Job's ironic response to his friends as a warning against claiming a "monopoly of wisdom." Second, he explores the reality that divine providence operates as sovereign—that God "acts as a sovereign, reserving the exact distribution of rewards and punishments for the future state." Third, Henry emphasizes that outward prosperity cannot be used as a measure of spiritual standing, noting "we cannot judge of men's piety by their plenty."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers. He warns against harsh judgment of others, noting that "when we are tempted to be harsh in our censures of those we differ from...we ought to consider that they also have understanding as well as we." He counsels humility regarding our own wisdom: "It may justly keep us from being proud of our knowledge to consider how many there are that know as much as we do." 

For those facing persecution or mockery, Henry offers comfort, explaining that "it was of old the lot of honest good people to be despised and derided; we are not therefore to think it strange." He also critiques the tendency of the prosperous to despise the afflicted, calling it "the common fault of those that live in prosperity."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verses 2-3, Henry highlights Job's masterful irony: "No doubt you are the people, and wisdom shall die with you." He explains this as Job's rebuke to those who "think themselves fit to dictate and give law to all mankind."

Regarding verse 4's complaint about being "mocked," Henry notes the particular pain when "they were his neighbours, his friends, his companions...and the scoffs of such are often most spitefully given, and always most indignantly received."

For verse 6's observation about robbers prospering, Henry provides a crucial interpretive key: "one observation built upon matter of fact is worth twenty notions framed by an hypothesis."

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary. He references Romans 14:3 and James 3:1 regarding judging others, and Psalm 55:12-13 about betrayal by friends. He connects Job's suffering to the broader pattern of persecution (Matthew 5:12, 1 Peter 4:12) and links the prosperity of the wicked to Psalm 73:7. The commentary also references Habakkuk 1:14 and Romans 8:20-22 regarding creation's subjection to wickedness, demonstrating Henry's systematic approach to biblical interpretation.

Henry concludes that this chapter serves as both a rebuke to spiritual pride and a realistic acknowledgment that divine justice often awaits future vindication rather than immediate temporal consequences.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 13 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job in a bold but conflicted spiritual state, caught between **faith and frustration**. The commentary emphasizes two primary theological tensions: Job's legitimate confidence in his integrity versus his dangerous proximity to challenging God's justice directly. Henry identifies **God's sovereignty and omniscience** as central themes, noting that "God's truth needs not our lie, nor God's cause either our sinful policies or our sinful passions."

The commentary highlights the **inadequacy of human wisdom** compared to divine understanding, and warns against **false advocacy for God** - attempting to defend Him through uncharitable judgment of others.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On Disputation and Pride**: "Those who enter into disputation enter into temptation to magnify themselves and vilify their brethren more than is fit, and therefore ought to watch and pray against the workings of pride."

**On Silence as Wisdom**: When facing suffering believers, "Better say nothing than nothing to the purpose or that which tends to the dishonour of God and the grief of our brethren." He notes that "Even a fool, when he holds his peace, is accounted wise."

**On Fair Hearing**: "We should be very willing and glad to hear what those have to say for themselves whom, upon any account, we are tempted to have hard thoughts of."

**Verses 1-2**: Job asserts intellectual equality with his friends - Henry sees this as necessary self-defense rather than pride, comparing it to Paul's forced self-commendation.

**Verse 3**: Job's desire to "reason with God" demonstrates that "The prince himself will perhaps give audience to a poor petitioner with more mildness, patience, and condescension, than the servants will."

**Verses 7-8**: Henry emphasizes that "God's cause suffered by such management" - defending God through uncharitable judgment actually dishonors Him.

**Verse 15**: The famous declaration "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him" represents faith at its boldest, which Henry calls "not more bold than welcome."

Henry weaves numerous biblical references throughout:
- **2 Corinthians** (Paul's forced self-commendation)

- **Proverbs 17:28** (wisdom of silence)

- **Judges 6:31** (God can plead for Himself)

Henry concludes by noting Job's **holy confidence mixed with dangerous boldness** - while his faith remains admirable, his direct challenges to God's methods border on presumption. The commentary serves as both vindication of sincere suffering and warning against judicial pride in others.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Job Chapter 14

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Job 14 as a profound meditation on human mortality and divine sovereignty. The central theological themes include:

**Original Sin and Human Depravity**: Henry emphasizes that humanity's frailty stems from our corrupt nature inherited from birth. He notes Job's question "Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean?" as pointing to original sin - "the original of all our sins." This corruption is "bred in the bone" and affects both our mortality and moral state.

**Divine Sovereignty Over Life**: Henry stresses that God determines the exact length of human life: "The number of our months is with God, at the disposal of his power." This isn't fatalism but reflects God's "settled rules of moral government" working through natural means while maintaining complete sovereignty.

**Human Mortality vs. Divine Eternity**: The commentary contrasts temporary earthly suffering with eternal perspectives, noting that while earthly days are "few" and "full of trouble," in heaven "our days will be many, and perfectly free from trouble."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**Humility Before God**: Given human frailty, Henry urges believers to pray "Lord, enter not into judgment with thy servant." Our weakness should drive us to seek God's compassion rather than His strict judgment.

**Proper View of Suffering**: Henry suggests Job's plea for respite - "let me not feel those uncommon tortures" - shows how believers can "find relief under great troubles by recommending ourselves to the compassion of that God who knows our frame."

**Preparation for Death**: The chapter serves as ideal material "for funeral solemnities" and helps believers "both to get good by the death of others and to get ready for our own."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry's famous description captures humanity's condition: man is "like a flower" (fading beauty) and "a shadow" (fleeting existence). Life is measured in days, not years, because "we cannot be sure of any day but that it may be our last."

**Verse 4**: The rhetorical question about bringing "a clean thing out of an unclean" demonstrates hereditary corruption, though Henry notes the Chaldee paraphrase suggests hope: "Cannot one? that is, God" can cleanse the polluted.

**Verses 7-10**: Henry's elegant comparison shows trees can revive from stumps through "the scent of water," but human death is permanent - "the rational soul, when once retired, is too great, too noble, a thing to be recalled by any of the powers of nature."

**Verse 13**: Job's desire to be hidden in the grave until God's wrath passes shows death as potential refuge from present sufferings.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:

- **Psalm 51:5** - for original sin and corruption from birth
- **1 Corinthians 7:31** - on the passing nature of worldly existence  
- **Daniel 5:26** - regarding numbered and finished days
- **Galatians 4:4** - noting Christ was "made of a woman" in taking on human nature
- **Jeremiah 51:30** - on how "mighty men become as women" when God acts

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic approach of connecting Job's experience to broader biblical theology while drawing practical applications for Christian living and dying.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 15 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job 15 as Eliphaz's second attack on Job, structured around three key theological principles. **The doctrine of universal human depravity** dominates, with Eliphaz arguing that no one can be truly righteous before God: "What is man, that he should be clean? and he which is born of woman, that he should be righteous?" Henry notes this doctrine is "sound" but "misapplied to Job." 

The **relationship between suffering and sin** remains central, with Eliphaz maintaining his retributive theology while Job refuses to confess to hypocrisy. Henry emphasizes the **danger of presumptuous knowledge**, as Eliphaz accuses Job of claiming divine secrets: "Hast thou heard the secret of God?"

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons, even while noting Eliphaz's misapplication. He warns against **"vain knowledge"** and **"unprofitable talk,"** stating "We must give an account in the great day not only for wicked words, but for idle words." 

The commentary strongly emphasizes that **true religion consists of "fearing God and praying to him"** - identifying these as "the most needful principle" and "the most needful practice." Henry warns that "those who restrain prayer do thereby give evidence that they cast off fear."

He cautions against **misrepresenting opponents' views** in theological disputes, noting it's unjust "to charge upon their adversaries those consequences of their opinions which are not fairly drawn from them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry explains Eliphaz's accusation about "east wind" knowledge, noting that while there is indeed "vain knowledge, science falsely so called," Eliphaz wrongly applies this to Job's legitimate defense.

**Verse 4**: The charge of casting off fear and restraining prayer represents the essence of apostasy. Henry observes this is "especially bad with those who have had some fear of God, but have now cast it off."

**Verses 7-9**: Eliphaz's sarcastic questions about Job being "the first man" reveal the friends' arrogance. Henry notes Job had made only the reasonable request to "Allow that I have understanding as well as you."

**Verses 14-16**: Henry acknowledges the theological truth that man "drinketh iniquity like water," but emphasizes this general principle doesn't prove Job's specific guilt.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout:

- **Matthew 5:19** regarding teaching others to break commandments
- **Psalm 130:4** on forgiveness encouraging fear of God  
- **Proverbs 8:23** referencing Wisdom's antiquity
- **Daniel 6:7** on restraining others from prayer
- **Romans 16:18** about deceptive speech
- **Galatians 2:6** on not being impressed by human authority

The commentary concludes with Henry's balanced assessment: "A good use may be made both of his reproofs (for they are plain) and of his doctrine (for it is sound), though both the one and the other are misapplied to Job." This encapsulates Henry's approach of finding spiritual value even in flawed human reasoning while maintaining Job's innocence.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary - Job Chapter 16

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets Job 16 as a continuation of Job's lament, highlighting the tension between divine sovereignty and human suffering. A key theological theme is **the failure of human comfort compared to divine consolation**. Henry notes that Job's friends represent how "miserable comforters are they all" when compared to God, emphasizing that "it is only the blessed Spirit that can comfort effectually."

The commentary presents Job as **a type of Christ**, drawing parallels between Job's suffering and Christ's passion. Henry specifically connects the descriptions of enemies gathering against Job (v. 10) to Psalm 22:13 and Micah 5:1, noting how Job was "delivered into wicked hands" just as Christ would be "by the determinate counsel and fore-knowledge of God."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes **proper Christian friendship and comfort ministry**. He outlines specific duties toward afflicted brethren: "We should say and do all we can to strengthen them" and "assuage their grief." He warns against the friends' failures: needless repetitions, unskilful applications, and violation of friendship's sacred laws.

The commentary advocates for **empathetic perspective-taking**, highlighting Job's challenge to his friends to "put their souls in his soul's stead." Henry notes the wisdom of considering "how soon they may be so" afflicted ourselves, promoting humility and compassion.

Regarding suffering, Henry warns against both extremes - neither venting complaints nor keeping silence provided Job relief (v. 6), suggesting **balanced expression of grief** rather than either suppression or endless complaint.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry criticizes the friends' "needless repetitions" and "endless impertinence," noting how "the longer the saw of contention is drawn the hotter it grows."

**Verses 4-5**: The golden rule application - Job's hypothetical kindness contrasted with his friends' actual harshness demonstrates proper Christian response to suffering.

**Verses 9-11**: Henry offers three interpretations of Job's "enemy" - Eliphaz, Satan, or God himself, noting that "nothing can be more grievous to a good man than to apprehend God to be his enemy."

**Verse 12**: The image of God shaking Job "as a strong man in a passion would take a child" illustrates Job's perception of divine fury, though Henry clarifies "fury is not in God."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Job's experience to Christ's sufferings, citing **Psalm 22:13** (enemies gaping with mouths), **Micah 5:1** (smiting the judge), and **Matthew 26:67** (literal fulfillment in Christ's passion).

He references **Psalm 17:13** and **Isaiah 10:5** regarding God using wicked men as instruments of judgment. **Acts 2:23** connects Job's delivery to wicked hands with Christ's crucifixion.

The commentary also draws from **Psalm 35:15** regarding "the abjects" gathering against sufferers, reinforcing the typological connection between Job and Christ's experiences of rejection and persecution.

Matthew Henry presents Job 17 as a study in suffering and divine providence. # Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 18 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry identifies this chapter as Bildad's "second assault upon Job," noting a stark shift from his first discourse which offered encouragement. Here, Bildad "has grown more peevish" and presents the inevitable fate of the wicked through vivid imagery of darkness, snares, and terror. Henry emphasizes that while Bildad's description of wickedness and its consequences contains "a great deal of certain truth," his application to Job is fundamentally flawed. The central theological tension revolves around the false assumption that all suffering indicates divine judgment for sin.

Henry observes that Bildad, though "a wise and good man," was unwittingly "serving Satan's design in adding to Job's affliction"—a sobering reminder that even well-intentioned religious discourse can become destructive.

## Key Practical Applications

**On Passionate Speech and Listening:** Henry warns against Bildad's impatience to speak, noting "we ought to be swift to hear and slow to speak." He observes how "contenders monopolize the reputation of wisdom" and become dictators in conversation.

**On Pride and Social Status:** Henry notes the cruel irony that Job, who once had the final word in debates during his prosperity, now "could scarcely be allowed to speak at all" in poverty. This leads to his memorable observation: "Wisdom therefore (as the world goes) is good with an inheritance (Eccl. vii. 11)."

**On Anger's Self-Destruction:** Regarding Bildad's accusation that Job "teareth himself in his anger," Henry provides the practical insight: "Anger is a sin that is its own punishment. Fretful passionate people tear and torment themselves."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-4:** Henry sees Bildad's charges as unfair exaggerations. When Bildad complains about being "counted as beasts," Henry notes this was "invidious"—Job never actually called them beasts, but "proud men are apt to think themselves slighted more than really they are."

**Verses 5-6:** On the wicked man's light being "put out," Henry distinguishes between temporary worldly pleasures and true spiritual light: "his light is but a spark... It is not the light of the Lord (that is sun-light), but the light of his own fire."

**Verses 7-10:** Henry identifies three agents preparing the wicked man's destruction: Satan as "the robber," the sinner himself through his own choices, and God through divine justice. He notes how "his own counsels cast him down" and "he is cast into a net by his own feet."

**Verses 11-21:** The vivid description of terror and destruction represents both present consequences of sin and ultimate judgment. Henry emphasizes that while some of this may occur in this life, it particularly points to eternal consequences.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:
- **Ecclesiastes 7:11** and **9:16** on wisdom and poverty
- **Isaiah 50:4** on hearing "as the learned"
- **Proverbs 8:36** on sin wounding the soul
- **2 Corinthians 5:1** on the body as "tabernacle of the soul"
- **Proverbs 11:7** on the wicked man's expectation perishing
- **Psalm 9:16** on being "snared in the work of his own hands"
- **2 Peter 2:9** on God reserving "the wicked for the day of judgment"

Henry's commentary demonstrates how Job's experience connects to broader biblical themes of suffering, divine justice, and the danger of presumptuous judgment, while maintaining that Bildad's theological accuracy about sin's consequences doesn't justify his harsh application to Job's situation.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 19: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job's response to Bildad as a study in human suffering under divine sovereignty. The central theological tension involves Job's perception that **"God hath overthrown me, and hath compassed me with his net"** while simultaneously maintaining faith in God's ultimate justice. Henry emphasizes that Job's **"gross mistake, but a very common one"** was thinking **"that whom God afflicts he treats as his enemies; whereas, on the contrary, as many as he loves he rebukes and chastens."** The commentary highlights how God's disciplinary love can be misunderstood during intense suffering.

The chapter's theological climax centers on Job's **"believing hopes of happiness in the other world"** and his **"solemn confession of faith"** regarding future vindication. Henry notes that Job's **"cheerful views of the future state...may shame us Christians"** who have clearer revelation yet often display less faith during trials.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons about handling criticism and suffering:

**On False Comfort**: Job's friends **"vexed his soul"** and **"broke him in pieces with words"** while pretending to help. Henry warns against those who **"set themselves to rob him of the only comfort he had now left him in a good God, a good conscience, and a good name."**

**On Fair Treatment**: Henry advocates for charitable judgment, noting that if someone errs, **"I keep it to myself, and do not impose it upon others"** and **"it is I that must smart for it; and therefore you need not concern yourselves."**

**On Friendship During Trials**: True friends don't **"make themselves strange"** during troubles. Henry criticizes those **"governed by the spirit of the world"** who abandon friends in distress, emphasizing that **"A friend loves at all times."**

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-7**: Henry explains Job's **"How long"** opening as justified defense rather than mere complaint, since **"Better cause may be shown for defending ourselves...than for offending our brethren."**

**Verses 8-12**: The imagery of God's troops besieging Job's tabernacle shows how **"Time was when God's hosts encamped round him for safety...Now, on the contrary, they surrounded him, to his terror."**

**Verses 13-22**: Henry details the social isolation accompanying Job's physical suffering, noting how even servants and children showed contempt, illustrating how suffering compounds through relational breakdown.

## Cross-References and Scripture Connections

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:
- **Psalm 6:2-3** on soul vexation versus physical pain
- **Jude 15** regarding accountability for harsh words against God's people  
- **2 Corinthians 1:12** on pleading integrity against reproach
- **Psalm 69:26** condemning persecution of those God has smitten

He connects Job's experience to broader biblical themes of divine discipline, noting how **"enlightened consciences fear it [God's wrath] now, but shall not feel it hereafter"** while **"seared consciences will feel it hereafter, but do not fear it now."**

Henry concludes that Job's complaints, while potentially justifying our own, should ultimately be balanced by his superior faith in future vindication, challenging Christians with greater revelation to show equal trust during trials.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 20 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies the central theme as **the temporal prosperity but ultimate destruction of the wicked**. Zophar argues that divine justice operates with predictable consistency - the wicked may prosper briefly, but their ruin is inevitable. However, Henry highlights the "great mistake" and "flaw" in Zophar's reasoning: **assuming God never varies from this method**, leading to the false conclusion that Job's suffering proves his wickedness.

The commentary emphasizes the distinction between **open wickedness and hypocrisy**, noting that "dissembled piety is double iniquity" and that hypocrites face the "hottest place in hell." Henry affirms that while divine justice is certain, its timing and manifestation remain in God's sovereign discretion.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On handling criticism**: "The best counsel is too often ill taken from an antagonist." He warns against **high spirits that are impatient of contradiction**, noting how Zophar's pride prevented him from receiving Job's wise counsel about fearing divine judgment.

**On judging others**: Henry cautions against the friends' error of **assuming outward circumstances reflect inner spiritual condition**. Their "spirits too high to deal with a man in his low condition" blinded them to Job's genuine faith.

**On sin's deception**: Using vivid imagery of wickedness being "sweet in his mouth" but becoming "gall of asps within him," Henry illustrates how sin promises pleasure but delivers destruction.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry observes that Zophar "seems to be in a great heat," mistaking "the dictates of their passion for the dictates of their reason." This shows how emotional reactions can masquerade as spiritual discernment.

**Verses 4-5**: The phrase "**The triumphing of the wicked is short, and the joy of the hypocrite but for a moment**" represents truth wrongly applied. Henry notes this doctrine has been "known of old, since man was placed upon earth."

**Verses 6-9**: Henry describes the wicked man's destruction as **shameful** ("like his own dung"), **surprising** ("Where is he?"), **swift** ("fly away as a dream"), and **utter** (completely final).

**Verses 12-14**: The imagery of sin hidden "under his tongue" becoming poison illustrates how cherished sins ultimately destroy those who harbor them.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout:

- **Acts 28:4** - "whom vengeance suffers not to live"
- **Isaiah 3:11** - "Woe to the wicked; it shall be ill with him"  
- **Matthew 24:51** - regarding hypocrites' judgment
- **Psalm 73:19** - the wicked "brought into desolation in a moment"
- **Proverbs 23:4** - "Labour not to be rich"
- **Ephesians 4:28** - working to give, not merely accumulate

These references demonstrate how Zophar's observations about divine justice align with broader biblical teaching, even while his application to Job remains flawed.

Henry concludes that Zophar's theology, while containing truth about ultimate divine justice, errs by **assuming invariable timing and method** in God's dealings with humanity.

Chapter 21
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry identifies the central theological dispute between Job and his friends: **whether outward prosperity indicates divine approval and conversely, whether suffering proves hypocrisy**. His friends asserted this prosperity-righteousness connection, but Job emphatically denied it.

Henry emphasizes that while God sometimes makes examples of the wicked "in terrorem—as a terror to others," He commonly allows even the worst sinners to prosper throughout their lives. The commentary stresses that **this world is not the place of final retribution**—that belongs to the afterlife. Henry notes: "the chief reason is because he will make it to appear there is another world which is the world of retribution, and not this."

The discourse highlights **God's inscrutable providence**: "The providences of God, in the government of the world, are sometimes very astonishing even to wise and good men, and bring them to their wits' end."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On handling controversy and suffering**: Job's approach models proper discourse—requesting fair hearing, allowing opponents to speak, and appealing ultimately to God rather than human judgment. "Losers, of all men, may have leave to speak."

**On prosperity's spiritual dangers**: The commentary warns that material success often hardens people in sin, noting "the prosperity of fools destroys them, by hardening them in sin." Prosperous sinners become spiritually complacent, thinking they need neither God nor religion.

**On divine mystery**: When God's ways seem incomprehensible, believers should "sit down and admire them" rather than hastily judge, "lay your hand upon your mouth, silently wait the issue, and judge nothing before the time."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6**: Job requests patient hearing as basic courtesy, arguing his case ultimately rests with God, not human judges. His situation is so extraordinary that even he trembles remembering it.

**Verses 7-13**: Henry details how wicked people often enjoy complete prosperity—longevity, power, family success, security, wealth, and joy—spending "all their days in wealth" and dying peacefully "in a moment."

**Verses 14-16**: Prosperity breeds spiritual arrogance. The wicked tell God to "Depart from us" and ask "What is the Almighty, that we should serve him?" They actively avoid God's knowledge and presence.

**Verses 17-26**: Henry notes the "very great variety in the ways of God's providence," showing divine judgment doesn't follow human expectations or timelines.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout:

- **Psalm 73:7-9** and **Proverbs 1:32** on prosperity destroying fools
- **Isaiah 65:20** regarding sinners living to old age
- **Matthew 25:41** on divine judgment ("Depart")
- **John 3:19** on preferring darkness to light
- **Job 17:8** on upright men being astonished at providence
- **Genesis 18:19** contrasting Abraham's child-rearing with worldly parents
- **Exodus 23:26** and **Deuteronomy 7:14** on promised prosperity

Henry concludes that **"in this Job was clearly in the right"**—the destruction of the wicked is often "reserved for the other world," challenging simplistic views of divine justice operating primarily through earthly circumstances.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job 22: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**God's Independence from Human Merit**: Henry emphasizes that God receives no benefit from human righteousness. As Eliphaz argues (though wrongly applied to Job), "Our wisdom and piety are that by which we ourselves are...great gainers," but "can a man be thus profitable to God? No, for such is the perfection of God that he cannot receive any benefit or advantage by men." This establishes that God's actions toward humans are not based on debt or obligation.

**Divine Justice vs. Human Judgment**: Henry highlights the danger of false accusations among believers. He notes that "Eliphaz, in accusing Job falsely...was guilty of as great a sin and as great a wrong to Job as the Sabeans and Chaldeans that robbed him; for a man's good name is more precious and valuable than his wealth."

**The Problem of Inferring Sin from Suffering**: The commentary reveals how Job's friends erroneously conclude that extraordinary suffering must indicate extraordinary sin, leading to specific but unfounded accusations of oppression, injustice, and atheism.

## Key Practical Applications

**Guarding Against False Accusations**: Henry warns against "all the laws of justice, charity, and friendship" when we "either raise or receive calumnies, jealousies, and evil surmises, concerning others." He emphasizes it's "more base and disingenuous if we thus vex those that are in distress and add to their affliction."

**Responding to Unjust Criticism**: When falsely accused, believers should "learn how to pass by evil report as well as good, and commit our cause, as Job did his, to him that judgeth righteously." Henry notes that "Innocency itself will be no security against a false and foul tongue."

**Understanding God's Motives**: Unlike human rulers who act from fear or self-interest, "God does not...pervert justice for fear of any." His corrections come from love, not insecurity about His position.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-4**: Henry explains that while Eliphaz's theology about God's independence is "very true and good," it was "unjustly applied to Job." The commentary emphasizes that God needs neither our service nor fears our greatness.

**Verses 5-11**: Henry details how Eliphaz's specific accusations of oppression were "utterly false, as appears by the account Job gives of himself (ch. xxix. 12) and the character God gave of him." Yet Eliphaz "ventures to descend to particulars...laying to his charge that which he knew not."

**Verses 12-14**: The accusation of practical atheism - that Job believed God was too distant to observe human affairs - represents the climax of Eliphaz's misguided argument.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the themes to multiple biblical passages:
- **Romans 11:35**: "Who has given to him, and it shall be recompensed to him again?" supporting God's independence from human merit
- **1 Timothy 4:8**: "Godliness is profitable to all things" - showing religion's benefit to believers themselves
- **Proverbs 9:12**: "If thou be wise, thou shalt be wise for thyself"
- **Luke 14:12-14**: Christ's teaching against partiality to the rich
- **Exodus 22:26** and **Deuteronomy 24:13**: Laws protecting debtors that Eliphaz falsely accuses Job of violating

Henry concludes that this chapter demonstrates both sound theology misapplied and the danger of judging others' hearts based on their circumstances, making it a cautionary tale about pastoral care and Christian friendship.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job 23 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job 23 as illustrating "a struggle between flesh and spirit, fear and faith throughout this chapter." The central theological tension involves Job's desire to appeal directly to God while experiencing divine withdrawal. Henry notes that Job's day was "like that spoken of, Zech. xiv. 6, 7, neither perfectly clear nor perfectly dark, but 'at evening time it was light.'"

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job 24: Divine Providence and the Prosperity of the Wicked

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**The Mystery of Divine Justice**: Henry presents Job's central argument that God's judgment is not immediately visible despite His omniscience. While "times are not hidden from the Almighty," those who know God "do not presently see his day" of judgment. Henry emphasizes that we must hold fast to "general truths" about God's character even when we cannot reconcile them with particular events we observe.

**Divine Omniscience vs. Hidden Judgments**: The commentary stresses that God's complete knowledge of all times—past, present, and future—doesn't negate the reality that His judgments often work invisibly. Henry notes that "the curse they laid themselves under working invisibly" suggests God's justice operates beyond immediate observation.

**Theodicy and Faith**: Henry frames this chapter as addressing why "judgment is deferred," arguing that God uses these delays to "exercise their faith and patience, and excite their prayers for the coming of his kingdom."

## Key Practical Applications

**Social Justice Concerns**: Henry provides extensive commentary on oppression of the poor, widows, and orphans. He emphasizes that "Cruelty to the poor is great wickedness and cries aloud for vengeance" and warns that "Those who show no mercy to such as lie at their mercy shall themselves have judgment without mercy."

**Warning Against Exploitation**: The commentary strongly condemns various forms of economic oppression—removing landmarks (property theft), taking advantage of the fatherless and widows, and exploiting laborers. Henry specifically notes that masters who deny basic necessities to workers "forget that they have a Master in heaven."

**Patience in Suffering**: Henry encourages believers to maintain faith despite apparent injustices, noting that God will "exercise their faith and patience" through these trials.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry interprets "times are not hidden from the Almighty" as affirming God's complete temporal knowledge while acknowledging that "wise and good people" don't immediately see His judgments unfold.

**Verses 2-4**: The commentary details property crimes and personal abuses, with Henry noting that removing landmarks was "forbidden by the law of Moses...under a curse" and comparing modern deed fraud to ancient boundary theft.

**Verses 5-8**: Henry characterizes robbers as "wild asses in the desert, untamed, untractable, unreasonable," emphasizing their systematic approach to evil as they "rise betimes for a prey."

**Verse 12**: The commentary highlights urban oppression, noting that even in cities where justice should be more accessible, "Men groan from out of the city" while oppressors remain "inexorable and deaf to their groans."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 25: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Bildad's brief response as fundamentally about the **infinite distance between God and man**. The central theological theme contrasts God's absolute sovereignty with human frailty and sinfulness. Henry interprets this chapter as teaching two essential lessons: "To think highly and honourably of God" and "To think meanly of ourselves."

The commentary emphasizes God's **perfect sovereignty** - "He that gave being has an incontestable authority to give laws" - and His **irresistible power** through "numberless numbers of armies at his beck and disposal." Henry highlights the **universal scope of God's providence**: "Upon whom does not his light arise?" demonstrating that God's knowledge and goodness extend to all creation.

Regarding human nature, Henry addresses both **actual guilt** ("man, by reason of his actual transgressions, is obnoxious to God's justice") and **original corruption** ("by reason of his original corruption, as he is born of a woman, is odious to God's holiness").

## Key Practical Applications

Henry commends Bildad for **knowing when to end a debate**, noting that "if disputants would understand one another better, perhaps they would find themselves nearer one another than they imagined." This teaches wisdom in theological discussions.

The primary application centers on cultivating proper perspectives: **"If we would all get our hearts filled with awful thoughts of God and humble thoughts of ourselves, we should not be so apt as we are to fall out about matters of doubtful disputation."**

Henry emphasizes the need for spiritual transformation: "We have need therefore to be born again of water and of the Holy Ghost, and to be bathed again and again in the blood of Christ."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry explains God's dominion as both ownership and rulership, noting that "He maketh peace in his high places" refers to perfect heavenly harmony where "holy angels never quarrel with him, nor with one another."

**Verse 4**: The rhetorical question about justification leads Henry to explain man cannot be clean "(1.) In comparison with God... (2.) In debate with God... (3.) In the sight of God."

**Verses 5-6**: Henry's most memorable imagery appears here. Even celestial bodies "have no glory, by reason of the glory which excelleth, as a candle, though it burn, yet does not shine when it is set in the clear light of the sun." Humans are "worms" - "not only mean and despicable, but noxious and detestable."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: **Daniel 4:35** (God's absolute will), **Psalm 107:29** and **65:7** (God commanding peace), **Luke 2:14** (heavenly hosts proclaiming peace), **Matthew 5:45** (universal providence), **Psalm 51:4** and **Romans 3:4** (God's perfect justice), **Isaiah 24:23** (God's glory eclipsing creation), and **Psalm 22:6** (Christ as worm).

The commentary concludes with wonder at "God's condescension in taking such worms as we are into covenant and communion with himself, especially at the condescension of the Son of God" who said "I am a worm, and no man."

Henry transforms Bildad's harsh words into a foundation for understanding both human need for redemption and divine grace in providing it.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 26: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job's response as a masterful vindication of God's infinite power and wisdom. The central theological theme is **God's incomprehensible sovereignty** over all creation - from the depths of hell to the heights of heaven. Henry emphasizes that when believers unite in glorifying God rather than disputing, "truth triumphs" and "brightly shines." He notes how "truth received a great deal of light" when Job and his friends focused on their shared belief in God's glory rather than their differences.

A key interpretative insight is Henry's understanding of divine hiddenness - that God deliberately "holds back the face of his throne" and "spreads his clouds upon it," requiring us to "live by faith, not by sense" in our current "state of probation."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Proper Christian discourse**: Ministers should "learn rightly to divide the word of truth" and speak what is "suitable and seasonable." To the "humbled, and broken, and grieved in spirit," preachers should proclaim "grace and mercy" rather than only "greatness and majesty."

**Humility in theological discussion**: Even when defending truth, we must not think God "needs our service" or accept it if it comes "from a spirit of contention and contradiction" rather than "sincere regard to God's glory."

**Perspective on earthly things**: Since earth "hangs upon nothing," it "will never serve us to set our hearts on, nor bear the weight of our souls."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-4**: Henry notes Job's ironic rebuke of Bildad's self-congratulation, showing how "weak disputants, when warm, are apt to think truth more beholden to them than it really is."

**Verse 7**: The earth "hangs upon nothing" - "The art of man could not hang a feather upon nothing, yet the divine wisdom hangs the whole earth so."

**Verse 14**: Henry's memorable conclusion: "Lo, these are parts of his ways: but how little a portion is heard of him?" emphasizes that even our greatest theological insights only scratch the surface of God's infinite nature.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Job's words to other biblical texts:
- **Job 13:2** - "What you know, the same do I know also"
- **Romans 15:6** - glorifying God "with one mind and one mouth"
- **Isaiah 50:4** - Christ knowing "how to speak what is proper for the weary"
- **Psalm 104:2** - God stretching "out the heavens like a curtain"
- **Psalm 33:6** - creation by "the breath of his mouth"
- **Revelation 6:14** - heavens "rolled together as a scroll"

The commentary particularly emphasizes how Job's description of God's power over the sea connects to both the natural order (Jeremiah 5:22) and redemptive history, possibly alluding to the Red Sea crossing and Egypt's destruction.

Henry concludes that all human attempts to describe God's glory inevitably "come short of the merit of the subject" - our finest theological discourse remains "far from being exhausted" when confronting the infinite majesty of the Creator.

## Matthew Henry Commentary on Job 27: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job's discourse as a triumphant moment where "great is the truth and will prevail." Job's friends have essentially conceded defeat, being either convinced of Job's righteousness or despairing of convincing him otherwise. The chapter explores three major theological themes: the nature of sworn integrity, the horror of hypocrisy, and divine justice.

Henry emphasizes that Job's oath, while containing some "unbecoming" complaints against God (saying God had "taken away his judgment"), demonstrates remarkable faith. ### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Job's protestation of integrity. Most notably, he advocates for unwavering honesty: "we must not bear false witness against our neighbour, then not against ourselves." Job's resolve never to "speak wickedness" should inspire lifelong commitments to truth-telling.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verses 2-4, Henry observes the striking contrast in Job's oath formula - while appealing to "the living God," Job simultaneously accuses God of injustice. Yet this demonstrates that "our breath will not always be in us" and is called "the spirit of God, because he breathed it into us."

Verses 7-10 receive extended treatment as Henry explores Job's expression of horror at hypocrisy. The rhetorical question "what is the hope of the hypocrite?" leads to Henry's analysis of how hypocrites may "gain" temporarily but face ultimate disappointment when "God taketh away his soul." Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout his commentary. He references Hebrews 6:16 regarding oaths ending strife, and Hebrews 13:18 about maintaining good conscience. The hypocrite's false hope connects to earlier speeches by Bildad (Job 8:13-14) and Zophar (Job 11:20).

Particularly striking are Henry's New Testament parallels: Luke 12:20 ("Thy soul shall be required of thee") and the sobering warning "Depart from me, I know you not" for those whose religious profession proves superficial. # Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 28: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry views this chapter as Job's philosophical discourse on the limits of human wisdom versus divine wisdom. The central theological theme revolves around **the hiddenness of God's providential counsels**. Job demonstrates that while humans can master natural philosophy and discover earthly treasures through ingenuity, "the caverns of the earth may be discovered, but not the counsels of heaven."

Henry interprets this as Job's response to his friends' attempts to explain God's justice—essentially arguing that **"secret things belong not to us, but things revealed" (Deut. 29:29)**. The commentary distinguishes between worldly wisdom (human achievement in mining and natural discovery) and true wisdom, which "is found nowhere but in the principles and practices of religion."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from the mining metaphor:

**For Spiritual Diligence**: He challenges readers to emulate miners' dedication: "Go to the miners then, thou sluggard in religion; consider their ways, and be wise. Let their courage, diligence, and constancy in seeking the wealth that perisheth shame us out of slothfulness and faint-heartedness in labouring for the true riches."

**For Proper Valuation**: Henry emphasizes that God placed earthly treasures "out of our sight, to teach us not to set our eyes upon them" and "under our feet, to teach us not to lay them in our bosoms, nor to set our hearts upon them, but to trample upon them with a holy contempt."

**For Understanding Divine Providence**: Rather than demanding explanations for why some wicked prosper while others suffer, believers should accept that God's reasons remain hidden and focus on revealed truth.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich commentary on the mining passages (vv. 1-11), noting how **"God has made the getting of gold, and silver, and precious stones, so difficult"** for two purposes: "(1.) For the exciting and engaging of industry" and "(2.) For the checking and restraining of pomp and luxury."

On wisdom's inestimable value (vv. 15-19), he observes that wisdom **"cannot be bought with money"** because **"it is a gift of the Holy Ghost, which cannot be bought with money" (Acts 8:20)**. He emphasizes that **"few put a due value upon it"** and **"none can possibly give a valuable consideration for it."**

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Job's teaching to multiple scriptures: Deuteronomy 29:29 on secret versus revealed things, Proverbs 23:5 on not setting eyes upon riches, Psalm 24:1 on God's ownership of earth's fullness, and Acts 8:20 on spiritual gifts being beyond monetary purchase. He also references Proverbs 17:16 about having "no heart" for wisdom despite having the means to acquire it.

The commentary concludes that true wisdom lies in **"sound divinity, which shows us the way to the joys of heaven"** rather than in natural philosophy that merely helps us **"find a way into the bowels of the earth."**

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Job Chapter 29: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets Job 29 as Job's nostalgic reflection on his former prosperity, serving both to "move the pity of his friends" and "justify his own complaints." The primary theological theme centers on **divine favor as the foundation of all blessing**. Henry emphasizes that Job attributed his prosperity not to fortune or personal merit, but to God's protection and presence. 

A key theological insight is Henry's distinction between material and spiritual prosperity. Job's truest joy came from four divine blessings: God's protection ("the days when God preserved me"), divine favor ("God's candle shone upon his head"), communion with God ("the secret of God was upon my tabernacle"), and assurance of God's presence ("the Almighty was yet with me").

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**Verses 7-10**: Henry observes that judgment occurred publicly "in the gate, in the street" for transparency, and notes how Job's character commanded respect across all social levels.

**Verses 11-17**: Henry emphasizes Job's reputation was built on practical righteousness - delivering the poor, helping the fatherless, and being "eyes to the blind, and feet to the lame."

- **Psalm 30:7** - "Thou, by thy favour, hast made my mountain stand strong" (on attributing prosperity to God's favor)

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job Chapter 31 - Summary

Matthew Henry presents Job's lengthy self-vindication as a comprehensive moral inventory that confirms "God's character of him, that there was none like him in the earth." **Verses 13-15**: Job's treatment of servants reveals his understanding of common humanity: recognizing that the same God who made him also made his servants.

- **Isaiah 33:15** - describing the righteous person's clean hands

The commentary positions Job 31 as both vindication of Job's character and a timeless moral standard, demonstrating that true righteousness encompasses both heart and hands, both private thoughts and public actions.

**Respectful Discourse**: Henry commends Elihu's patience in waiting for his elders to speak first, modeling proper respect for age and experience while maintaining commitment to truth. Young people should "be swift to hear the sentiments of others and slow to speak our own."

**Balanced Judgment**: Henry praises Elihu's impartiality, noting he "took part with neither" but addressed faults on both sides. This teaches believers to "separate between the precious and the vile" rather than wholesale acceptance or rejection.

**Righteous Anger**: When God's honor is at stake, anger can be appropriate. Henry notes it's "no breach of the law of meekness to be angry at our friends when they are offensive to God."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5**: The three friends ceased speaking because Job was "righteous in his own eyes," but Henry suggests this was merely to "save their own credit" when they found themselves intellectually defeated.

**Verses 6-10**: Elihu's humility is exemplary - though young and able, he was "not pert, and confident, and assuming." Henry emphasizes that "Great men are not always wise" (v.9), challenging automatic deference to authority.

**Verses 11-14**: Elihu's patient listening entitled him to speak and enabled proper judgment. Henry warns against those who answer "a matter before he hears it."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Job 33: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Job 33 as Elihu's direct address to Job, offering a more balanced theological perspective than Job's three friends. The central theological theme is **God's sovereign yet gracious dealings with humanity**. Unlike the friends who condemned Job entirely, Elihu acknowledges Job's general righteousness while correcting specific errors.

The commentary emphasizes **God's absolute greatness and authority**: "God is greater than man" serves as Elihu's foundational argument. A key theme is **God's redemptive purpose in suffering**. Henry draws several practical lessons. **Honest communication** is essential - Elihu declares his words "shall be of the uprightness of my heart," contrasting with those who condemn others merely "to serve a turn" while thinking well of them privately.

**Humble acceptance of God's providence** emerges as crucial. Henry argues we fail to "render to God his due" unless we "acknowledge his equity and kindness in all his dispensations."

On verses 1-7, Henry highlights Elihu's careful approach - speaking with "deliberation and design," promising clarity, and positioning himself as the mediator Job had wished for. Significantly, Elihu offers to be "in God's stead" while remaining approachable: "I also am formed out of the clay."

Regarding verses 8-11, Henry notes Elihu's fairness in charging Job only with specific statements heard publicly, not private conversations or secondhand reports. However, he suggests Elihu somewhat misrepresented Job's claims of innocence.

For verses 12-13, Henry emphasizes the key phrase "in this thou art not just," showing Elihu's balanced approach - not condemning Job entirely but correcting specific errors.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Elihu's clay imagery to **Genesis 2:7** (man's formation from dust) and **Job 10:9** (Job's own appeal to his clay origins). The concept of speaking "in God's stead" references **Deuteronomy 5:24** regarding Israel's preference for human mediators.

The commentary's emphasis on God's ways being beyond human understanding implicitly references passages about divine inscrutability throughout Scripture.

Henry concludes that this chapter demonstrates how God uses various means - including human counselors like Elihu - to bring conviction and restoration to his people.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Job Chapter 35

## Main Theological Themes

Matthew Henry presents Job 35 as Elihu's third rebuke addressing Job's perceived theological errors. The central theme is **God's transcendent independence from human actions**—that the Almighty neither gains from human righteousness nor suffers loss from human sin. A second major theme concerns **proper response to suffering**. Henry argues that the oppressed often fail to receive divine help not because God is deaf, but because their cries are "complaining cries, not penitent praying cries—the cry of nature and passion, not of grace."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On Religious Motivation**: Believers should not expect material profit from righteousness, as "we can demand nothing from [God], nor have any reason to complain if we have not what we expect, but to be thankful that we have better than we deserve."

**On Suffering and Prayer**: Those experiencing oppression must examine their hearts, asking not merely for relief but seeking God himself: "Where is God my Maker?" Henry notes that affliction should "drive them to God" rather than merely produce complaints.

**On Gratitude in Trials**: Even in darkness, God "gives songs in the night"—providing inner comfort and joy that should lead to thanksgiving rather than despair.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry acknowledges that Elihu's charge that Job claimed "My righteousness is more than God's" was somewhat unfair, noting "it was not fair to charge these words upon Job" when Job had actually attributed such sentiments to the wicked.

**Verses 5-8**: The call to "Look unto the heavens" serves as an "ocular demonstration" of God's transcendence. **Verses 9-13**: Henry explains that unanswered prayers often result from improper motives, not divine indifference. The oppressed fail because "none saith, Where is God my Maker?"

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry skillfully weaves biblical connections throughout:
- **Romans 11:35** on God's independence: "Who hath first given to God...and it shall be recompensed to him again?"
