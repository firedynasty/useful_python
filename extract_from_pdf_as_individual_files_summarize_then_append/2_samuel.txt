# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel: Introduction Summary

## **Main Theological Themes and Interpretations**

Matthew Henry frames 2 Samuel as a profound study in divine sovereignty working through human frailty. The book presents David as "very much the favourite of heaven," yet simultaneously reveals the complex relationship between God's choosing and human failure. Henry establishes a theological tension between David's divine calling and his moral struggles, noting that "his honour shines brighter in his Psalms than in his Annals."

The overarching theme centers on God's faithfulness despite human unfaithfulness. Henry structures the narrative around triumph and trouble, suggesting that even God's chosen leaders experience the full spectrum of human experience under divine providence.

## **Key Practical Applications**

Henry draws several practical lessons from David's story. He emphasizes that leadership brings both privilege and responsibility, with failures having far-reaching consequences. The commentary suggests that success can be more dangerous than struggle, as David's greatest failures followed his greatest triumphs.

The introduction implies that readers should expect to find instruction in "many things in his history," particularly regarding the consequences of moral compromise and the need for genuine repentance when failures occur.

## **Notable Structural Insights**

Henry organizes the book into two major sections that reveal his interpretive approach:

**I. David's Triumphs:** Including victories over Saul's house, the Jebusites, Philistines, the successful bringing of the ark, and conquests over neighboring nations. These chapters (1-10) demonstrate God's blessing on David's reign.

**II. David's Troubles:** Beginning with "his sin in the matter of Uriah" and cascading through family dysfunction, rebellions, and national calamities. Henry sees these as directly connected to David's moral failure.

## **Cross-References and Connections**

Henry references the Septuagint's title "The Third Book of the Kings," connecting David's reign to the broader narrative of Israel's monarchy. He links this book to the preceding volume's account of "his designation to the government, and his struggles with Saul."

Most significantly, Henry cross-references David's psalms, suggesting they provide a more complete picture of David's character than the historical narrative alone. This implies readers should study 2 Samuel alongside the Psalter for full theological understanding.

The commentary promises that David's story offers rich "instruction" through both positive and negative examples of leadership under God's covenant.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents several key theological themes in his commentary. He emphasizes **God's providential sovereignty** in political transitions, noting that "the secret springs of revolutions are unaccountable, and must be resolved into that Providence which turns all hearts as the rivers of water." The commentary highlights **divine justice**, observing that Saul, who had disobeyed God's command to destroy the Amalekites, ironically received his death wound from an Amalekite—demonstrating God's poetic justice.

The concept of **reverence for God's anointed** emerges as central, with Henry showing how David's respect for Saul's divine appointment transcended personal grievances. Henry also explores the **danger of unrepentant sin**, suggesting Saul's final anguish reflected his conscience bringing "to mind the javelin he had cast at David, his pride, malice, and perfidiousness, and especially the murder of the priests."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from David's response. He emphasizes **genuine forgiveness and mercy toward enemies**, noting that David's mourning "was not pretended" but reflected sincere grief even for those who had wronged him. The commentary warns against **rejoicing in enemies' downfall**, referencing Proverbs: "if we rejoice when our enemy falls the Lord sees it, and it displeases him."

Henry also highlights **patient waiting on God's timing**. David didn't send spies to monitor Saul's fate, demonstrating that "He that believes does not make haste, takes good news when it comes and is not uneasy while it is in the coming."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides detailed analysis of the Amalekite's account, questioning its truthfulness while noting the irony of divine justice. He observes David's character: "David's breast appears very happily free from the sparks both of revenge and ambition." 

Regarding Saul's final moments, Henry notes the tragic irony that "He is now sick of his dignity and willing to be trampled upon, sick of his life and willing to be slain."

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this passage to various scriptures, including Proverbs 24:17-18 and 17:5 regarding not rejoicing over fallen enemies. He references 1 Chronicles 12 describing David's growing support, and draws parallels to Acts 1:18 and Matthew 27:5 regarding different accounts of the same events. The commentary also alludes to Revelation 9:6 in discussing those who desire death but cannot find it.

Henry concludes that David's actions reveal his true character—one whose harsh psalmic language against enemies stemmed from "holy zeal for the glory of God and the public good" rather than personal revenge, as evidenced by his genuine grief at Saul's death.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes **divine guidance in leadership transitions**, noting that David "enquired of the Lord" before making any move, demonstrating that "he that believeth doth not make haste, but waits God's time." The chapter illustrates God's **gradual establishment of His chosen king**, with David rising "by degrees" - first anointed in private, then over Judah, eventually over all Israel. This prefigures Christ's kingdom, which is "set up by degrees" as the Messiah who is "Lord of all by divine designation, but we see not yet all things put under him."

The **sovereignty of God versus human opposition** appears prominently, as Henry notes "such a spirit of contradiction is there, in the devices of men, to the counsels of God." David's kingdom serves as a type of Christ's, "against which the heathens rage and the rulers take counsel" (Psalm 2:1-2).

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons: **Seek divine direction in major decisions** - David consulted God even when circumstances seemed favorable, showing we must "apply to God not only when we are in distress, but even when the world smiles upon us." 

**Exercise patience in God's promises** - David didn't immediately claim the throne by force but "proceeded leisurely," teaching that "assurance of hope in God's promise will quicken pious endeavours" rather than promote inaction.

**Honor predecessors graciously** - David's respectful message to those who buried Saul demonstrates how leaders should "show respect to their remains when they are dead," proving his motives were pure, not driven by "ambition or enmity to Saul."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-4**: God directed David specifically to Hebron, "a priest's city, one of the cities of refuge," symbolizing that "God himself would be to him a little sanctuary." The nearby patriarchal sepulchres would remind David "of the ancient promise."

**Verses 5-7**: David's message to Jabesh-Gilead shows shrewd diplomacy combined with genuine gratitude, as he both honors their loyalty to Saul and wisely positions himself as Saul's legitimate successor.

**Verses 12-17**: The tragic "sport" at Gibeon, where twelve from each side killed each other simultaneously, illustrates the futility of civil war and opposition to God's will.

## Cross-References

Henry connects David's gradual rise to **2 Timothy 2:12** ("if we suffer with Christ, we shall reign with him") and **Luke 22:29-30** (Christ appointing a kingdom to his followers). He references **Hebrews 2:8** regarding Christ's ultimate but not yet complete dominion, and **Psalm 72:14** about how precious blood is to the righteous king.

The commentary emphasizes that civil conflict in Israel serves as a warning about resisting God's appointed order, while David's patient, God-seeking approach models proper leadership transition under divine sovereignty.

Chapter 3
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 3 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents the central theological theme through the spiritual allegory of David's gradual triumph over Saul's house. He draws a powerful parallel between this political struggle and the believer's sanctification: "The contest between grace and corruption in the hearts of believers...may fitly be compared to this recorded here. There is a long war between them, the flesh lusted against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh; but...corruption, like the house of Saul, grows weaker and weaker; while grace, like the house of David, grows stronger and stronger."

Henry emphasizes divine sovereignty working through human circumstances, noting how "God can serve his own purposes by the sins and follies of men." Even Abner's selfish motivations—first ambition, then revenge—ultimately serve God's plan to establish David's kingdom.

## Key Practical Applications

**On Leadership and Character**: Henry criticizes David's polygamy as contrary to Deuteronomy 17:17, warning that "it was a bad example to his successors." He notes that despite having multiple wives, David gained little lasting benefit, as "three of them were infamous" sons.

**On Pride and Reproof**: Through Abner's response to Ishbosheth's accusation, Henry observes that "Proud men will not bear to be reproved, especially by those whom they think they have obliged."

**On Marriage and Reconciliation**: David's insistence on Michal's return demonstrates "the sincerity of his conjugal affection" and leads Henry to counsel: "If any disagreement has separated husband and wife...let them be reconciled, and come together again."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides interesting details about David's sons, particularly explaining why Abigail's son had two names (Daniel/Chileab): the child resembled David so strongly that he was called "Chileab"—meaning "like his father"—to counter enemies' claims that he was actually Nabal's son.

Regarding Abner's defection, Henry notes that while Abner doesn't explicitly deny the accusation about Saul's concubine, his failure to deny it suggests guilt.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects the narrative to Psalm 127:5 regarding children as "arrows" that strengthen a leader's position. He references Psalm 62:3 to describe Ishbosheth's weakened state as "like a bowing wall and a tottering fence."

The commentary emphasizes how this chapter fulfills God's earlier promises to David, showing divine faithfulness despite human weakness and opposition. Henry demonstrates how even enemies can become "a footstool, not only to be trodden upon, but to ascend by," illustrating God's ability to use unlikely instruments for His purposes.

## Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 4: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine providence** as the driving force behind Israel's unification under David. While David was "at a loss" after Abner's death, "Providence brings it about by the removal of Ish-bosheth." He stresses that David's dependence on God, not human schemes, would secure the promised throne: "God had redeemed him from all adversity hitherto...therefore he would depend upon him to crown and complete his own work."

The commentary strongly distinguishes between **divine justice and human vengeance**. Henry notes that when "the Lord smote Nabal," David gave thanks, "for he is the God to whom vengeance belongeth; but, if wicked men smite Ish-bosheth, they deserve to die for taking God's work out of his hand."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several moral lessons about **laziness and vulnerability**. Regarding Ish-bosheth's midday slumber: "When those difficulties dispirit us which should rather invigorate us...we betray both our crowns and lives. Love not sleep, lest thou come to poverty and ruin. The idle soul is an easy prey to the destroyer."

He warns against **false religious zeal**, comparing the murderers to those who "think to serve the interests of the Son of David by any immoral practices...under colour of religion, murder princes." Henry concludes that "Christianity was not intended to destroy humanity."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **Mephibosheth's disability** (v.4), Henry reflects on divine protection in infancy: "What reason have we to be thankful to God for the preservation of our limbs and senses...through the many perils of the weak and helpless state of infancy."

Regarding the **assassination's treachery** (v.6-7), he emphasizes the heinousness of murdering someone "in his own house, which should have been his castle, and upon his bed...this is treacherous and barbarous."

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Ish-bosheth's fate to **Psalm 91:12** regarding angelic protection. He references the **Amalekite's false testimony** about Saul's death, suggesting the man "lied against his own head." The commentary also alludes to **Jehu's false zeal** and draws parallels to **Simeon and Levi** as "partners in iniquity."

Henry's treatment reveals David as a man of integrity who refused to gain his throne through bloodshed, preferring to trust divine timing over human manipulation.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 5 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents David's full kingship as a **type of Christ's gradual kingdom**, noting that "his advances were gradual, that his faith might be tried and that he might gain experience. And thus his kingdom typified that of the Messiah, which was to come to its height by degrees." He emphasizes divine sovereignty in David's establishment, stating that **God's hand orchestrates all advancement**: "Those who grow great must ascribe their advancement to the presence of God with them."

The conquest of Jerusalem carries **spiritual symbolism** - Henry interprets it as representing how "the Jebusites, Christ's enemies, must first be conquered and dispossessed, the blind and the lame taken away, and then Christ divides the spoil, sets up his throne there."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On Leadership**: Rulers should "feed their people as shepherds, in every thing consulting the subjects' benefit, feeding them and not fleecing them." David exemplifies servant leadership, recognizing his kingdom was exalted "for his people Israel's sake."

**On God's Favor**: "Many have the favour of God and do not perceive it, and so want the comfort of it." Believers should recognize and acknowledge God's presence in their advancement.

**On Unity**: David's acceptance by all tribes teaches that **past faithful service in small matters** prepares one for greater responsibility: "He that is faithful in a little deserves to be entrusted with more."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry notes the tribes' three-fold appeal to David: familial relationship ("we are thy bone and thy flesh"), past faithful service, and most importantly, divine appointment ("The Lord said, Thou shalt feed my people").

**Verse 6-8**: The Jebusites' mockery with "blind and lame" defenders represents false confidence. Henry observes: "The enemies of God's people are often very confident of their own strength and most secure when their day to fall draws nigh."

**Verse 12**: Henry emphasizes David's perception of God's favor as crucial for spiritual growth and gratitude.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout:
- **Hebrews 2:17** - Christ making himself like his brethren (paralleling David's kinship with Israel)
- **1 Chronicles 12:23-40** - Details of tribal support for David
- **Deuteronomy 17:15** - Qualifications for kingship
- **Psalm 89:21-28** - God's covenant with David as type of Christ
- **Isaiah 60:10** - Strangers building up God's people (regarding Hiram's assistance)

Henry consistently points toward Christ as the ultimate fulfillment of Davidic typology, making this chapter fundamentally messianic in interpretation.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Samuel Chapter 6

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents the ark as both "the token of God's presence and a type of Christ," making this narrative deeply instructive for Christian faith. The central theme is God's holiness and the requirement for proper reverence in worship. Henry emphasizes that "in sacred things he is a jealous God" and that divine holiness cannot be approached casually, even with good intentions.

The commentary highlights the contrast between human enthusiasm and divine order. While David's desire to honor the ark was commendable, Henry notes that "a good intention will not justify a bad action; it will not suffice to say of that which is ill done that it was well meant."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for believers:

**On Public Worship**: "Public worship is better the more public it is; and we have reason to rejoice when restraints are taken off." He encourages believers to celebrate when worship receives civil support and protection.

**On Reverence**: Using Uzzah's death as a warning, Henry teaches that "familiarity, even with that which is most awful, is apt to breed contempt." Long acquaintance with sacred things should increase, not diminish, our reverence.

**On Obedience**: The transportation error illustrates that convenience cannot override divine commands. The Philistines could transport the ark in a cart "with impunity" because "they knew no better," but "if Israelites do so, they do it at their peril."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry emphasizes the ark's full title as "the ark of God whose name is called by the name of the LORD of hosts that dwelleth between the cherubims," noting how after long neglect, its honor now revives under David's reign.

**Verses 3-5**: The transportation method reveals a crucial error—using a cart instead of priestly shoulders. Henry observes that "it mended the matter very little that it was a new cart; old or new, it was not what God had appointed."

**Verses 6-7**: Uzzah's death demonstrates that proximity doesn't grant privilege. Though raised around the ark, "Uzzah was a Levite, but priests only might touch the ark."

**Verses 8-11**: David's reaction shows human nature's tendency to question God's justice. Henry notes David "was displeased" with God rather than examining his own error in methodology.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes extensive scriptural connections:

- **Numbers 4:15** regarding Levitical restrictions on touching holy objects
- **Psalm 68** as possibly written for this occasion, beginning with Moses' prayer about the ark
- **Hebrews 10:29** drawing parallels between ark reverence and covenant respect
- **Matthew 28:2** connecting the ark to modern ordinances as "tokens of God's presence"
- **Revelation 12:14** comparing the ark's obscurity to the church's wilderness periods

The commentary masterfully weaves together themes of divine holiness, human responsibility, and proper worship, using this historical event to teach timeless principles about approaching God with both joy and "trembling."

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel 7: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**The Davidic Covenant and Messianic Hope**: Henry emphasizes that God's covenant with David has profound messianic implications, noting "in both these, there is an eye to the Messiah and his kingdom." The eternal throne promised to David ultimately points to Christ's kingdom.

**Divine Sovereignty in Service**: A central theme is that God chooses how He will be served. Though David's desire to build a temple was good, God had different plans. Henry observes that "only that worship is acceptable which is instituted" - David must "wait for a warrant" rather than proceed with good intentions alone.

**Grace Preceding Works**: God reminds David of His past faithfulness before making future promises, demonstrating that human service to God is always a response to divine grace, not an attempt to put God in debt.

## Key Practical Applications

**Contentment vs. Ambition**: Henry contrasts David's humble gratitude with Nebuchadnezzar's pride: "That proud man thought of nothing but the might of his own power...this humble soul is full of contrivance how to glorify God." When God grants rest and prosperity, believers should use it for His glory rather than self-indulgence.

**Encouraging Others' Good Purposes**: Nathan's initial encouragement shows we should "do all we can to encourage and promote the good purposes and designs of others, and put in a good word...to forward a good work."

**Remembering Humble Beginnings**: God's reminder that He "took thee from the sheep-cote" teaches that "it is good for those who have come to great preferment to be often reminded of their small beginnings, that they may always be humble and thankful."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: David's concern for the ark dwelling "within curtains" while he enjoyed "a house of cedar" reveals a heart that "cannot enjoy their own accommodations while they see the church of God in distress."

**Verses 4-7**: God's response shows He values substance over show - "God regards not outward pomp in his service; his presence was as surely with his people when the ark was in a tent as when it was in a temple."

**Verses 12-16**: The promise of an eternal kingdom demonstrates God's faithfulness spanning generations, with conditional discipline but unconditional mercy.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's temple concern to Haggai's rebuke of those "who dwelt in their ceiled houses while God's house lay waste" (Haggai 1). He references Psalm 132:4-5 regarding David finding "a place for the ark" and contrasts David with those in Amos 6:4-6 who lacked concern for God's afflicted people.

The commentary links this covenant to 1 Kings 8:18 where God affirms David "didst well that it was in thy heart," and connects the tabernacle's temporary nature to Christ's earthly ministry: "Christ, like the ark, when here on earth walked in a tent."

Henry presents this chapter as foundational to understanding God's sovereign choice in service while affirming the eternal nature of His covenant promises through the Davidic line.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry's central theme revolves around divine providence and reward for faithfulness. He opens by noting that "David having sought first the kingdom of God and the righteousness thereof, settling the ark as soon as he was himself well settled, we are here told how all other things were added to him." This establishes the theological principle that obedience to God precedes material blessing.

The conquests are presented as divinely ordained fulfillment of God's promises to Abraham regarding Israel's territorial inheritance. Henry emphasizes that "The LORD preserved David whithersoever he went," attributing David's military success entirely to divine protection rather than human strength.

Most significantly, Henry sees David's victories as typological of Christ's spiritual warfare: "All David's victories were typical of the success of the gospel against the kingdom of Satan, in which the Son of David rode forth, conquering and to conquer."

## Key Practical Applications

**Stewardship and Dedication**: Henry highlights David's dedication of all spoils to God for the temple, noting this "crowned all his victories" and distinguished them from worldly conquerors like Alexander or Caesar who "sought their own glory, but he aimed at the glory of God."

**Justice in Leadership**: David's administration demonstrates that leaders must not neglect domestic governance while engaged in external affairs. Henry praises David's impartial justice: "He never perverted justice through favour or affection, nor had respect of persons in judgment."

**Spiritual Application**: Henry draws parallels to personal spiritual conquest: "Thus in the conquest of a soul, by the grace of the Son of David, what stands in opposition to God must be destroyed, every lust mortified and crucified, but what may glorify him must be dedicated."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On the Philistine conquest (v.1), Henry explains that "Metheg-ammah" represents removing "a bridle or curb upon the people of Israel" - when enemies are defeated, their own weapons become instruments against them.

Regarding the controversial Moabite decimation (v.2), Henry suggests it was justice for their betrayal of David's family during his exile, but emphasizes mercy: "the line that was to keep alive, though it was but one, is ordered to be a full line" - showing that "Acts of indemnity must be construed so as to enlarge the favour."

On David's military restraint (v.4), Henry notes David "houghed the horses" because "he placed his strength not in chariots nor horses, but in the living God," demonstrating proper dependence on divine rather than military power.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout: Genesis 15:18 regarding Abraham's territorial promise, Numbers 24:17 for Balaam's prophecy about smiting Moab, Psalm 72:1-2 concerning righteous judgment, and Revelation 19:11 linking David's just rule to Christ's ultimate reign.

He references Luke 11:22 about disarming the strong man, and Revelation 21:24 regarding kings bringing glory into the New Jerusalem, connecting David's temple preparations to eschatological fulfillment.

The commentary concludes by noting how David's diverse servants prefigured varied Christian callings: "God employs his servants variously, some in one employment, others in another, some in the spiritual battles, others in the spiritual buildings; and one prepares work for the other, that God may have the glory of all."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents David's kindness to Mephibosheth as a profound example of **covenant faithfulness** and **divine grace**. The central theme revolves around David calling his kindness "the kindness of God" (v. 3), which Henry interprets three ways: kindness in fulfillment of his covenant with Jonathan, kindness following God's merciful example, and kindness "done after a godly sort, and with an eye to God."

Henry emphasizes that **true friendship transcends death**, noting how David remained "mindful of our friendships, our old friendships" even after Jonathan's death. He also highlights the theological principle of **overcoming evil with good**, demonstrating how believers should show kindness even to former enemies' families.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**Seeking Opportunities for Good:** "Good men should seek opportunities of doing good. The liberal deviseth liberal things." David didn't merely wait to be asked but actively inquired about those in need.

**Covenant Keeping:** "The kindness we have promised we must conscientiously perform, though it should not be claimed." Henry emphasizes faithful performance of our commitments as reflecting God's faithfulness.

**Humility in Receiving Grace:** Mephibosheth's response ("What is thy servant, that thou shouldest look upon such a dead dog as I am?") exemplifies proper humility when receiving unmerited favor. Henry notes: "It is good to have the heart humble under humbling providences."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1:** Henry observes this occurred long after David's accession, noting "David had too long forgotten his obligations to Jonathan, but now, at length, they are brought to his mind. It is good sometimes to bethink ourselves whether there be any promises or engagements that we have neglected to make good; better do it late than never."

**Verse 7:** David's "Fear not" demonstrates how "Great men should not take a pleasure in the timorous approaches of their inferiors (for the great God does not), but should encourage them."

**Verse 11:** The phrase "as one of the king's sons" represents complete restoration and adoption into royal privilege.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary:

- **Acts 13:36** - David "served his generation according to the will of God"
- **Matthew 5:44** and **1 Peter 3:9** - loving enemies and blessing those who curse us
- **Proverbs 18:24** - "He that has friends must show himself friendly"
- **Isaiah 32:8** - "The liberal deviseth liberal things"
- **Job 6:14** - showing pity to friends in misery
- **1 Samuel 20:14-15** - Jonathan's original request for "the kindness of the Lord"

Henry concludes by noting the providence in Machir's kindness to both Mephibosheth and later to David during Absalom's rebellion, illustrating the principle that "he that watereth shall be watered also himself" (Proverbs 11:25).

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Samuel Chapter 10

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a study in divine justice and the consequences of prideful suspicion. The narrative demonstrates how God vindicates those who act with genuine kindness while bringing judgment upon those who repay good with evil. Henry emphasizes that "sinners daringly provoke God, and expose themselves to his wrath, and never consider that he is stronger than they." The account serves as a warning that "none ever hardened his heart against God and prospered."

The commentary also explores themes of gratitude and reciprocal kindness, noting how David's desire to show appreciation for past favors reflects godly character, even when those favors came from unlikely sources.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**On bearing unjust reproaches:** "Let us learn not to lay too much to heart unjust reproaches; after awhile they will wear off of themselves...the injured reputation in a little time grows again, as these beards did." He encourages patience, citing Psalm 37:6-7.

**On mutual support:** Through Joab's military strategy, Henry teaches that "mutual helpfulness is brotherly duty...Christ's soldiers should thus strengthen one another's hands in their spiritual warfare." He emphasizes that believers must "counsel, and comfort, and pray for" one another.

**On charitable interpretation:** Henry warns against unfounded suspicion, noting that "false men are ready to think others as false as themselves" and that "charity thinketh no evil."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides memorable commentary on David's gracious response to his humiliated ambassadors (v. 5), observing David's tender care in directing them to "stay at Jericho" until their beards regrew, showing how leaders should protect their servants' dignity.

On Joab's pre-battle speech (vv. 11-12), Henry highlights the famous phrase **"Be of good courage and let us play the men for our people, and for the cities of our God."** He interprets this as fighting "not for pay and preferment, for honour and fame, but...for the public safety and welfare, in which the glory of God is so much interested."

Henry particularly admires Joab's conclusion: **"Let the LORD do that which seemeth him good,"** commenting that "when we make conscience of doing our duty we may, with the greatest satisfaction, leave the event with God."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple biblical texts:
- **1 Samuel 11:2** - Referencing Nahash's previous cruelty to contextualize his kindness to David
- **Deuteronomy 23:3** - Questioning David's wisdom in befriending Ammonites who were excluded from God's congregation
- **1 Corinthians 10:22** - Warning against provoking God's strength
- **Psalm 7:12** and **Isaiah 5:19** - Describing God's response to those who defy Him
- **Luke 22:32** and **1 Corinthians 12:21** - Supporting the theme of mutual Christian support

Henry masterfully weaves these references to show how this historical account illuminates broader spiritual principles about God's justice, human relationships, and faithful service under divine sovereignty.

Chapter 11

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Samuel Chapter 11

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents David's fall as a sobering reminder that even "the man after God's own heart" can succumb to grievous sin when God leaves people to themselves. He emphasizes that Scripture faithfully records the failures of its heroes "for our learning," serving as both warning and evidence of biblical sincerity. The commentary highlights how sin follows a downward trajectory: "The beginning of lust, as of strife, is like the letting forth of water; it is therefore wisdom to leave it off before it be meddled with."

Henry stresses that this dark chapter demonstrates "what the best of men are when God leaves them to themselves," referencing how God tested Hezekiah similarly to reveal "what was in his heart" (2 Chronicles 32:31).

## Key Practical Applications

The commentary draws several crucial warnings:

**Against Idleness and Neglecting Duty**: David's absence from battle where he belonged created opportunity for temptation. "When we are out of the way of our duty we are in the way of temptation." Henry notes David's afternoon sloth and possible neglect of his usual prayer schedule.

**On Guarding the Eyes**: Henry emphasizes the need for spiritual vigilance, referencing Job's covenant with his eyes and David's own prayer to "turn away my eyes from beholding vanity."

**Daily Prayer for Protection**: The commentary concludes with the urgent need to pray "Father, in heaven, lead us not into temptation, and to watch, that we enter not into it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: David's remaining in Jerusalem while his army fought was the first misstep that led to greater sin.

**Verse 2**: Henry sees significance in David rising from his bed at evening - suggesting laziness and missed prayer opportunities.

**Verses 6-13**: Henry condemns David's attempts to make Uriah drunk as particularly wicked: "Woe to him that does so... Robbing a man of his reason is worse than robbing him of his money."

**Uriah's Noble Response (v. 11)**: Henry praises Uriah's dedication, noting the irony that while David indulged himself, the wronged husband demonstrated exceptional virtue and military honor.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:

- **Job 31:11** - on adultery as "an iniquity to be punished by the judges"
- **Romans 2:22** - on David's hypocrisy as a judge condemning others for sins he committed
- **Jeremiah 5:8** - contrasting David's single failure with habitual adulterers
- **Proverbs 6:34** - on the "rage" of jealous husbands
- **Habakkuk 2:15-16** - warning against making others drunk

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Samuel Chapter 16

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as a study in divine providence operating through human deception and suffering. # Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Samuel Chapter 17: Summary

Matthew Henry identifies **divine wisdom in governance** as a central theme, contrasting David's emotional excess with proper kingly conduct. # Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel 23: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as David's spiritual testament, emphasizing **divine inspiration and messianic prophecy**. The commentary highlights the dual nature of David's "last words" - serving both as practical guidance for earthly rulers and as prophetic revelation pointing to Christ. Henry notes that "The Spirit of God spoke by David" and sees verses 3-4 as applicable both to David's royal line and prophetically to "Christ, the Son of David."

The **everlasting covenant** emerges as the central theological theme. Henry interprets David's covenant confidence as pointing beyond the temporal "covenant of royalty" to "the covenant of grace made with all believers" through Christ, calling it "the sure mercies of David."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**For Leaders**: Magistrates "must be just, ruling in the fear of God" and serve as "keeper of both tables" - protecting both godliness and honesty. He emphasizes that rulers govern "over men—not over beasts" and must "rule under God, and for him."

**For Believers**: The commentary stresses finding comfort in God's covenant despite family disappointments. Henry notes that "the children of godly parents are often neither so holy nor so happy as might be expected," yet believers can declare with David: "this is all my salvation, and all my desire."

**For the Dying**: Henry advocates that those approaching death should "endeavor both to honour God and to edify those about us with our last words."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: David is described in his "meanness of original" (son of Jesse) yet "height of elevation" as both political leader and "sweet psalmist of Israel." Henry observes: "It is true preferment to be serviceable to the church in acts of devotion."

**Verses 3-4**: The promise that just rulers "shall be as the light of the morning" represents both earthly blessing for righteous governance and messianic prophecy, as Christ "is the light of the world."

**Verse 5**: David's confession becomes a model of covenant faith. Despite acknowledging "my house be not so with God," David finds ultimate comfort in God's "everlasting covenant, ordered in all things, and sure."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to other biblical texts:
- **Psalm 72:6, 16** for parallel descriptions of righteous rule
- **Isaiah 11:1-5; 32:1-2** for messianic applications
- **Hebrews 3:3** linking David's house to Christ's church
- **Isaiah 55:3** connecting to "the sure mercies of David"
- **Deuteronomy 1:16-17** for magistrates' duties
- **2 Corinthians 4:8-9** for comfort amid troubles

The commentary presents David's final words as both practical wisdom for governance and prophetic hope, with the everlasting covenant serving as the ultimate source of comfort for believers facing earthly disappointments.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Samuel 24: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as illustrating the complex interplay between divine sovereignty, human sin, and judgment. The central theological tension revolves around God's anger against Israel and how He uses David's pride to accomplish His purposes. Henry notes that "God is not the author of sin" but permits Satan's temptation while using David's sin "to take an occasion to punish Israel for other sins."

The commentary emphasizes that **"God judges not of sin as we do"** - what appears harmless to humans may be grievous to God who sees the heart's motives. Henry identifies the core issue as David's pride, describing it as both "a proud conceit of his own greatness" and "a proud confidence in his own strength."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for both rulers and subjects:

**For Leaders:** Princes should "learn to suspect that their sins are the ground of the controversy" when God's judgments come, leading to personal repentance that influences national reformation. The commentary warns against the "unhappiness to great men to have those about them that will aid them and serve them in that which is evil."

**For All Believers:** Henry emphasizes the value of faithful friends who provide godly counsel, noting how **"it would be good for us to have a friend that would faithfully admonish us when we say or do any thing proud or vain-glorious."** He also highlights the importance of tender conscience, stating it's "a good thing, when a man has sinned, to have a heart within him to smite him for it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1:** Henry reconciles the apparent contradiction between God moving David and Satan provoking him (1 Chronicles 21:1), explaining that Satan suggested it as sin while God permitted it as righteous judgment.

**Verses 2-3:** He commends Joab's wisdom in questioning David's motives, noting Joab's respectful approach: "Now the Lord thy God add unto the people a hundred fold... but why doth my lord the king delight in this thing?"

**Verse 10:** Henry emphasizes the timing of David's conviction - **"his conscience was awakened, and he felt the pain of it just then when he promised himself the pleasure of it."**

**Verse 14:** David's choice to "fall into the hand of the LORD" demonstrates his understanding that **"his mercies are great"** compared to human judgment.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical themes:

- **Exodus 30:12** - The required half-shekel ransom when numbering people
- **1 Chronicles 27:23-24** - The illegality of David's census method
- **Proverbs 6** - Pride as the first of seven things God hates
- The numbering under Moses as legitimate precedent
- Hezekiah's similar sin of pride in showing his treasures
- The promise to Abraham about innumerable descendants

## Conclusion

Henry concludes that despite David's "last works" being "none of the best," David "repented, and did his first works again, and so he finished well." The chapter serves as a sobering reminder that even godly leaders can fall into pride, but also demonstrates God's mercy in correction and restoration. The establishment of an altar represents "God's reconciliation to him and his people," showing how divine judgment ultimately serves redemptive purposes.
