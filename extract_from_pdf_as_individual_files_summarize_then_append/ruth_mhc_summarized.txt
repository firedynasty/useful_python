Chapter 0

# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ruth: Introduction

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Ruth as a book of profound theological significance despite its domestic focus. The **two primary purposes** he identifies are: **"I. To lead to providence"** and **"II. To lead to Christ."** Henry emphasizes God's intimate involvement in private affairs, showing "how conversant [providence] is about our private concerns." 

The **typological significance** is central to Henry's interpretation—he sees "the conversion of Ruth the Moabitess, and the bringing of her into the pedigree of the Messiah" as **"a type of the calling of the Gentiles in due time into the fellowship of Christ Jesus our Lord."** This transforms Ruth's story from mere historical narrative into prophetic anticipation of Gospel inclusion.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws a crucial practical lesson about **acknowledging divine providence in everyday life**. He instructs readers "to teach us in them all to have an eye to it, acknowledging God in all our ways and in all events that concern us." This represents a call to see God's hand in ordinary circumstances rather than only in miraculous interventions.

The commentary emphasizes that Ruth **"relates not miracles nor laws, wars nor victories, nor the revolutions of states"** but focuses on domestic matters—affliction, comfort, conversion, and social advancement—suggesting God's care extends to all aspects of human experience.

## Notable Structural Insights

Henry provides a clear **four-chapter framework**: afflictions (chapter 1), industry and humility (chapter 2), alliance formation (chapter 3), and happy settlement (chapter 4). This structure emphasizes the progression from suffering to blessing through faithful character.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Ruth to **1 Samuel 2:7-8 and Psalm 113:7-9** regarding divine providence. The **genealogical connection to Matthew 1** establishes the Christological significance. He notes the book's placement between Judges and Samuel, showing its **historical bridge function while highlighting its focus on David's lineage**.

The **Bethlehem setting** receives special attention as "the city where our Redeemer was born," reinforcing the book's messianic connections and demonstrating God's sovereign orchestration of redemptive history through ordinary family circumstances.

Chapter 1
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ruth Chapter 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry frames Naomi's story within God's providential care, emphasizing that "all these things were melancholy and seemed against her, and yet all were working for good." He sees God's particular attention to Bethlehem as providential preparation for the Messiah's lineage, noting how "Providence takes particular cognizance of Bethlehem, and has an eye to a King, to Messiah himself, who should descend from two Gentile mothers, Rahab and Ruth."

The famine represents divine judgment for Israel's sins, demonstrating that "when he judges he will overcome." Henry observes the irony that "even in Bethlehem, which signifies the house of bread, there was scarcity," showing how "a fruitful land is turned into barrenness, to correct and restrain the luxury and wantonness of those that dwell therein."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry criticizes Elimelech's decision to move to Moab, calling it evidence of "a discontented, distrustful, unstable spirit." He argues it's "folly to think of escaping that cross which, being laid in our way, we ought to take up." This reflects his broader principle that "it is seldom that changing our place is mending it."

He condemns the sons' marriages to Moabite women as violations of God's law, warning that those who "bring young people into bad acquaintance, and take them out of the way of public ordinances...know not what they do, nor what will be the end thereof."

The deaths teach that "we cannot expect to prosper when we go out of the way of our duty" and that "wherever we go we cannot out-run death."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides etymological analysis of the names: Elimelech means "my God a king," Naomi "my amiable one," while Mahlon and Chilion mean "sickness and consumption," perhaps reflecting their weak constitutions.

On Ruth's famous declaration (vv. 16-17), Henry emphasizes her complete conversion and commitment, seeing it as a model of genuine religious conversion that encompasses people, place, and deity.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the narrative to broader biblical themes through multiple references:
- Links the famine to covenant curses in Leviticus 26:19-20
- Compares Elimelech's journey to Abraham's descent to Egypt and Isaac's move to Philistine territory
- References 1 Timothy 5:8 regarding family provision
- Cites Isaiah 47:9 and 51:19 concerning comfort in desolation
- Connects to Jeremiah 48:11 about Moab's ease versus Israel's trials

Henry's commentary presents Ruth 1 as a complex interplay of human choices and divine providence, where even wrong decisions ultimately serve God's redemptive purposes in preparing the lineage of Christ.

Chapter 2
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ruth Chapter 2 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes that this seemingly humble narrative of a poor Moabite widow gleaning corn serves profound theological purposes. The entire story points to Ruth "being grafted into the line of Christ and taken in among his ancestors," making her "a figure of the espousals of the Gentile church to Christ" (referencing Isaiah 54:1). This transforms an ordinary story of poverty and labor into a picture of God's redemptive plan for the Gentiles.

The commentary highlights God's providence working through seemingly random events. When Ruth's "hap was to light on the field of Boaz," Henry notes that "to her it seemed casual...but Providence directed her steps." He emphasizes that "God wisely orders small events; and those that seem altogether contingent serve his own glory and the good of his people."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws multiple character lessons from Ruth's conduct:

**Humility**: Ruth exemplifies proper response to reduced circumstances. Rather than being "ashamed" to glean, she "cheerfully stoops to the meanness of her circumstances." Henry observes that "High spirits can more easily starve than stoop; Ruth was none of those."

**Industry**: Ruth chooses meaningful labor over leisure, becoming "an example to young people" to "learn betimes to labour." Henry emphasizes that "we must not be shy of any honest employment, though it be mean" because "Sin is a thing below us, but we must not think any thing else so that Providence calls us to."

**Dependence on Providence**: Ruth trusts God to "raise her up some friend" rather than presuming on charity, demonstrating that "while we do well it [Providence] will do well for us."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry notes the "rare and excellent conjunction" when someone is "mighty in wealth and mighty in the scriptures too," describing Boaz as having "might in his name...in him is strength."

**Verses 4-9**: The exchange between Boaz and his workers ("The LORD be with you"/"The LORD bless thee") exemplifies proper master-servant relationships built on "mutual respect" and "joint-dependence upon the divine providence."

**Verses 11-12**: Boaz's recognition of Ruth's sacrificial loyalty shows how God notices and rewards faithfulness, with Henry emphasizing the significance of Ruth coming "under whose wings thou art come to trust."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Ruth's story to broader biblical themes through several references:
- **Isaiah 54:1** - Ruth as a type of the Gentile church's union with Christ
- **Lamentations 4:5** - The reality that well-born people may face reduced circumstances
- **Psalm 129:7-8** - The custom of blessing harvest workers

The commentary positions Ruth within salvation history, noting Boaz's lineage from Nahshon (prince of Judah) and likely from Salmon and "Rahab, the harlot of Jericho," connecting her to the messianic line.

Henry concludes that this chapter demonstrates how God's providence works through ordinary circumstances to accomplish extraordinary purposes in redemptive history.

Chapter 3
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ruth Chapter 3 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Ruth's bold approach to Boaz as a complex moral situation requiring careful interpretation. The central theological theme centers on **redemption and kinship**, with Henry drawing explicit parallels between Boaz as Ruth's kinsman-redeemer and Christ as humanity's Redeemer. He states: "This may encourage us to lay ourselves by faith at the feet of Christ, that he is our near kinsman; having taken our nature upon him, he is bone of our bone and flesh of our flesh."

Henry emphasizes God's providential care working through human relationships and the importance of **covenant faithfulness**. The narrative demonstrates how God provides for the vulnerable through the institution of levirate marriage and the kinsman-redeemer system.

## Key Practical Applications

**Marriage and Rest**: Henry views marriage as "a state of rest to young people" where "wandering affections are then fixed." He emphasizes that parents should seek good marriages for their children, considering "that it may be well with them" and remembering "that is best for us which is best for our souls."

**Intergenerational Wisdom**: Naomi's counsel exemplifies how "the younger submit to the elder, and to their grave and prudent counsels." Henry notes that "Age must not make itself a standard to youth," showing Naomi's wisdom in not imposing her own widowhood on young Ruth.

**Character and Virtue**: Henry praises Boaz's character - his diligent oversight of servants, generous treatment of workers, and temperate lifestyle. He "kept good hours" and maintained spiritual discipline, blessing God before sleep.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5**: Henry defends Naomi's unusual plan by emphasizing the cultural context and the participants' known virtue. He argues that "the goodness of those times was such as saved what is recorded here from being ill done, and yet the badness of these times is such as that it will not justify any now in doing the like."

**Verses 8-9**: Ruth's request to "spread thy skirt over thine handmaid" becomes a spiritual metaphor: "Thus must we by faith apply ourselves to Jesus Christ as our next kinsman...and beg of him to spread his skirt over us."

**Verses 10-11**: Henry highlights Boaz's recognition of Ruth as "a virtuous woman" and his blessing of her faithfulness, noting her choice of duty over following "young men, whether poor or rich."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical parallels throughout his commentary:
- **Matthew 23:37** - Coming under Christ's wings as Ruth sought Boaz's protection
- **Acts 9:6** - Submitting to God's direction as Ruth did to Boaz
- **Deuteronomy 25:7-9** - The legal basis for levirate marriage
- **Genesis 34:31** - Contrasting honorable vs. dishonorable treatment
- **Numbers 25:1** - Distinguishing Ruth from other Moabite women
- **Titus 2:5** - Ruth as an example of feminine virtue

Henry's commentary navigates the moral complexity of Ruth 3 by emphasizing the participants' virtue, cultural context, and ultimate divine purpose while drawing rich spiritual applications for Christian readers.

Chapter 4
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ruth Chapter 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes Ruth's marriage to Boaz as a powerful **typological prefiguring of Christ's union with the Gentile church**. The commentary notes that "from this marriage descended David, and the Son of David, whose espousals to the Gentile church were hereby typified." This theological lens transforms the narrative from a simple love story into a prophetic picture of redemption.

The theme of **divine providence** runs throughout, as Henry observes how "Providence favoured Boaz in ordering it so that this kinsman should come by thus opportunely." The commentary presents God's hand orchestrating events to fulfill His redemptive purposes through seemingly ordinary circumstances.

Henry also explores the **doctrine of redemption** through the kinsman-redeemer laws, using the unnamed kinsman's refusal as an allegory: "This makes many shy of the great redemption: they are not willing to espouse religion... Heaven they could be glad of, but holiness they can dispense with."

## Key Practical Applications

**Integrity in Business Dealings**: Henry praises Boaz's transparency, noting he "would not do so mean a thing as to take a bargain over another man's head." The commentary advocates for being "above-board" and "fair and honourable in all our dealings."

**The Value of Virtue**: Henry emphasizes that Boaz loved Ruth because "all her neighbours agreed she was a virtuous woman. This set her price with him far above rubies." This challenges worldly values that prioritize wealth or beauty over character.

**Proper Use of Wealth**: The commentary asks, "What is a great estate good for, but that it enables a man to do so much the more good in his generation?" Henry presents Boaz as a model of using resources for righteous purposes.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-8**: Henry notes the unnamed kinsman's exclusion from biblical record "because he refused to raise up the name of the dead, he deserved not to have his name preserved to future ages." This serves as a warning about the consequences of refusing God's purposes.

**Verses 9-12**: The public nature of the marriage ceremony emphasizes transparency. Henry observes that "the more public the sales of estates are the better they are guarded against frauds," applying this principle beyond mere property transactions.

**Verse 11-12**: The community's blessing comparing Ruth to "Rachel and Leah, which two did build the house of Israel" shows God's acceptance of Gentiles into His covenant people.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects the narrative to broader biblical themes through multiple references:

- **Deuteronomy 25:5** regarding levirate marriage laws
- **Leviticus 25:24-25** on inheritance redemption
- **Proverbs 31:10** on the virtuous woman whose price is "far above rubies"
- **Jeremiah 32:10** regarding property transfer procedures

The commentary concludes by noting that this story was likely "inserted in the sacred canon" to honor David's lineage, connecting Ruth's faithfulness to the Messianic line and demonstrating how God uses faithful individuals, regardless of their ethnic background, to accomplish His redemptive purposes.
