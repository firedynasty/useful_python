# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy: Introduction Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Deuteronomy as "the second law, or a second edition of the law" - not requiring amendments "for there needed none, but with additions, for the further direction of the people." He emphasizes the divine inspiration of Moses' commentary on previous laws, noting this repetition demonstrates "how great were the things of that law which was thus inculcated, and how inexcusable would those be by whom they were counted as a strange thing!"

A key theological insight is Henry's comparison of Deuteronomy to the gospel: "The gospel is a kind of Deuteronomy, a second law, a remedial law, a spiritual law, a law of faith; by it we are under the law of Christ, and it is a law that makes the comers thereunto perfect."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry identifies three practical purposes for Deuteronomy's repetition:

**For the New Generation**: Since "the men of that generation to which the law was first given were all dead," Moses needed to repeat God's law to ensure lasting impression on the new generation entering Canaan.

**For Covenant Understanding**: As Israel prepared to possess the promised land, "Moses must read the articles of agreement to them, that they might know upon what terms and conditions they were to hold and enjoy that land."

**For Pastoral Care**: Henry emphasizes the need for repeated instruction, stating "Precept must be upon precept, and line upon line." He applies this to Christian ministry: "The great and needful truths of the gospel should be often pressed upon people by the ministers of Christ."

## Notable Structural Insights

Henry outlines Deuteronomy's structure systematically: beginning with historical rehearsal, followed by "pathetic exhortation to obedience" (chapter 4), particular laws (chapters 12-27), and covenant blessings and curses (chapters 27-28). He notes the book covers only two months of history, delivered during Moses' final month of life, observing "how busy that great and good man was to do good when he knew that his time was short."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry provides several significant cross-references: Hosea 8:12 regarding the law's importance, Isaiah 28:10 on repetitive instruction, and Philippians 3:1 on Paul's willingness to repeat vital truths. Most notably, he highlights that "when our Saviour would answer the devil's temptations with, It is written, he fetched all his quotations out of this book" (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10), demonstrating Deuteronomy's special authority and Christ's validation of its teachings.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 1 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Deuteronomy 1 as the opening of Moses's farewell sermon, emphasizing **God's faithfulness to His covenant promises**. He interprets the timing as significant - after God's controversy over the golden calf, "the first and surest sign of God's being reconciled to them was the renewing of the tables." Henry sees God's law as evidence of divine favor: "There is no better evidence and earnest of God's favour than his putting his law in our hearts."

The commentary emphasizes **divine progression in the believer's journey**. Mount Sinai represents spiritual preparation through "terrors of the law," but Henry notes that "God knows when they have dwelt long enough in it" and advances believers "from the terrors of the spirit of bondage to the spirit of adoption" (referencing Romans 8:15).

## Key Practical Applications

**For Leadership**: Henry draws extensive lessons from Moses's appointment of judges, praising Moses for not being "ambitious of monopolizing the honour of the government." He emphasizes that "magistracy is a burden" and that "the best magistrates complain most of the burden." Leaders should seek qualified assistants rather than concentrating power.

**For Justice**: Henry provides detailed applications for judges - they must "hear both sides, hear them fully, hear them carefully" and "judge righteously" without partiality. Crucially, judges must "not be afraid of the face of man" because "the judgment is God's."

**For Christian Progress**: When God commands believers to "go forward in our Christian course he sets the heavenly Canaan before us for our encouragement," paralleling Israel's journey toward the Promised Land.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 6** ("You have dwelt long enough in this mount"): Henry sees this as God's gracious timing in spiritual development - bringing people through necessary trials but not leaving them there permanently.

On **verse 11** (Moses's prayer for multiplication): Henry celebrates how "greatly are his desires enlarged when he prays that they might be made a thousand times more!" noting that "we are not straitened in the power and goodness of God."

On **verse 17** (judgment belongs to God): Henry emphasizes that judges are "God's vicegerents" who "act for him" and must "act like him," representing God correctly through righteous judgment.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects the narrative to **Hebrews 12:18** (the mount that burned with fire) and **Galatians 4:24** (gendering to bondage) to show the progression from law to grace. He references **Psalm 147:19-20** regarding God's law as a token of favor.

For church government, he parallels Moses's judicial appointments with **Acts 6:3,6**, where apostles had the multitude choose deacons whom they then ordained.

The commentary consistently draws parallels between Israel's physical journey to Canaan and the Christian's spiritual journey toward heavenly rest, making the historical narrative immediately applicable to New Testament believers.

Henry concludes this section by noting Israel's tragic unbelief at Kadesh-barnea, setting up the contrast between God's faithfulness and human rebellion that characterizes the book's themes.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Providence and Timing**: Henry emphasizes God's sovereign control over Israel's wilderness journey, noting that "though God contend long, he will not contend for ever." The 38-year wilderness period served dual purposes: preparing Israel for Canaan through humbling and mortification of lusts, while allowing the Canaanites' "measure of iniquity" to fill up completely.

**God's Respect for National Boundaries**: A central theme is God's establishment of territorial rights for various peoples. Henry notes that "dominion is not founded in grace" - God's chosen people cannot claim all lands simply because of their covenant status. This demonstrates divine justice extending even to non-covenant peoples.

**Generational Transition**: The commentary highlights the significance that Israel's conquest began only after "all the men of war were consumed and dead from among the people," ensuring "the excellency of the power might the more plainly appear to be of God and not of men."

## Key Practical Applications

**Forgiveness and Family Relations**: Despite the Edomites' earlier hostility, Moses calls them "brethren." Henry observes: "Though our relations fail in their duty to us, we must retain a sense of the relation, and not be wanting in our duty to them."

**Economic Ethics**: Israel must trade fairly with neighbors, buying food and water rather than taking by force. Henry emphasizes that "religion must never be made a cloak for injustice," and believers should "live by faith and not by thy sword."

**Restraint in Power**: When the Edomites would fear Israel's approach, Henry warns of "need of great caution and a strict government of our own spirits, to keep ourselves from injuring those against whom we have an advantage."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides historical context for the giant races (Emim, Zamzummim, Horim) that previously inhabited these lands, showing "how soon the world was peopled after the flood" and that "the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong." These accounts were recorded to "encourage the children of Israel" facing similar giants in Canaan.

The brook Zered crossing marks a significant transition - the complete fulfillment of God's judgment that none from the Sinai generation would enter the promised land.

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this passage to Isaiah 5:8 regarding God's people not expecting to dwell "alone in the midst of the earth." He references Genesis accounts of the various peoples' origins and draws parallels to the antediluvian giants in Genesis 6:4. The commentary also connects to Numbers passages about Israel's earlier conflicts and the census at Sinai.

The treatment of Lot's descendants (Moabites and Ammonites) demonstrates how "children often fare the better in this world for the piety of their ancestors," showing God's temporal blessings extending beyond the immediate covenant community while preserving greater spiritual blessings for His people.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents several key theological themes throughout this chapter. **Divine sovereignty and faithfulness** dominate his interpretation, as God delivers seemingly insurmountable enemies into Israel's hands. Henry emphasizes that "when God pleads his people's cause he can deal with giants as grasshoppers," demonstrating God's absolute power over human strength and opposition.

The theme of **progressive revelation** appears prominently in Henry's analysis of Moses' prayer. He notes that Moses had experienced God's greatness partially and desired to see more, explaining that "the more we see of God's glory in his works the more we shall desire to see." This reflects the principle that spiritual understanding grows through experience with God's faithfulness.

**God's justice and mercy** intertwine in Henry's treatment of Moses' denied request. While Moses cannot enter Canaan due to his disobedience, God grants him a vision from Pisgah as compensation, showing "the denial of that request, but the grant of an equivalent."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living. Regarding **community responsibility**, he emphasizes that the two-and-a-half tribes must help their brethren conquer Canaan, noting "we are not born for ourselves, but are members one of another." He adds that "a good man cannot rejoice much in the comforts of his family unless withal he sees peace upon Israel."

For **spiritual warfare**, Henry encourages believers to remember past victories: "they ought to be encouraged by their former victory to trust in God for another victory, for he is God, and changeth not." He applies this principle to Joshua's encouragement, showing how experienced believers should strengthen newcomers in faith.

Concerning **prayer and submission**, Henry commends Moses' humble approach: "We should never allow any desires in our hearts which we cannot in faith offer up to God by prayer." Even when denied, believers should accept God's will gracefully while still bringing their requests before Him.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-11 (Og's Defeat)**: Henry marvels at Og's physical stature, calculating his height at "three yards and a half high" based on his iron bedstead dimensions. He notes that Og "trusted to his own strength, and so was hardened to his destruction," warning that those who ignore God's judgments on others "are ripening apace for the like judgments upon themselves."

**Verses 12-20 (Land Distribution)**: Henry observes the subdivision of Manasseh, suggesting "Jacob's prediction of the smallness of that tribe was now accomplished in these divisions and subdivisions." He emphasizes the conditional nature of the eastern tribes' inheritance, tied to their military service.

**Verses 23-29 (Moses' Prayer)**: Henry analyzes Moses' prayer structure, noting he pleads both God's past faithfulness ("Thou hast begun to show thy servant thy greatness") and his own spiritual response ("For what God is there in heaven or earth that can do according to thy works?").

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary. He references **Psalms 135:11 and 136:19-20** regarding the conquest of Sihon and Og. For community responsibility, he cites **Philippians 2:4** ("look not at their own things only, but at the things of others") and **Psalm 128:6** about seeing "peace upon Israel."

When discussing Moses' prayer, Henry connects it to **Exodus 15:11** and **Psalm 86:8**, showing Moses' consistent theology about God's uniqueness. He also references **Numbers 32** for the fuller account of the eastern tribes' settlement.

For encouragement principles, Henry alludes to passages about God fighting for His people and the importance of not fearing human opposition, though he doesn't cite specific verses for these themes.

The commentary consistently demonstrates Henry's method of connecting Old Testament narratives to New Testament spiritual principles, making ancient accounts relevant for Christian discipleship and community life.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy Chapter 4 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets this chapter as Moses' "most earnest and pathetic exhortation to obedience," emphasizing three core theological themes. First, the **absolute uniqueness of God's revelation** to Israel - no other nation has "God so nigh unto them" or possesses "statutes and judgments so righteous" (verses 7-8). Second, the **prohibition against idolatry** receives extensive treatment, with Henry noting that Israel saw "no similitude" at Horeb because "to represent an infinite Spirit by an image...is the greatest affront we can put upon God." Third, **God's covenant faithfulness** spans both judgment and mercy - while disobedience brings scattering, "the LORD thy God is a merciful God" who will not ultimately forsake His people.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several vital applications for Christian living. He emphasizes the need for **diligent attention to Scripture**, noting that "hearing must be in order to doing, knowledge in order to practice." The command to neither add nor diminish from God's word (verse 2) applies both to practice - not committing forbidden evils or omitting required goods - and to doctrine, warning against human innovations in worship. Most prominently, Henry stresses **careful spiritual vigilance**: "Those that would be religious must be very cautious...we have great need to look about us and to keep our hearts with all diligence."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights several key passages. On verse 9's charge to "keep thy soul diligently," he observes this requires teaching subsequent generations, making religious education a perpetual duty. Regarding the cities of refuge (verses 41-43), he sees them as types of Christ, our spiritual refuge. The famous phrase "the LORD thy God is a consuming fire, even a jealous God" (verse 24) demonstrates God's intolerance of spiritual adultery, while verses 29-31 reveal that even in exile, those who "seek the LORD...with all thy heart" will find Him.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this passage extensively with other Scripture. He references Ecclesiastes 3:14 regarding God's perfect work, Revelation 22:18-19 concerning adding to Scripture, and Hosea 8:6's argument against idolatry. The Horeb theophany connects to Exodus 19:21 and Numbers 12:8. Most significantly, he sees the entire exhortation as foundational for understanding how "faith comes by hearing" and anticipates the New Testament principle that God's word is the proper means of knowing Him rather than visual representations.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry views this chapter as presenting the **"second edition of the ten commandments"** within a covenant framework. The central theological theme is God's **wonderful condescension** in transforming mere commands into covenant promises, binding both divine promise and human consent. He emphasizes the **progressive nature of revelation**, noting that "the light of divine revelation shone gradually" - the covenant made at Horeb differed from the earlier covenant of circumcision given to the patriarchs.

A crucial theme is **mediation**. Henry sees Moses as "a type of Christ, who stands between God and man, to show us the word of the Lord, a blessed days-man." The terrifying display at Sinai was "a dispensation of terror, designed to make the gospel of grace the more welcome."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes that hearing God's word must lead to transformation: **"When we hear the word of God we must set ourselves to learn it...and what we have learned we must put in practice, for that is the end of hearing and learning; not to fill our heads with notions, or our mouths with talk, but to rectify and direct our affections and conversations."**

Regarding the Sabbath commandment's variation from Exodus 20, Henry draws practical lessons about compassion for servants and honoring God for redemption. He notes this points forward to the Christian Sabbath commemorating Christ's resurrection, "in remembrance of that glorious work of power and grace."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry observes important textual variations from Exodus 20, particularly in the fourth commandment where the rationale shifts from creation to Egypt's deliverance - a type of Christian redemption. The fifth commandment's addition "that it may go well with thee" shows God's comprehensive care for both longevity and quality of life.

The final five commandments being "connected or coupled together" demonstrates that **"God's commands are all of a piece: the same authority that obliges us to one obliges us to another."**

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry extensively cross-references scripture throughout. He connects Moses' mediation to Christ's work, cites Acts 7:8 regarding the covenant of circumcision, references Philippians 3:1 on repetition's necessity, and links to James 2:10-11 about breaking one commandment making one "guilty of all."

The terrifying theophany at Sinai connects to Psalm 50:3-4 as a preview of judgment day terrors. Henry also draws parallels between textual variations in the Ten Commandments and differences between versions of the Lord's Prayer in Matthew and Luke.

**Divine desire for human salvation emerges powerfully** in God's response: **"O that there were such a heart in them!"** - showing that "the God of heaven is truly and earnestly desirous of the welfare and salvation of poor sinners."

## Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 6

### Main Theological Themes

Matthew Henry presents Deuteronomy 6 as a comprehensive "Summary of Religion" containing the foundational principles of faith and obedience. The central theological theme is **monotheism paired with total devotion**: "Jehovah our God is one Jehovah" (v.4) serves as the bedrock truth that "would effectually arm them against all idolatry." Henry suggests this passage contains "a plain intimation of the trinity of persons in the unity of the Godhead" through the threefold mention of God's name while declaring Him one.

The **primacy of love over fear** emerges as another key theme. While Moses begins by teaching fear of God (v.2), Henry notes "he here teaches them to love him, for the warmer our affection to him the greater will be our veneration for him." This represents God's remarkable condescension: "Did ever any prince make a law that his subjects should love him? Yet such is the condescension of the divine grace that this is made the first and great commandment."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes that **religion must be generational**: "The entail of religion in a family, or country, is the best entail." He outlines four practical means for maintaining faith: meditation (keeping God's words "in thy heart"), religious education of children, pious discourse in daily conversation, and frequent engagement with Scripture.

The commentary warns against **prosperity's spiritual dangers**. When Israel enjoys "great and goodly cities, which thou buildedst not" and "houses full of all good things, which thou filledst not" (vv.10-11), they must "beware lest thou forget the LORD" (v.12). Henry notes this makes God's mercy "really much more" valuable because it comes without their effort.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 4-5**: Henry breaks down the love command into five dimensions: sincere love ("not in word and tongue only"), strong love ("with great ardour and fervency"), superlative love ("above any creature whatsoever"), intelligent love ("we must know him"), and entire love ("our hearts must be united").

**Verse 7**: On teaching children "diligently," Henry offers an alternative reading: "whet them diligently upon thy children" - like sharpening a knife by turning it repeatedly, parents should "try all ways of instilling them into their minds."

**Verses 8-9**: Regarding phylacteries and Scripture displays, Henry shows practical wisdom, noting that when "Bibles came to be common among them there was less occasion for this expedient," but the principle remains: making God's word "familiar to us" and never being "ashamed to own our religion."

### Cross-References

Henry connects this passage to Christ's ministry through **Matthew 28:20** (ministers teaching "all that he has commanded, and neither more nor less") and **Mark 12:32-33** (the greatest commandment). He references the **phylacteries** mentioned in **Matthew 23:5** and draws parallels to **Proverbs 7:1-3** regarding keeping God's word always before our eyes.

The commentary demonstrates how Old Testament foundations directly inform New Testament teachings, particularly Jesus' identification of these verses as the greatest commandment, showing the enduring relevance of Moses' instruction for Christian discipleship and family faith transmission.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 7: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies **divine election and covenant faithfulness** as the central theological themes. Israel's chosenness was based purely on God's sovereign grace, not their merit: "The LORD did not set his love upon you...because ye were more in number...for ye were the fewest of all people" (v. 7). Henry emphasizes that "He loved you because he would love you" - divine love flows from God's free will alone. The covenant relationship operates on clear terms: God proves "kind to his friends" who love Him and keep His commandments, yet "just to his enemies" who hate Him.

The theme of **holiness through separation** permeates Henry's interpretation. Israel must remain distinct as "a holy people unto the LORD thy God" (v. 6), which requires complete separation from idolatrous influences. Henry views this as both positional (their special status) and practical (their behavior).

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for Christian living. Regarding **spiritual warfare**, he connects the destruction of Canaanite nations to believers' battle against sin: "Thus we must deal with our lusts that war against our souls...let us not make covenants with them, nor show them any mercy, but mortify and crucify them."

On **marriage choices**, Henry strongly warns against "mixed marriages," noting that "thousands in the world have been undone by irreligious ungodly marriages." He references the New Testament principle of not being "unequally yoked with unbelievers" (2 Cor. 6:14), arguing there's "more ground of fear that the good will be perverted than hope that the bad will be converted."

Henry also emphasizes **complete rejection of idolatrous influences**, comparing ancient Israel's destruction of pagan altars to the burning of conjuring books in Acts 19:19.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides nuanced commentary throughout. On verses 1-2, he notes this was "bloody work...and yet it is God's work, and good work" while carefully limiting its application: "after-ages were not to draw this into a precedent." He observes that under Christ's dispensation, "human blood has become perhaps more precious than it was."

Regarding verse 9's promise of covenant mercy "to a thousand generations," Henry marvels: "so inexhaustible is the fountain, so constant are the streams!" He interprets verse 10's warning that God "repays those that hate him to their face" as divine justice that appears unmistakably as punishment for idolatry.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout his commentary. He links the prohibition against intermarriage to the corruption of the antediluvian world (Gen. 6:2) and Solomon's downfall. The destruction of idolatrous objects connects to earlier commands in Exodus 23:24 and 34:13, later reforms under good kings (2 Chron. 31:1, 34:3,7), and New Testament parallels (Acts 19:19, 2 Cor. 6:14).

Henry references Hosea 14:4 on God's free love, Psalm 21:12 on divine judgment, and Proverbs 11:31 on earthly recompense for sin. He concludes by noting the Jerusalem Targum's interpretation of future judgment, showing continuity in Jewish understanding of divine justice extending beyond this life.

The commentary masterfully balances historical context with timeless spiritual principles, demonstrating Henry's skill at practical theological application.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry emphasizes **remembrance and divine discipline** as central themes. He views the wilderness experience as God's "school" where Israel was "boarded and taught, under tutors and governors" for forty years. The chapter presents God's relationship with Israel through the lens of **paternal love**: "As a man chasteneth his son, for his good, and because he loves him, so the Lord thy God chasteneth thee."

Henry identifies three key purposes for Israel's wilderness trials: **(1) humbling them** to prevent pride from miraculous provisions, **(2) proving them** to reveal their heart's true condition, and **(3) teaching dependence** on God's word rather than material sustenance alone.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Looking backward and forward**: "Look which way we will, both our reviews and our prospects will furnish us with arguments for obedience." Christians should remember God's past faithfulness while anticipating His future provision.

**Trusting divine provision**: The manna teaches that "God can, when he pleases, command support and nourishment" beyond ordinary means. This encourages believers not to "take any sinful indirect course for the supply of their own necessities."

**Embracing divine discipline**: Afflictions should "be engaged and quickened to our duty" rather than cause resentment, recognizing God's fatherly correction.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 3** receives extensive treatment regarding "man doth not live by bread only." Henry explains this both literally (God can sustain life through extraordinary means) and spiritually ("the word of God...is the food of the soul"). He connects this to Christ's temptation, noting the manna "typified Christ, the bread of life."

**Verses 4-5** highlight miraculous preservation: clothing that "waxed not old" and feet that didn't swell despite harsh travel conditions, demonstrating that "those that follow God's conduct are not only safe but easy."

**Verses 7-9** describe Canaan's abundance as both literal inheritance and spiritual type, representing "the gospel church" and ultimately "heaven is the good land."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: Christ's wilderness temptation (Matthew 4:3-4), the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:6), Jesus teaching about anxiety (Matthew 6:25), disciples sent without provisions (Luke 22:35), God keeping "the feet of his saints" (1 Samuel 2:9), and chastening for instruction (Psalm 94:12).

The commentary consistently connects Old Testament narrative to New Testament fulfillment, seeing the wilderness journey as preparation for understanding gospel truths and eternal realities.

Henry concludes that obedience must be "careful," "universal," and flow from "holy fear" - reverence for God's majesty, submission to His authority, and awareness of His justice.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry's central focus in Deuteronomy 9 is the complete rejection of self-righteousness. He emphasizes that Israel's conquest of Canaan comes "Not for your sake" (Ezek. 36:32), making this the cornerstone theme. Henry argues that God's favor stems from His justice against wicked nations and His faithfulness to the patriarchal covenant—never from Israel's merit.

The commentary strongly connects this to Christian theology, noting that "Our gaining possession of the heavenly Canaan...must be ascribed to God's grace and not to our own merit: in Christ we have both righteousness and strength." Henry sees Israel's experience as a type of Christian salvation by grace alone.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Against Pride**: He warns that "it is hard to bring people from a conceit of their own merit, and yet very necessary that it be done." The commentary serves as a mirror for believers to examine their own tendency toward self-righteousness.

**Dependence on God**: Regarding the conquest, Henry notes, "We must do our endeavour in dependence upon God's grace, and we shall have that grace if we do our endeavour." This balances divine sovereignty with human responsibility.

**Remembrance of Sin**: Henry emphasizes the importance of remembering past failures: "We are apt to forget our provocations...and have need to be often put in mind of them, that we may never entertain any conceit of our own righteousness."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry contrasts Moses' description of formidable enemies with the earlier evil spies' report, noting that Moses uses the same facts "to drive them to God and to engage their hope in him" rather than discourage faith.

**Verses 4-6**: The repeated emphasis on "not for thy righteousness" leads Henry to observe that while "all those whom God rejects are rejected for their own wickedness...none of those whom he accepts are accepted for their own righteousness."

**The Golden Calf Incident**: Henry highlights the aggravating circumstances—the sin occurred "in the very place where the law was given" and while "the mountain was yet burning before their eyes."

**Verse 24**: Moses' statement "Ye have been rebellious against the LORD from the day that I knew you" prompts Henry to observe this as "a very sad character Moses now at parting leaves of them."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout:

- **Romans 3:19-20**: Used to prove that human works condemn rather than justify
- **Romans 11:28**: "It was for their fathers' sakes that they were beloved"
- **Ephesians 1:9, 11**: Referenced to show how "boasting is for ever excluded"
- **Psalm 119:120**: Applied to Moses' fear of God's anger
- **Numbers 13:28, 33**: Contrasted with Moses' different use of similar language about the enemies

The commentary consistently points forward to Christian theology, using Israel's experience as a foundation for understanding salvation by grace through faith rather than works.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 10 Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes **God's mercy triumphing over human rebellion** as the central theme. Despite Israel's provocations, God demonstrates His reconciliation through four key acts: renewing the covenant tables, leading them toward Canaan, establishing the Levitical priesthood, and accepting Moses as intercessor. Henry notes how "God's goodness took occasion from their badness to make him the more illustrious."

The commentary highlights the **permanence of God's covenant**. Though the first tablets were broken because "Israel had broken the commandments," God's renewed tables demonstrate that His law remains "a standing pledge of his favour." Henry sees Christ as the ultimate fulfillment: "Christ is the ark in which now our salvation is kept safely, that it may not be lost as it was in the first Adam."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws powerful applications about **grateful obedience**. Moses' rhetorical question "what doth the Lord thy God require of thee?" becomes Henry's framework for Christian duty. He argues that after receiving God's mercy, "it becomes us to enquire what returns we shall make to him."

He emphasizes **comprehensive devotion**: "we must serve him with all our heart and soul, devote ourselves to his honour." This includes both internal dispositions (fear, love) and external actions (walking in His ways, keeping commandments).

Regarding social responsibility, Henry stresses care for strangers and outsiders, noting that "if the Israelites that were such a peculiar people...must be kind to strangers, much more must we."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verses 1-5, Henry observes that "Moses is told to hew the tables; for the law prepares the heart by conviction and humiliation for the grace of God, but it is only that grace that then writes the law in it."

Verse 12's comprehensive requirements lead Henry to note: "Consider what he requires, and you will find it is nothing but what is highly just and reasonable in itself and of unspeakable benefit and advantage to you."

Henry finds significance in Moses' statement "there they be" (v.5), applying it to modern believers: "Thus we may say to the rising generation, 'God has entrusted us with Bibles, sabbaths, sacraments...and there they be; we lodge them with you.'"

## Cross-References

Henry connects extensively to other scripture. He references **2 Timothy 1:13-14** regarding faithful transmission of God's word, **Revelation 14:6-7** for gospel duty of fearing God, and **Psalm 116:12** ("What shall I render?") for grateful response to mercy.

The commentary links Moses as intercessor to Christ, who "as he ever lives making intercession for us...has all power both in heaven and in earth." Henry also connects God's care for strangers to **Psalm 146:9** and emphasizes how Christ fulfills the priestly succession mentioned in verses 6-9.

Henry masterfully weaves together covenant theology, practical ethics, and christological typology to show how God's past faithfulness demands present obedience.

## Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 11: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Deuteronomy 11 as Moses's concluding preface before restating God's laws, emphasizing the **covenant relationship between obedience and blessing**. The central theological theme is that "love will work in obedience, and that only is acceptable obedience which flows from a principle of love" (referencing 1 John 5:3). Henry highlights God's sovereignty over both judgment and mercy, demonstrating how divine power protects the faithful while destroying enemies.

The commentary emphasizes **progressive revelation and experiential faith**—addressing "the seniors among the people" who had witnessed God's mighty acts firsthand, distinguishing their eyewitness testimony from children who "knew it by hearsay only."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical applications:

**Remembrance and Education**: Believers should "remember and retain the impressions" of God's mercies from youth into old age, and diligently teach God's words to children through constant conversation and visible reminders.

**Dependence on Providence**: The contrast between Egypt and Canaan teaches that "the closer dependence we have upon God the more cheerful we should be in our obedience to him." While Egyptians labored intensively irrigating fields, Israelites could "sit in their houses, warm and easy" trusting God for rain.

**Spiritual Vigilance**: Henry warns against spiritual deception, noting that "a little care would have prevented their being imposed upon by the great deceiver."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-7**: Henry emphasizes that God's past mercies—deliverance from Egypt, protection from internal rebellion (Dathan and Abiram)—create obligations for present obedience.

**Verses 10-12**: The land contrast illustrates divine providence. Canaan's dependence on heavenly rain rather than human irrigation shows "mercies bring...the greatest comfort...when we see them coming from heaven."

**Verses 18-21**: The threefold method of maintaining faith—internal meditation, external symbols, and family education—creates generational continuity.

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout: 1 John 5:3 (love and obedience), Isaiah 43:3 (Egypt as ransom), Psalm 65:9 ("river of God"), Acts 14:17 (rain from heaven), Hosea 14:5 (God as dew), and Hosea 6:3 (spiritual rain representing divine comfort). He references the present desolate state of the land as evidence of God's departed blessing, calling it "as barren a spot...as perhaps any under heaven."

The commentary concludes with Moses presenting the fundamental choice: "the blessing or the curse," establishing the covenantal framework that governs Israel's entire relationship with God.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 12: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Deuteronomy 12 as fundamentally about the **second commandment and proper worship**. He emphasizes that Moses begins the specific statutes with worship regulations "because God is in a special manner jealous" about this area. The chapter serves as exposition of the second commandment, establishing principles that extend through the following chapters.

Henry identifies **centralized worship as theologically significant**, representing "those two great truths...That there is one God, and one Mediator between God and man" (1 Tim. 2:5). The requirement for one worship location wasn't merely practical but symbolized unity of the Godhead and prefigured Christ as the singular means of approaching God.

The **progressive nature of revelation** emerges as another key theme. Henry notes God doesn't immediately designate the worship location but reserves this choice "that hereby they might be made to expect further directions from heaven" after Moses' departure.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes that **religious devotion must persist through all life circumstances**—"when we come to possess a good land, still we must keep up the worship of God in Canaan as well as in a wilderness." Prosperity shouldn't diminish spiritual commitment.

He warns against **syncretism and religious innovation**, noting believers must not "indulge a luxurious fancy in your worship, nor think that whatever pleases that will please God." The principle that human preferences shouldn't determine worship practices remains constant.

Henry stresses **complete separation from idolatrous practices** for both "the reputation of their land" and "the safety of their religion," recognizing that remnants of false worship endanger future generations.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verses 2-3** (destroying idolatrous places): Henry observes these locations were chosen for natural beauty or solemnity but "concealed those works of darkness which could not bear the light." Even aesthetically appealing worship settings must be rejected if tainted by false religion.

Regarding **verse 4** ("Ye shall not do so unto the LORD your God"): Henry interprets this as prohibiting the transfer of pagan worship elements into true worship, even "under colour of beautifying and improving it."

On the **blood prohibition (verses 23-25)**: Henry notes the theological significance—"the blood is the life"—connecting this to broader biblical themes about life's sanctity.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the requirement to "abhor that which is evil" before "steady adherence to that which is good" to **Romans 12:9**. He emphasizes that "the kingdom of God must be set up...upon the ruins of the devil's kingdom; for they cannot stand together."

The **1 Timothy 2:5** reference links the centralized worship requirement to New Testament theology about Christ's unique mediatorial role.

Henry's interpretation consistently demonstrates how Old Testament ceremonial laws pointed toward Christ and New Testament fulfillment, while extracting enduring principles about worship purity and devotional consistency that transcend the ceremonial system.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 13 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies the central theme as God's **absolute priority over all human relationships and experiences**. The chapter addresses three potential sources of temptation to idolatry: false prophets with apparent miraculous powers, beloved family and friends, and popular movements within entire cities. 

Henry emphasizes that God permits such temptations as divine tests: "The Lord your God proveth you, to know whether ye love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul" (v. 3). He interprets this as God allowing trials to **distinguish between genuine and false believers**.

The theological principle of **God's unchanging nature** runs throughout—Henry argues that no miracle, regardless of authenticity, could validate worship of other gods because "the divine law against that is certainly perpetual and unalterable."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Immediate rejection of temptation**: "Not only thou shalt not do the thing he tempts thee to, but thou shalt not so much as patiently hear the temptation." Some temptations are "so grossly vile that they will not bear a debate."

**Loyalty to God above human relationships**: When family members entice toward sin, believers must remember these are "ways which they have not known, they nor their fathers." Henry warns against breaking God's law "in complaisance to our friends."

**Spiritual vigilance**: He notes it's Satan's "policy" to send temptations through those we love and trust most, citing how "Satan tempted Adam by Eve and Christ by Peter."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry finds it "strange" that anyone with prophetic knowledge could promote idolatry, comparing it to modern Catholics who "give divine honour to saints and angels, which is no better than going after other gods."

**Verse 5**: The death penalty for false prophets serves both punishment and prevention—"the seducer must die, though none were seduced by him."

**Verses 6-11**: Henry emphasizes the comprehensive nature of potential tempters, from "a brother or child that are near by nature" to "a wife or friend that are near by choice."

**Verses 12-18**: Regarding apostate cities, Henry notes this applies only to those "within the jurisdiction of their courts," distinguishing between internal apostasy and external opposition.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes several scriptural connections:

- **Galatians 1:8** - Paul's hypothetical about angels preaching false gospels parallels the impossible scenario of true miracles supporting false doctrine
- **2 Thessalonians 2:9** - "Lying wonders" that believers must resist
- **Proverbs 1:10** - "If sinners entice thee, consent thou not"
- **1 Corinthians 5:12-13** - The church's jurisdiction over internal members only
- **2 Peter 2:21** - The severity of apostasy after knowing truth

Henry concludes that these severe measures were necessary because "dangerous diseases as these must be taken in time" and the punishment serves "in terrorem—to terrify" others from similar sins.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy Chapter 14: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies three foundational spiritual privileges that distinguished Israel as God's peculiar people, which he sees as "figures of those spiritual blessings in heavenly things with which God has in Christ blessed us":

**Election**: "The Lord hath chosen thee" - not for merit or foreseen good works, but to magnify "the riches of his power and grace," paralleling believers' election in Ephesians 1:4.

**Adoption**: "You are the children of the Lord your God" - Israel as God's family, "a people near unto him, nearer than any other," with each Israelite being "a partaker of his nature and favour."

**Sanctification**: "Thou art a holy people, separated and set apart for God, devoted to his service, designed for his praise."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes that God's people must maintain "sober singularity" from surrounding nations through distinct practices. He draws particularly powerful applications regarding grief and mourning, noting that excessive sorrow is inappropriate for God's children: "We that have a God to hope in, and a heaven to hope for, must bear up ourselves with that hope under every burden."

Regarding dietary laws, Henry sees them as "a mark of peculiarity" that preserved Israel from "mingling themselves with, and conforming themselves to, their idolatrous neighbours." He emphasizes faithful stewardship in tithing: "We must be sure to give God his full dues out of our estates; for, being but stewards of them, it is required that we be faithful."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On cutting themselves for the dead (v.1), Henry offers a memorable analogy: "This is like a parent's charge to his little children...Children, you shall not cut yourselves. This is the intention of those commands which oblige us to deny ourselves...Do yourselves no harm."

He quotes extensively from Jewish writers who understood prohibitions against excessive mourning: when your father dies, "you are not fatherless, you have a Father, who is great, living, and permanent, even the holy blessed God."

Regarding dietary restrictions, Henry notes God provided abundant variety, making violations "inexcusable" when "of all these...they might freely eat."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Old Testament practices to New Testament fulfillment:
- Links Israel's election to Ephesians 1:4
- References 1 Thessalonians 4:13 on grieving "not as those that have no hope"
- Cites 1 Kings 18:28 regarding pagan self-cutting practices
- Notes gospel fulfillment in 1 Timothy 4:4: "every creature of God is good, and nothing now to be refused"
- References Numbers 18:24 regarding Levitical inheritance
- Connects to Jeremiah 2:27 regarding idolatrous practices

Henry consistently demonstrates how these ceremonial laws, while specific to Israel's covenant relationship, point toward greater spiritual realities fulfilled in Christ and applicable to all believers in principles of holiness, stewardship, and hope.

## Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 15: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry identifies three core theological themes in Deuteronomy 15. First, the **year of release typifies gospel grace**, with Henry noting that "This year of release typified the grace of the gospel, in which is proclaimed the acceptable year of the Lord, and by which we obtain the release of our debts, that is, the pardon of our sins." Second, the chapter emphasizes **God's providential care and covenant faithfulness** - God promises to bless those who obey these laws so abundantly that "what they might lose by bad debts...should not be missed out of their stock." Third, Henry stresses that **spiritual laws govern the heart**, observing that "The law is spiritual and lays a restraint upon the thoughts of the heart."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical applications for Christian living. He emphasizes **generous, cheerful giving**, noting that we must "open thy hand wide" and give "freely, for God loves a cheerful giver." He warns against **calculating charity based on potential loss**, condemning the "covetous ill-natured thought" that refuses to lend near the year of release. Henry also advocates for **practical mercy over rigid justice**, explaining that sometimes "there is as much charity in prudent lending as in giving, as it obliges the borrower to industry and honesty."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verses 1-6, Henry interprets the debt release as potentially permanent for poor Israelites, though he acknowledges scholarly debate about whether it was temporary or perpetual forgiveness. Regarding verse 9's warning against evil thoughts, he emphasizes that "thoughts are free from divine cognizance and check" is a dangerous mistake. For verses 13-14 on furnishing released servants, Henry sees this as ensuring human dignity, since "their liberty would do them little good, having nothing to begin the world with."

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage extensively to other biblical texts. He references **2 Corinthians 8:14** regarding wealth inequality, **Luke 6:35** on lending "hoping for nothing again," **Proverbs 22:7** on the borrower-lender relationship, and **James 2:15-16** on compassionate action. He also draws parallels to **Exodus 21:2-6** for the servant laws and **Ecclesiastes 11:3** regarding generous hearts producing generous actions.

The commentary presents these laws as both practical social legislation and profound spiritual instruction, emphasizing that obedience to God's commands regarding mercy and generosity results in divine blessing and reflects the gospel's greater themes of forgiveness and grace.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 16 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry views this chapter as fundamentally about maintaining communion between God and His people through structured worship and just governance. The three yearly feasts serve to preserve "a face of religion" in the nation, emphasizing that regular, corporate worship is essential for spiritual vitality. 

The theological centerpiece is remembrance—particularly of God's deliverance from Egypt. Henry notes that the Passover was instituted "that thou mayest remember the day when thou camest forth out of Egypt...all the days of thy life, as a constant inducement to obedience." He draws a direct parallel to Christian communion: "Thus we celebrate the memorial of Christ's death at certain times, that we may remember it at all times."

## Key Practical Applications

**Corporate Worship**: Henry emphasizes that frequent gathering "at the same place, and by the same rule" keeps believers "faithful and constant" to their religion. The requirement that "none must appear before God empty" teaches that worship demands tangible expression of dependence and gratitude.

**Holy Joy**: Henry stresses that "it is the will of God that his people should be a cheerful people." He notes that if those under the law must rejoice, "much more must we that are under the grace of the gospel." This joy should be inclusive—"we should do what we can to assist others also to rejoice in him, by comforting the mourners and supplying the necessitous."

**Justice**: The establishment of judges reflects God's concern for societal order, with Henry noting that gifts "blind the eyes of the wise, and pervert the words of the righteous."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry explains that observing "the month of Abib" (v.1) meant the entire month was made significant through preparation and reflection, not just the festival week itself. 

Regarding unleavened bread as "bread of affliction" (v.3), Henry notes it was "neither grateful to the taste nor easy of digestion," symbolizing both their bondage and hasty departure. He references Jewish custom where the family head would break the bread saying, "This is the bread of affliction which your fathers did eat," which "explains that saying of our Saviour, This is my body."

The phrase "tribute of a free-will offering" (v.10) beautifully captures the paradox of required generosity—"required of them as a tribute...yet because the law did not determine the quantum...it is therefore called a free-will offering."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects the Passover to Christ as "our passover" (1 Cor. 5:7), urging believers to "keep the feast in a holy conversation, free from the leaven of malice...and with the unleavened bread of sincerity and love."

He links the harvest timing to Leviticus 23:10 regarding first-fruits, and connects the generous giving principle to 1 Corinthians 16:2 and 2 Corinthians 8:3. The requirement for holy joy echoes the New Testament call to "rejoice evermore" and "rejoice in the Lord always."

The judicial reforms connect to broader biblical principles of justice, with Henry emphasizing that proper governance reflects God's character and ensures societal flourishing.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 17: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies four primary themes in this chapter, emphasizing **God's holiness, justice, and divine authority** over Israel's religious and civil life. The theological foundation rests on maintaining **covenant faithfulness** - Henry notes that idolatry is not merely breaking a command but "a transgression of the covenant" that voids Israel's special relationship with God. He describes idolatry as "spiritual adultery, which breaks the marriage bond" between God and His people.

The **typological significance** of unblemished sacrifices receives special attention, as Henry explains they "were types of Christ, who is a Lamb without blemish or spot, perfectly pure from all sin and all appearance of it" (referencing 1 Peter 1:19).

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for governance and spiritual life:

**Worship Standards**: "God is the best of beings, and therefore whatsoever he is served with ought to be the best in its kind." This principle extends beyond ritual sacrifice to all service offered to God.

**Legal Procedures**: Henry emphasizes proper judicial process, noting that even in heinous crimes like idolatry, "they must not punish any for it, unless there were good proof against them, by two witnesses at least."

**Authority and Submission**: Regarding appeals to higher courts, Henry stresses that "it is for the honour of God and the welfare of a people that the authority of the higher power be supported." He advocates obedience "not only for wrath, but also for conscience' sake."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7 (Sacrifices and Idolatry)**: Henry emphasizes that blemished sacrifices are "abomination to God" and connects this to the later prophetic critique in Malachi 1:8. He notes idolatry was "particularly abominable in Israel, a people so blessed with peculiar discoveries of the will and favour of the only true and living God."

**Verses 8-13 (Appeals to Higher Courts)**: Henry explains the judicial hierarchy, noting these difficult cases "hitherto had been brought to Moses" but would now go to "the supreme power." He observes that judges deciding such cases had "the advantage of a divine promise" and "the support of a divine institution."

**Verses 14-15 (Kingship)**: Henry interprets this as divine anticipation of Israel's future desire for monarchy, establishing principles for godly kingship before the need arose.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout his commentary:

- **1 Peter 1:19** - Christ as the unblemished Lamb
- **Malachi 1:8** - Later Jewish violations of sacrifice laws
- **Numbers 35:30** - The two-witness requirement
- **Matthew 18:16** - New Testament application of witness laws
- **Hebrews 10:28-29** - Greater punishment for rejecting Christ's authority
- **Deuteronomy 13** - Related laws about seducing others to idolatry

Henry concludes his section on judicial authority by noting that rebellion against legitimate government reflects rebellion against God, as "the powers that be are ordained" by Him. The commentary demonstrates his concern for both theological precision and practical governance, showing how God's law addresses both spiritual and civil order in the covenant community.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 18: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry divides this chapter into four key themes: the maintenance of religious ministers, warnings against pagan practices, the promise of true prophecy culminating in Christ, and tests for distinguishing authentic prophecy.

**Divine Institutions for God's Kingdom**: Henry emphasizes that "magistracy and ministry are two divine institutions of admirable use for the support and advancement of the kingdom of God among men." The Levitical priesthood represents God's provision for spiritual leadership.

**Christ as the Ultimate Prophet**: While acknowledging the immediate reference to a succession of prophets, Henry interprets verse 15's promise of a prophet "like unto me" as ultimately fulfilled in Christ, "the great prophet."

## Key Practical Applications

**Supporting Religious Ministers**: Henry argues that ministerial support shouldn't depend on generosity but be legally secured: "Their maintenance must not depend upon the generosity of the people, but they must be by law entitled to it." He applies this principle: "He that is taught in the word ought in justice to communicate to him that teaches him."

**Contentment in Ministry**: Those with "God for their inheritance...should not be greedy of great things in the world" but "look upon all present things with the indifference which becomes those that believe God to be all-sufficient."

**Rejecting Occult Practices**: Henry warns that involvement with fortune-tellers or wizards means "they can have no fellowship with God while thus they have fellowship with devils." He finds it "amazing" that such practices exist "in such a land and day of light as we live in."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: The principle that "The Lord is their inheritance" teaches that spiritual calling requires detachment from worldly accumulation while ensuring practical needs are met.

**Verses 6-8**: Henry commends religious zeal that transcends rigid structures: "A hearty pious zeal to serve God and his church, though it may a little encroach upon a settled order...ought to be gratified and not discouraged."

**Verse 13**: The command to "be perfect with the LORD thy God" means giving "divine honours to him, to him only, and to no other."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects these passages to broader biblical themes:
- Links priestly maintenance laws to Leviticus 7:32-34 and Acts 14:16
- Compares the warning against pagan customs to Ephesians 4:17-18: "You have not so learned Christ"
- References the principle of supporting ministers to general biblical teaching about caring for the poor
- Connects the promise of prophets to 1 Samuel 9:6 and Deuteronomy 34:10

Henry concludes that God's provision of "the oracle of urim and thummim" served as "a preservative from all unlawful arts of divination," making it foolish to "consult the father of lies when they had such a ready way of consulting the God of truth."

## Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 19: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Deuteronomy 19 as Moses transitioning from religious duties to interpersonal righteousness, systematically addressing three commandments: "Thou shalt not kill" (vv. 1-13), "Thou shalt not steal" (v. 14), and "Thou shalt not bear false witness" (vv. 15ff). The chapter reveals God's careful balance between justice and mercy.

**Cities of Refuge as Christ-Types**: Henry sees profound christological significance in the refuge cities, noting that "Christ is not a refuge at a distance" but readily accessible through the gospel. Like the carefully maintained roads to refuge cities with their "Miklat, Miklat--Refuge, Refuge" signposts, "gospel ministers are to show people the way to Christ, and to assist and direct them in flying by faith to him for refuge."

### Key Practical Applications

**For Gospel Ministers**: Henry emphasizes their role in removing obstacles to Christ: "They must be ready to remove their prejudices, and help them over their difficulties." Ministers should maintain clear spiritual "highways" to Christ.

**For Civil Magistrates**: Wilful murderers must never find sanctuary in earthly institutions. Henry critiques medieval church sanctuary practices, arguing that proper justice follows Moses rather than Roman precedents.

**For Daily Living**: The accidental death example (axe-head flying off) reminds readers "how human life lies exposed daily, and what deaths we are often in, and what need therefore we have to be always ready."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 4-6** (Accidental killing): Henry emphasizes the distinction between ignorance and presumption. Just as accidental killers found refuge, "Paul that had been a persecutor obtained mercy, because he did it ignorantly."

**Verses 11-13** (Intentional murder): Henry warns that "in Jesus Christ there is no refuge for presumptuous sinners." Those expecting Christ to shelter them *in* their sins rather than *from* their sins will find "divine justice will fetch them even from the city of refuge."

**Verse 14** (Landmarks): Henry applies this broadly to forbid fraudulent property practices, sowing discord among neighbors, and "breaking in upon the settled order and constitution of civil government."

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:

- **Genesis 9:6** - Noah's covenant regarding bloodshed
- **Romans 10:8** - Christ's accessibility compared to refuge cities  
- **1 John 3:15** - New Testament's heightened view of murder's spiritual danger
- **Hebrews 10:26** - No sacrifice remaining for willful sin
- **Ecclesiastes 9:12** - Humanity's vulnerability to sudden calamity

Henry notes that Jewish writers acknowledged the conditional nature of God's promise for three additional cities, recognizing it found "spiritual accomplishment" in Christ's expanded refuge for "gospel Israel."

**Central Message**: God's law perfectly balances protecting the innocent while ensuring justice for the guilty, all pointing toward Christ as ultimate refuge for those who flee to him in faith.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 20: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry views Deuteronomy 20 as establishing divine principles for warfare that emphasize God's sovereignty and mercy. The central theological theme is **divine presence in battle** - "The Lord thy God is with thee" serves as the foundation for courage. Henry interprets Israel's military campaigns as theocratic warfare, where God fights alongside His people. - **Psalm 30** - connects to house dedication practices

- **Joshua 11:20** - explains divine hardening of Canaanite hearts

The commentary consistently moves from Old Testament warfare to New Testament spiritual applications, treating the passage as both historical instruction and typological teaching about Christian discipleship and spiritual warfare.

Henry concludes that these laws reveal a God who is both just in judgment and merciful in His dealings, establishing principles that extend beyond ancient warfare to encompass Christian living and leadership.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry emphasizes the **sanctity of life and community responsibility** as central themes in this chapter. He views the undetected murder law as demonstrating that "blood defiles not only the conscience of him that sheds it, but the land in which it is shed." Henry makes extensive scriptural connections throughout:

- **Psalm 26:6** ("I will wash my hands in innocency") parallels the elders' handwashing ritual
- **Matthew 27:24** shows Pilate's misapplication when he "wretchedly misapplied it when he condemned Christ, knowing him to be innocent"

Henry's commentary consistently emphasizes both the immediate practical wisdom of these laws and their pointing toward higher Gospel standards of justice, mercy, and moral purity.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 22: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies the overarching theme as **preservation**—of charity, order, life, and moral purity. He emphasizes that while these laws "seem to stoop very low, and to take cognizance of things mean and minute," they reflect God's comprehensive care: "because God's providence extends itself to the smallest affairs, his precepts do so." Henry argues that seemingly minor commandments, "being found among the things of God's law, which he has written to us, they are to be accounted great things."

The commentary reveals a theological principle that **obedience in small matters demonstrates great regard for God's law**. Henry notes that "disobedience in a small matter shows a very great contempt of the law, so obedience in a small matter shows a very great regard to it."

## Key Practical Applications

**Neighborly Kindness (vv. 1-4):** Henry emphasizes active love for neighbors through returning lost property and helping with fallen animals. He states this reflects "doing as we would be done by, which is one of the fundamental laws of equity." The application extends spiritually: "If such care must be taken of a neighbour's ox or ass going astray, much more of himself going astray from God and his duty."

**Gender Distinction (v. 5):** The prohibition against cross-dressing serves "for the preservation of our own and our neighbour's chastity." Henry sees this as maintaining proper social order and preventing occasions for sin.

**Compassionate Conservation (vv. 6-7):** The bird's nest law teaches compassion toward creatures and protection of the vulnerable. Henry draws parallels to human relationships, warning against taking "advantage against any, from their natural affection and the tenderness of their disposition."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Safety Regulations (v. 8):** Henry emphasizes the preciousness of human life to God, extending the battlement law to all safety measures: "fence, or remove, every thing by which life may be endangered, as to cover draw-wells, keep bridges in repair."

**Mixed Practices (vv. 9-11):** While acknowledging no inherent moral evil in these mixtures, Henry interprets them as avoiding "conformity to some idolatrous customs" and maintaining consistency in Israel's distinct identity—"all of a piece, all of a kind."

**Sexual Purity Laws (vv. 13-30):** Henry treats these extensively, emphasizing the severity of sexual crimes and the importance of protecting both individual reputation and community purity through the principle "so shalt thou put evil away from among you."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 23: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry identifies **purity and holiness** as the central theological theme running throughout this chapter. He emphasizes that God's people must maintain separation from moral, ceremonial, and natural pollution. The overarching principle is that "the camp of the Lord must have nothing offensive in it" because "the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp."

The commentary interprets the **exclusion laws** (verses 1-8) as protecting the honor and purity of Israel's families, though Henry notes scholarly disagreement about whether this means exclusion from religious services, public office, or intermarriage with Israelites. He sees these regulations as demonstrating God's concern for maintaining **covenant community standards** while showing varying degrees of mercy based on past relationships.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**Military Conduct**: Soldiers must "keep themselves from every wicked thing" because "sin takes off the edge of valour; guilt makes men cowards." He emphasizes that those who "put their lives in their hands are concerned to make and keep their peace with God."

**Times of Crisis**: "Times of war should be times of reformation, else how can we expect God should hear and answer our prayers for success?" This principle extends beyond military conflict to any period requiring divine intervention.

**Treatment of the Vulnerable**: The law protecting escaped servants demonstrates that "the land of Israel is here made a sanctuary, or city of refuge" for those "wronged and abused by their masters."

**Personal Integrity**: Regarding vows, Henry warns that breaking promises to God "would be sin," emphasizing the importance of keeping one's word.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 3-6 (Ammonites and Moabites)**: Henry notes that God judges not just active opposition but omission of help: "God will certainly reckon, not only with those that oppose his people, but with those that do not help and further them." He references the final judgment: "I was hungry, and you gave me no meat."

**Verses 7-8 (Edomites and Egyptians)**: The more lenient treatment teaches that "the unkindness of near relations, though by many worst taken, yet should with us, for that reason, because of the relation, be first forgiven."

**Verses 12-14 (Camp Cleanliness)**: Henry finds profound spiritual meaning in basic hygiene requirements, teaching "modesty and decorum," "cleanliness," and most importantly, "reverence of the divine majesty" because of God's presence among them.

**Verses 19-20 (Usury Laws)**: The prohibition against charging interest to fellow Israelites while permitting it with foreigners reflects covenant community obligations versus general business relationships.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter to numerous biblical passages:

- **Nehemiah 13:1-2** regarding post-exile separation practices
- **Leviticus 18** concerning forbidden marriages that produce "bastards"
- **Isaiah 56:3-5** promising spiritual blessings to excluded eunuchs
- **Psalm 28:4** on God judging according to people's endeavors
- **1 Samuel 7:3** linking reformation to military success
- **Psalm 66:18** on sin hindering prayer

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic approach of finding **spiritual applications** in ceremonial laws, emphasizing that external regulations point toward **inward purity** and **reverence for God's presence** among his people.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 24: Summary

He warns that "where there is not the love that should be, there is an inlet ready to abundance of guilt and grief."

**Economic Ethics**: The commentary strongly condemns creditors who prioritize profit over human welfare. Henry criticizes those who "care not though his debtor and his family starve" as violating both Christian and Mosaic law.

**Contentment vs. Change**: Reflecting on divorce restrictions, Henry offers timeless wisdom: "It is best to be content with such things as we have, since changes made by discontent often prove for the worse."

**Verses 1-4 (Divorce Laws)**: Henry clarifies that "uncleanness" meant something serious but less than adultery—possibly "light carriage," difficult temperament, or disease. The written requirement prevented hasty decisions by creating "room for consideration."

**Verses 8-9 (Leprosy)**: Using Miriam's case, Henry draws spiritual parallels: those under conviction of sin "must not cover it, nor endeavour to shake off their convictions, but by repentance, and prayer, and humble confession, take the appointed way to peace and pardon."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 25: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine justice tempered with mercy** throughout this chapter. **Judicial Restraint**: Henry emphasizes that the forty-stripe limit exists "lest thy brother should seem vile unto thee," teaching that even criminal punishment must preserve human dignity. He notes that "men must not be treated as dogs" and criminals remain brothers who may yet receive God's grace.

**Economic Justice**: The laws on weights and measures receive strong emphasis, with Henry noting that dishonest business practices make one "an abomination to the LORD." The commentary concludes with the Amalek command, which Henry sees as divine justice against those who show no fear of God and prey upon the weak and defenseless.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 26: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as Moses' conclusion to the particular statutes given to Israel, focusing on covenant relationship and grateful remembrance. The central theological theme is **acknowledgment of God's sovereignty and faithfulness** through ritual confession. Henry emphasizes that these ceremonies serve as "reasonable service" - not empty ritual but meaningful expressions of faith that trace "creature-comforts" back to "the fountain of the promise."

The **doctrine of divine grace and human humility** permeates Henry's interpretation. He notes how Israel must remember their "mean origin" - that "A Syrian ready to perish was my father" - to prevent pride and maintain perpetual thankfulness to God "whose grace chose them when they were so low and raised them so high."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from the first-fruits offering:

**Self-denial and Priority**: "What is first ripe we are most fond of," yet God requires these premium fruits, teaching believers "to prefer the glorifying of his name before the gratifying of their own appetites." Those who "consecrate the days of their youth, and the prime of their time, to the service and honour of God, bring him their first-fruits."

**Stewardship and Accountability**: The third-year tithe system demonstrates that "it is fit that God, who by his providence gives us all we have, should by his law direct the using of it." Henry emphasizes maintaining "conscience clear at all times" through faithful stewardship, noting the solemn protestation required ensures integrity.

**Public-spirited Prayer**: Believers must "be public-spirited in prayer, and to wrestle with God for blessings for the land and nation."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On the confession formula, Henry observes it functions "like giving up the bond" - a receipt acknowledging God's fulfilled promises. The phrase about the Syrian father, he notes, could refer to Jacob or his sons, all of whom were "more than once ready to perish, by Laban's severity, Esau's cruelty, and the famine."

The first-fruits offering Henry describes as "like a pepper-corn" - a quit-rent acknowledging God's ownership, since "Whatever we give to God, it is but of his own that we give him" (1 Chronicles 29:14).

Regarding the tithe protestation, Henry notes the Jews distinguished between speaking "with a low voice" (self-commendation) versus "loud voice" (glorifying God).

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes. He references the Feast of Pentecost (Exodus 34:22), the general principle that "all things are clean to us" when we give alms properly, and Solomon's acknowledgment that "There has not failed one word of all his good promise" (1 Kings 8:56).

He also draws parallels to the "Israel of God" in Galatians 6:16, applying the prayer for Israel to the universal church, and connects the principle of giving first-fruits to Jeremiah's words: "I remember the kindness of thy youth."

## Conclusion

Henry presents this chapter as establishing both **ritual remembrance and practical righteousness** - ceremonies that maintain historical memory while ensuring present faithfulness in stewardship and social responsibility.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 28: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Deuteronomy 28 as "a very large exposition of two words" - blessing and curse - emphasizing the **covenant principle of divine retribution**. He identifies the foundational theological truth that God's favor is "the spring of all blessings" while His wrath is "the spring of all curses." 

The commentary reveals God's character through the **order of presentation** - blessings before curses - demonstrating that "God is slow to anger, but swift to show mercy" and "would much rather we would obey and live than sin and die." Henry argues this structure encourages **filial rather than servile obedience**, noting "that obedience pleases best which comes from a principle of delight in God's goodness."

A crucial theological insight is Henry's treatment of these promises as both **conditional sanctions and predictive prophecy** - "delivered not only as sanctions of the law, what should be conditionally, but as predictions of the event, what would be certainly."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes that **material prosperity flows from spiritual faithfulness**: "religion and piety are the best friends to outward prosperity." He connects this to New Testament teaching, citing Matthew 6:33 that those who "seek first the kingdom of God" will have "all other things added."

The commentary stresses **comprehensive dependence on God**: "What a necessary and constant dependence we have upon God both for the continuance and comfort of this life. We need him at every turn, in all the various movements of life."

Henry notes that material blessings serve a higher purpose - "that they might have wherewithal to honour God, and might be helped and encouraged to serve him cheerfully."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: The condition requires not mere hearing but diligent attention - they must "use their utmost endeavours to acquaint themselves with his will."

**Verse 2**: Blessings "overtake" the obedient because "good people sometimes, under the sense of their unworthiness, are ready to fly from the blessing," but God pursues them with His goodness.

**Verses 3-6**: Divine blessing covers all circumstances - "Whether their habitation was in town or country, whether they were husbandmen or tradesmen" - showing God's care extends to every sphere of life.

**Verse 9**: God promises to "establish them a holy people" - Henry notes "those that are sincere in holiness God will establish in holiness."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter to **Leviticus 26** as parallel passages "setting before them life and death, good and evil." He references **Chapter 30** regarding restoration promises.

For the New Testament application, he cites **Matthew 25:37** regarding blessings overtaking the righteous, **Romans 16:25** on God's power to establish believers, and **Matthew 6:33** on seeking God's kingdom first.

**Psalm 65:9** is referenced regarding God's "good treasure" of rain, **Proverbs 10:4,22** on diligent work, and **Haggai 1:6,9** on God's ability to frustrate human efforts.

The commentary demonstrates how Old Testament covenant principles find fulfillment in New Testament grace while maintaining the fundamental truth that spiritual faithfulness leads to divine blessing, whether temporal or eternal.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 29 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a formal covenant renewal between God and Israel, emphasizing that Moses serves merely as mediator—"Moses himself, though king in Jeshurun, could not make the covenant any otherwise than as God gave him instructions." The central theological theme revolves around **covenant faithfulness** grounded in God's demonstrated mercy and power.

A key theological insight concerns **spiritual perception**: Henry interprets verse 4 ("Yet the LORD hath not given you a heart to perceive") not as divine withholding of grace, but as human rejection of it. He explains: "The Lord...no doubt would have crowned all his other gifts with this...if you had not by your own frowardness and perverseness frustrated his kind intentions." This preserves both divine sovereignty and human responsibility.

The commentary emphasizes the **inclusive nature of God's covenant**, extending beyond ethnic boundaries to include "the stranger that was in their camp"—which Henry sees as "an early indication of favour to the Gentiles."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for covenant living:

**Gratitude-motivated obedience**: "We are bound in gratitude and interest, as well as duty and faithfulness, to keep the words of the covenant." God's past mercies should compel present faithfulness.

**Universal covenant participation**: From "captains of tribes" to "hewers of wood and drawers of water," Henry emphasizes that covenant obligations and blessings transcend social status: "As none are too great to come under the bonds of the covenant, so none are too mean to inherit the blessings."

**The danger of presumptuous sin**: Henry gives particular attention to those who "bless himself in his heart, saying, I shall have peace, though I walk in the imagination of mine heart" (v.19), warning against false security in covenant membership without covenant obedience.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 29** ("The secret things belong unto the LORD"), Henry concludes that this "distinction between things secret and things revealed" teaches us to focus on revealed duties rather than hidden mysteries—"those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law."

Regarding the **wilderness provisions** (verses 5-6), Henry notes that "those very shoes which by the appointment of God they put on in Egypt, at the passover...never wore out, but served them to Canaan," highlighting God's detailed care.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the inclusion of children in covenant (v.11) to **Luke 19:9** and Christ's blessing of children, arguing "Little children...must be brought to Christ, and shall be blessed by him, for of such was and is the kingdom of God."

The universality of covenant participation references **Colossians 3:11** and **1 Corinthians 7:21**, showing continuity between Old Testament inclusion and New Testament equality in Christ.

The commentary also references **Exodus 24:4** regarding covenant ceremony and **Exodus 12:11** concerning the Passover preparation, demonstrating historical continuity in God's dealings with Israel.

Henry's exposition ultimately presents Deuteronomy 29 as both historical covenant renewal and timeless principles for faithful relationship with God.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Deuteronomy 30: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Deuteronomy 30 as God's ultimate triumph of **mercy over judgment**, emphasizing that despite the severe threatenings in the previous chapter, God preserves hope for restoration. He interprets the passage on two levels: as a **conditional promise** applicable to all people regarding repentance and forgiveness, and as a **prophetic prediction** of Israel's future restoration from exile.

The commentary highlights the **covenant of grace** versus the covenant of innocency, noting that grace "leaves room for repentance in case of misdemeanour, and promises pardon upon repentance." Henry emphasizes God's work in **heart circumcision** (v. 6) as divine grace that enables genuine love for God, stating: "It is the work of God's grace to circumcise the heart, and to shed abroad the love of God there."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On Repentance**: True repentance begins with "serious consideration" - calling to mind both "the blessing and the curse." Like the prodigal son, sinners must first "come to themselves" before returning to the Father.

**On Obedience**: Genuine conversion requires wholehearted commitment - "with all thy heart and with all thy soul" - and must be universal, respecting "all God's commandments" since breaking one involves "contemning them all."

**On God's Accessibility**: The commandments are not "hidden" or impossibly distant but are "very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart." This emphasizes that God's requirements are comprehensible and achievable through His grace.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry emphasizes that consideration precedes conversion: "Consideration is the first step towards conversion" (citing Isaiah 46:8).

**Verse 6**: The heart circumcision promise reveals that "whatsoever is required in the covenant is promised" - God provides the grace He demands.

**Verses 11-14**: Henry sees this as demonstrating the "plainness and easiness of the command," contrasting Israel's accessible revelation with philosophers who "travelled through many and distant regions in pursuit of learning."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively cross-references to support his interpretations:

- **Luke 15:17** (the prodigal's consideration)
- **Romans 10:6-8** (Paul's application of verses 12-14 to the gospel)
- **Nehemiah 1:9** (pleading this promise for dispersed Israel)
- **Jeremiah 31:18, 20** (God's compassion on penitents)
- **Isaiah 49:6** and **John 11:51-52** (Messianic gathering of scattered people)

He observes how Moses calls God "the Lord thy God" twelve times in ten verses, emphasizing the covenant relationship that gives both direction and encouragement to penitents.

The commentary concludes by noting the fulfillment in Israel's return from Babylonian captivity and potential future application to Jewish conversion to Christianity, though Henry adds soberly: "But, alas! who shall live when God doth this?"

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 32 - Summary

**God's Perfect Character vs. Human Corruption**: Henry emphasizes the central theological contrast between God's righteousness and Israel's rebellion. **Verses 1-2**: Heaven and earth serve as witnesses because "they revolt not from the obedience to their Creator" unlike "this perverse and unthinking people."

**Verses 3-4**: Henry explains God's greatness through His "faithfulness of his word, the perfection of his works, and the wisdom and equity of all the administrations of his government" rather than abstract attributes.

**Verses 5-6**: The phrase "their spot is not the spot of his children" distinguishes between believers' struggles with remaining sin versus willful, persistent rebellion.

**Conclusion**: Henry presents this song as both warning and instruction, demonstrating how God's perfect character makes human rebellion inexcusable while His faithfulness provides hope for those who return to Him as their Rock.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Deuteronomy 33 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Moses' final blessing as a powerful conclusion to his ministry, emphasizing **divine grace amid judgment**. After the stern warnings of Chapter 32, Moses "subjoins a blessing" to show he parts "not in anger," parallel to Christ's final act of blessing his disciples (Luke 24:50).

1. **Value of intercessory prayer**: "It is a very desirable thing to have an interest in the prayers of those that have an interest in heaven"

2. **Proper attitude toward Scripture**: Believers should sit "at thy feet, as scholars at the feet of their master, in token of reverence" - emphasizing humble submission to God's Word

3. **Balance of prayer and action**: "The voice of prayer should always be attended with the hand of endeavour, and then we may expect prosperity"

4. **Gratitude for spiritual inheritance**: The law as "the inheritance of the congregation of Jacob" teaches us to view God's Word as "their wealth and true treasure"

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-5**: Henry emphasizes the glorious theophany at Sinai, noting how even distant mountains Seir and Paran "were illuminated by the divine glory." He interprets "fiery law" as representing both judgment and purification.

**Verse 6 (Reuben)**: Despite Reuben's disgrace, Moses begins with him, showing "we should not insult over those that are disgraced, nor desire to perpetuate marks of infamy."

**Verse 7 (Judah)**: Henry sees this as pointing beyond the tribe to David and ultimately Christ, referencing Jacob's Shiloh prophecy (Genesis 49:10).

Notably, Henry explains Simeon's omission as due to remaining "under a brand" from Jacob's curse, having "never done any thing, as Levi had done, to retrieve its honour."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes:
- Luke 24:50 (Christ's final blessing)
- Habakkuk 3:3-4, Psalm 18:7-9 (divine appearances)
- Acts 7:53, Hebrews 2:2 (angels giving law)
- John 10:28 (security of saints)
- Revelation 1:16 (stars in Christ's hand)
- Genesis 49 (Jacob's tribal blessings)
- Psalm 119:111 (Word as inheritance)

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic method of weaving Old Testament passages into the broader tapestry of redemptive history, consistently pointing toward Christ while drawing practical applications for Christian living.

Chapter 34
----------------------------------------
Here's a comprehensive summary of Matthew Henry's commentary on Deuteronomy 34:

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Sovereignty and Promise Fulfillment**: Henry emphasizes that Moses' death occurs "according to the word of the Lord," demonstrating God's sovereign control even over his most faithful servants. The fulfillment of both God's threat (Moses wouldn't enter Canaan) and promise (he would see it) reveals divine faithfulness in judgment and mercy.

**Death as Ascension**: Henry interprets Moses climbing Mount Pisgah as symbolic, showing Moses "was willing to die" and viewed "death as his ascension." He notes that "the soul of a man, of a good man, when it leaves the body, goes upwards," presenting death not as defeat but as spiritual elevation for the faithful.

**Typological Significance**: The commentary presents Canaan as "Immanuel's land" and "a type of heaven." Moses' distant view of the Promised Land parallels how Old Testament saints "saw Christ's day afar off" and how believers now glimpse "the bliss and glory of their future state."

## Key Practical Applications

**Willing Submission to God's Will**: Henry draws parallels between Moses' acceptance of death and Christ's submission in Gethsemane, noting Moses "cheerfully submits" despite personal desires. This models how believers should respond to divine calling, even when it means leaving earthly blessings behind.

**Faithful Service Until Death**: Moses is honored as "the servant of the Lord," with Henry observing that "God's servants must die that they may rest from their labours, receive their recompense, and make room for others." This encourages lifelong dedication regardless of personal cost.

**Contentment with God's Timing**: Moses dies before enjoying Canaan's pleasures, teaching that "sometimes God takes his people away from the good to come." Henry encourages believers to find comfort knowing "those that shall come after us...will enter that promised land."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-4** (The Vision): Henry notes Moses went up "alone without help" despite his advanced age, demonstrating that "his natural force was not abated." The miraculous scope of Moses' vision required divine assistance: "The Lord showed it to him...it is he that gives the spirit of wisdom as well as the spirit of revelation."

**Verse 5** (Moses' Death): The phrase "died...according to the word of the Lord" is rendered "at the mouth of the Lord," leading Henry to quote Jewish tradition about Moses dying "with a kiss from the mouth of God"—suggesting "a delightful death" with "no bands in his death."

**Verse 6** (The Burial): God's personal burial of Moses through angels made this "funeral, though very private, yet very magnificent." The hidden location prevented idolatry: "lest the children of Israel...should have enshrined and worshipped the dead body of Moses."

**Verse 7** (Moses' Vitality): Henry divides Moses' 120 years into "three forties": courtier, shepherd, and leader, illustrating life's changeability while emphasizing that Moses retained full vigor until death.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes:
- **Hebrews 11:13-16**: Linking Moses' distant view to Abraham seeing Christ's day and the patriarchs seeking a heavenly country
- **John 16:32**: Comparing Moses' solitary death to Christ's assurance of the Father's presence
- **Romans 7:4**: Connecting Moses' burial to believers being "dead to the law"
- **Jude 9**: Referencing the mysterious contest between Michael and Satan over Moses' body
- **Luke 9 (Transfiguration)**: Suggesting Moses was "raised to meet Elias, to grace the solemnity of Christ's transfiguration"

Henry's commentary presents Moses' death not as tragic loss but as victorious transition, emphasizing themes of faithful service, divine sovereignty, and the eternal hope that sustains believers through earthly limitations and mortality.
