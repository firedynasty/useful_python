Chapter 0

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings: Introduction Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents 1 Kings as fundamentally **"the history of the kingdom of God among men"** rather than merely a chronicle of earthly monarchs. He emphasizes that while many histories focus on crowned heads, Scripture uniquely reveals God's eternal kingdom where **"the King is one and his name one."** 

A central theological theme is the **contrast between divine honor and worldly honor**. Henry notes that while the kings of Edom preceded Israel chronologically, "the history of the kings of Israel lives, and will live, in holy Writ, to the end of the world, whereas that of the kings of Edom is long since buried in oblivion; for the honour that comes from God is durable, while the honour of the world is like a mushroom, which comes up in a night and perishes in a night."

Henry also highlights the **Messianic expectation** woven throughout the narrative, noting that these books maintain "special regard to the house and lineage of David, from which Christ came" and contain "great expectations" of the Messiah.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws two primary practical applications from the Kings narrative:

1. **Political Wisdom**: "From these books we may collect rules of politics, for the directing of public affairs," just as Genesis provides rules for family governance.

2. **Divine Accountability**: The overarching lesson that **"kings, though gods to us, are men to God, mortal and accountable."** This fundamental truth applies to all human authority and leadership.

## Notable Insights and Cross-References

Henry provides memorable characterizations of Judah's kings, from **"David the devout, Solomon the wise"** through **"Josiah the tender-hearted"** to the final wicked rulers who "brought ruin quickly on themselves and their kingdom."

He references **Genesis 36:31** regarding Edom's kings and **Luke 10:24** about prophets and kings desiring to see gospel mysteries. Henry notes that while earlier biblical narratives featured more immediate divine encounters, the Kings period shows God working through "common providence" in ways more relatable to contemporary believers.

## Structure and Historical Context

Henry outlines the book's progression from David's death through Solomon's glorious reign and temple construction, the kingdom's division under Rehoboam, and the subsequent reigns culminating in Elijah's ministry and Ahab's downfall. He observes that while good and bad kings were nearly equal in number, "the reigns of the good were generally long and those of the bad short," suggesting God's providence in preserving His people despite political upheaval.

Chapter 1
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings Chapter 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents David's decline as a divine lesson on human frailty and God's sovereignty in succession. He emphasizes that **"our deliverances from diseases and dangers are but reprieves"** - even the mighty eventually succumb to mortality. The chapter illustrates God's faithfulness in preserving His covenant promises despite human weakness and rebellion.

The contrast between Adonijah's self-exaltation and Solomon's divine appointment reflects the broader biblical theme of human ambition versus God's sovereign choice. Henry notes how **"the kingdom of Christ is opposed, and there are those that say, 'We will not have him to reign over us,'"** drawing parallels between Adonijah's rebellion and resistance to Christ's kingship.

## Key Practical Applications

**Parental Discipline:** Henry strongly condemns David's failure to discipline Adonijah, stating that David **"had not displeased him at any time"** by correcting his behavior. This indulgence produced pride and rebellion. Henry warns that **"those who honour their sons more than God...forfeit the honour they might expect from their sons."**

**Strength and Youth:** David's physical decline teaches that **"the strong man [should not] glory in his strength"** and young people should **"remember their Creator in the days of their youth, before these evil days come."**

**Faithful Service:** Henry praises those like Nathan, Zadok, and Benaiah whose proven loyalty meant **"Adonijah had not the confidence so much as to propose his project to them."** Consistent faithfulness builds unshakeable integrity.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-4 (David's Decline):** Henry sees David's coldness as both natural aging and divine chastisement for past sins, particularly **"that in the matter of Uriah."** He criticizes the physicians' prescription of marriage as inappropriate preparation for one nearing death.

**Verses 5-10 (Adonijah's Rebellion):** Henry emphasizes the connection between parental indulgence and children's pride: **"Children that are indulged learn to be proud and ambitious."** He notes Adonijah's base ingratitude in seizing power while his father lived.

**Verses 11-31 (Nathan and Bathsheba's Intervention):** Henry commends their **"holy policy"** in reminding David of his oath and securing Solomon's immediate coronation to thwart usurpation.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's weakness to **Job 37:17** and **Haggai 1:6** regarding the futility of external warmth without God's blessing. He references **Matthew 19:5** and **1 Corinthians 7:1** regarding marriage principles, and draws parallels between Adonijah's feast and **Absalom's deception (2 Samuel 15:7)**. He also cites **Romans 16:18** about those who **"serve their own belly"** being easily deceived by seducers.

The commentary consistently weaves together themes of divine sovereignty, human responsibility, and the importance of godly discipline in producing faithful character.

Chapter 2
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Kings Chapter 2

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as a transition between two eras, with "David setting and Solomon at the same time rising." The central theological theme is **divine covenant faithfulness**, particularly God's conditional promise to David. Henry emphasizes that "We never let fall the promise till we let fall the precept"—highlighting how God's covenant blessings depend on human obedience.

Another key theme is **divine justice versus mercy**. Henry interprets Solomon's early acts not as harsh vengeance, but as necessary justice to establish righteous rule. He notes that "time does not wear out the guilt of any sin, particularly that of murder," demonstrating that justice delayed is not justice denied.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from David's deathbed charge. **Parental responsibility** is paramount—dying fathers must pass on spiritual wisdom, with Henry noting that "the authority of a dying father is much, but nothing to that of a living God." 

**Gratitude and loyalty** receive emphasis through David's instructions about Barzillai: "the kindnesses we have received from our friends must not be buried either in their graves or ours, but our children must return them to theirs." This leads to the principle that "Thy own friend, and thy father's friend, forsake not."

**Spiritual leadership** requires both strength and wisdom. Henry interprets David's charge to "be strong and show thyself a man" as meaning "Those that would keep the charge of the Lord their God must put on resolution."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On David's mortality (v. 2), Henry offers the memorable insight: "Death is a way; not only a period of this life, but a passage to a better. It is the way of all the earth...but they walk with pleasure in this way, through the valley of the shadow of death."

Regarding the conditional covenant (v. 4), Henry explains that while God's promise of the Messiah through David was absolute, "the promise that there should not fail him a man on the throne of Israel was conditional."

Concerning Adonijah's request for Abishag (v. 17), Henry sees this as revealing continued treasonous ambition, with Solomon rightfully recognizing the political implications.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's peaceful death to **Acts 13:36** and **Acts 2:29**, noting that David "served his own generation, by the will of God, fell asleep...and saw corruption." He references **Psalm 23:4** regarding walking through death's valley, and **Psalm 16:9** for David's hope in resurrection.

The principle of honoring parents' friends connects to **Proverbs 27:10** and Paul's prayer for Onesiphorus's household. Henry also references **Isaiah 65:20** regarding aged sinners facing judgment, emphasizing that even "a hoary head, if a guilty and forfeited head, ought not to be any man's protection from justice."

Chapter 3
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings Chapter 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Solomon's reign transitioning from the "bloody" acts of justice in chapter 2 to revealing God's mercy and wisdom. The central theological theme is that true greatness comes from loving God and seeking wisdom for service rather than personal gain. Henry emphasizes that **"very great he looks here, both at the altar and on the bench, and therefore on the bench because at the altar"** - Solomon's judicial wisdom flows from his worship and relationship with God.

The commentary highlights God's gracious condescension in offering Solomon whatever he desired, paralleling this with God's ongoing invitation to believers: **"God, in like manner, condescends to us, and puts us in the ready way to be happy by assuring us that we shall have what we will for the asking."**

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

1. **Humble Self-Assessment**: Solomon's confession "I am but a little child" demonstrates that **"those who are employed in public stations ought to be very sensible of the weight and importance of their work and their own insufficiency for it."**

2. **Proper Priorities in Prayer**: Solomon's choice of wisdom over personal gain shows that **"the best knowledge is which will be serviceable to us in doing our duty."**

3. **Generous Service to God**: Solomon's thousand burnt offerings illustrate that **"those that truly love God and his worship will not grudge the expenses of their religion."**

4. **Gratitude for Inherited Blessings**: **"Children should give God thanks for his mercies to their parents"** as Solomon honored David's legacy.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry identifies both commendable and questionable aspects of Solomon's conduct. Unquestionably good: Solomon "loved the Lord" (v.3), walked in David's statutes, and offered generous sacrifices. Questionable: his marriage to Pharaoh's daughter (though Henry suggests she may have been a sincere convert) and his worship at high places, which Henry calls an "irregularity" but notes God's gracious acceptance.

Regarding Solomon's dream at Gibeon, Henry observes that **"the nearer we come to the rule in our worship the more reason we have to expect the tokens of God's presence."** God's nighttime appearance suggests that **"silence and retirement befriend our communion with God."**

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout: David's love for Solomon (2 Sam. 12:24), Paul's similar humility (2 Cor. 2:16), God's promise to meet where His name is recorded (Ex. 20:24), Christ's invitation to ask (John 16:23), and the pattern of divine dreams to prophets (Num. 12:6). He suggests the marriage may have inspired Canticles and Psalm 45 as types of Christ's union with the Gentile church.

The commentary emphasizes that Solomon's wise choice, made even in sleep, demonstrates divine grace working to produce **"gracious desires"** that prioritize service over self-interest.

Chapter 4
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Kings 4

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Solomon's reign as the pinnacle of Israel's glory and **a powerful type of Christ's kingdom**. He emphasizes that "never was that kingdom ever so glorious a type of the kingdom of the Messiah as it was then," connecting Solomon's reign to messianic prophecies in Psalm 72. However, Henry maintains proper perspective, noting that "Thus great was Solomon, but our Lord Jesus was greater than he, though he took upon him the form of a servant; for divinity, in its lowest humiliation, infinitely transcends royalty in its highest elevation."

The chapter demonstrates **God's faithfulness in fulfilling promises**—both the immediate promise of wisdom and wealth to Solomon, and the ancient promises to Abraham regarding numerous descendants and territorial dominion.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for leadership and governance:

**Wise Personnel Choices**: Solomon's retention of his father's trusted officials shows that "when sons come to inherit their father's wealth, honour, and power, it is a piece of respect to their memory...to employ those whom they employed." Good leaders don't change everything merely to appear innovative.

**Good Stewardship**: The elaborate provision system teaches "good house-keeping, to be generous in spending according to their ability, but prudent in providing." Henry commends the organized distribution that benefited all regions equally and prevented any single official from becoming "exorbitantly rich."

**Property Rights**: Solomon's protection of private property—where people "dwelt every man under his vine and fig-tree"—demonstrates that good governance protects rather than confiscates citizens' possessions.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6**: Henry notes Solomon's wisdom in appointing "priests' sons" to key positions, showing "grateful respect" for their faithful fathers like Zadok and Nathan the prophet.

**Verses 7-19**: The twelve-district provision system demonstrated shrewd administration that "equally benefited" all parts of the kingdom and kept officials from becoming too powerful or wealthy.

**Verses 20-25**: Henry sees the fulfillment of God's promises in Israel's population growth—"as the sand of the sea in multitude"—and their security and prosperity. The phrase about dwelling "under his vine and under his fig tree" represents both physical safety and property protection.

**Verses 22-28**: The massive daily provisions (enough for 3,000-4,800 people) show the constant hospitality extended to those seeking Solomon's wisdom.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter extensively to other biblical passages:

- **Psalm 72**: The primary messianic psalm "for Solomon, but with reference to Christ"
- **Genesis 15:18; 22:17**: Fulfillment of promises to Abraham about territorial extent and numerous descendants  
- **Matthew 12:42**: Christ as greater than Solomon
- **Isaiah 49:6-7; 53:12**: Messianic prophecies about universal dominion
- **Revelation 7:9**: The multitude of God's spiritual Israel
- **Ecclesiastes 2:24; 9:7**: Solomon's own later reflections on enjoying God's gifts

## Summary Assessment

Henry presents this chapter as showcasing both **temporal blessing and prophetic typology**. Solomon's kingdom represents the high-water mark of Israel's earthly glory while pointing forward to Christ's eternal kingdom. The emphasis throughout is on **wise governance, generous hospitality, and divine faithfulness**—all ultimately fulfilled supremely in Jesus Christ.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Solomon's temple-building as deeply typological, emphasizing that "in this, especially, he was to be a type of Christ, for 'he shall build the temple of the Lord'" (Zech. 6:12). The commentary highlights God's sovereignty in timing—David's wars prevented temple construction, but Solomon's peace enabled it. Henry notes that "Satan does all he can to hinder temple work," but when opposition is bound, believers "should be busy."

The partnership between Solomon and Hiram illustrates God's providence in making nations interdependent, showing how "one country has need of another and is benefited by another, that there may be mutual correspondence and dependence, to the glory of God our common parent."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons: First, believers should seize peaceful seasons for spiritual building—"Days of peace and prosperity present us with a fair gale, which we must account for if we improve not." Second, he warns against pride: "Let us never look with disdain on those below us, because we know not how soon we may need them." 

Regarding business dealings, Henry emphasizes deliberation and fairness—Hiram "considered the things" before agreeing, and both parties honored their agreements with "justice on both sides, in every bargain."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verse 1, Henry observes that "David's character will win the affections even of those that are without," noting Hiram was likely "a worshipper of the true God." Regarding verse 4's mention of peace, Henry explains this as having "no Satan...no instrument of Satan to oppose it."

For verses 6-9, Henry praises the complementary skills—Israel excelled in divine matters while Sidonians mastered craftsmanship: "True piety is a much more valuable gift of heaven than the highest degree of ingenuity. Better be an Israelite skilful in the law than a Sidonian skilful to hew timber."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to Isaiah 60, seeing prophetic fulfillment where "sons of strangers shall build up the wall of the gospel temple" and "the glory of Lebanon shall be brought to it." He references 1 Thessalonians 2:18 and Zechariah 3:1 regarding Satan hindering temple work, and Acts 9:31 about churches having rest to be edified. The mutual dependence theme connects to Ezekiel 27:17, noting how "Israel will supply Tyre with corn" while receiving craftsmen.

Henry presents this chapter as both historical account and prophetic type, emphasizing God's timing, the value of peaceful cooperation, and the importance of integrity in all dealings.

Chapter 6
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Kings 6

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the temple as **"the house of the Lord"** with profound spiritual significance. He presents two key aspects: the temple was "directed and modelled" by God himself through "Infinite Wisdom as the architect," and it was "dedicated and devoted to him and to his honour." Henry notes this gave it "its beauty of holiness, that it was the house of the Lord, which far transcended all its other beauties."

The commentary stresses God's **conditional blessing** through the divine message to Solomon (vv. 11-13). Henry warns that "all this charge which he and his people were at, in erecting this temple, would neither excuse them from obedience to the law of God nor shelter them from his judgments in case of disobedience. **Keeping God's commandments is better, and more pleasing to him, than building churches.**"

Henry also highlights the temple as **"a shadow of good things to come"** (referencing Hebrews 9:9), connecting Old Testament worship to New Testament fulfillment.

## Key Practical Applications

**Preparation for God's Service**: Henry draws from Solomon's three-year preparation period that "It is not time lost which is spent in composing ourselves for the work of God, and disentangling ourselves from every thing which might distract or divert us. We are truly serving God when we are preparing for his service."

**Quiet Reverence**: The silent construction (v. 7) teaches that "Quietness and silence both become and befriend religious exercises: God's work should be done with as much care and as little noise as may be. **Clamour and violence often hinder the work of God, but never further it.**"

**Balanced Priorities**: Henry emphasizes that external religious works cannot substitute for obedience, noting that magnificent buildings mean nothing without faithful living.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: The 480-year timeline demonstrates God's patience - "he was in no haste for a temple" and shows "how little he values external pomp and splendour in his service."

**Verse 4**: The windows "broad within, and narrow without" symbolically teach that "Such should the eyes of our mind be, reflecting nearer on ourselves than on other people." Henry also notes "The narrowness of the lights intimated the darkness of that dispensation, in comparison with the gospel day."

**Verses 11-13**: God's conditional promise serves both to encourage Solomon in his work and remind him that the temple's glory depends on continued obedience.

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry connects the temple to **multiple scriptural contexts**: Moses' tabernacle pattern, David's tent as "the house of the Lord" (2 Sam. 12:20), the "worldly sanctuary" concept from Hebrews 9:1,10, Isaiah 54:1-2 regarding Israel's growth, Proverbs 24:27 on proper preparation, and Psalm 74:4,6 contrasting the temple's peaceful construction with its violent destruction.

The commentary consistently ties Old Testament temple worship to New Testament spiritual realities, particularly through Hebrews' typological framework, showing how the physical temple pointed toward greater spiritual truths fulfilled in Christ.

Chapter 7
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings Chapter 7 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry emphasizes the principle of **divine priority** throughout this chapter. He notes that Solomon "built for God first, and then all his other buildings were comfortable," establishing that "the surest foundations of lasting prosperity are those which are laid in an early piety" (referencing Matthew 6:33). This reflects the theological truth that God's honor should precede personal comfort.

Henry also highlights God's **sovereignty in using diverse gifts**. Regarding Hiram the craftsman (half-Israelite, half-Tyrian), he observes: "God will serve himself by the common gifts of the children of men." This demonstrates how God incorporates various talents and backgrounds into His divine purposes, as "the temple [was built] with the wit of Tyre."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Solomon's building projects:

**Proper Priorities**: Solomon was "less eager and intent" in building his palace than the temple, being "impatient till the temple was finished." Henry applies this: "Thus we ought to prefer God's honour before our own ease and satisfaction."

**Excellence in Service**: Emphasizing that "God, who is the best, must be served and honoured with the best," Henry advocates using the finest materials and efforts in serving God, as seen in the "bright brass" and costly stones used throughout.

**Balanced Living**: Henry notes that even great princes found their "most pleasant days" in "country retirements," suggesting the value of peaceful withdrawal from public duties.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-12** (Solomon's Buildings): Henry contrasts the thirteen years spent on Solomon's house versus seven years for the temple, attributing this not to less exactness but to proper spiritual priorities.

**Verses 13-22** (The Pillars): The craftsman Hiram represents the ideal blend of skill and devotion - having "the ingenuity of a Tyrian, and the affection of an Israelite."

**Verses 46-47** (Brass Casting): The uncounted vessels demonstrate both God's abundant provision and the integrity of the workers, showing "so honest were the workmen" that detailed accounting was unnecessary.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Solomon's building wisdom to **Proverbs 9:1**, where "Wisdom's building her house, and hewing out her seven pillars" parallels Solomon's architectural endeavors. He references **Matthew 6:33** to support the principle of seeking God's kingdom first. Additionally, he links **1 Chronicles 29:2** regarding David's iron preparations and **2 Chronicles 4:11** for details about Hiram's work.

The commentary presents Solomon's magnificent building projects as both historical achievement and spiritual instruction about priorities, excellence in divine service, and God's use of diverse human talents for His purposes.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Kings 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes that **spiritual worship exceeds material construction** in glory, noting that "prayer and praise, the work of saints, exceed the casting of metal and the graving of stones, the work of the craftsman." The temple's true value lies not in its physical beauty but in God's presence dwelling there.

The **divine presence and covenant faithfulness** form central themes. Henry observes that without the ark, the temple was "like a body without a soul" or "a house without an inhabitant." God's appearance in the cloud demonstrates His acceptance of Solomon's work and His commitment to dwell among His people, though "in thick darkness" to accommodate human frailty.

Henry highlights **God's progressive revelation**, noting the darkness of the Old Testament dispensation "in comparison with the light of the gospel, by which, with open face, we behold, as in a glass, the glory of the Lord."

## Key Practical Applications

**Unity in worship** emerges as a major application. Henry praises how all Israel—elders, tribal heads, and leaders—assembled together, demonstrating that "public mercies call for public acknowledgments." This teaches the importance of corporate worship and shared spiritual celebration.

**Generosity in service** appears in the countless sacrifices offered, reflecting how "the people in Solomon's time were very rich, very easy, and very cheerful," leading them to "consecrate not only their cheerfulness, but a part of their wealth, to God."

Henry draws practical lessons about **leadership transition**, noting how "David, like a very good man, brings the ark to a convenient place" while "Solomon, like a very great man, brings it to a magnificent place," teaching that "children [should] proceed in God's service where their parents left off."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verses 10-11** (the cloud filling the temple), Henry explains God's manifestation served multiple purposes: honoring the ark, confirming acceptance of the temple, striking awe in the assembly, and encouraging future prayer. The dark cloud signifies both accommodation to human weakness and the mystery of divine presence.

Regarding **verse 9** (only the stone tablets in the ark), Henry notes this "treasure far exceeding all the dedicated things both of David and Solomon," emphasizing Scripture's supreme value.

On **verses 12-13** (Solomon's response to the cloud), Henry shows how Solomon "bids it welcome" and declares the temple God's eternal dwelling place, demonstrating proper response to divine manifestation.

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects the temple dedication to **broader biblical themes**. He references Leviticus 16:2 regarding God appearing in clouds, and 1 Timothy 6:16 about God dwelling in light yet accommodating human limitations through darkness.

He draws **typological connections**, calling these "Israel's golden days, days of the Son of man in type," pointing toward Christ's greater temple ministry.

The commentary links to **future revelation**, noting how "all the church's holy things on earth...will be swallowed up in the perfection of holiness above," connecting earthly worship to heavenly reality.

Henry emphasizes **covenant continuity** by tracing God's promises from Moses through David to Solomon, demonstrating divine faithfulness across generations and preparing readers to see God's ultimate fulfillment in Christ's ministry.

----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Kings 9

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's **conditional covenant** with Solomon as the central theme. God's presence and blessings depend entirely on obedience. The temple's holiness comes from God's consecration, not human dedication: "Solomon had dedicated it, but it was God's prerogative to hallow it." 

The commentary stresses that **divine judgment is inevitable** for covenant-breaking. Henry warns against false security, noting that the temple "was designed to protect them in their allegiance to God, but not in their rebellion or disobedience." Even the magnificent temple cannot shield Israel from consequences of apostasy.

Henry also highlights God's **providence in human diversity**, observing how different people suit different callings - some preferring agriculture, others navigation - reflecting "the wisdom of him whose all souls are and all lands."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

- **No presumption on past blessings**: "Let them not be secure now, as if they might live as they please now that they have the temple of the Lord among them."

- **Inherited faith requires personal commitment**: "Let not the children of godly parents expect the entail of the blessing, unless they tread in the steps of those that have gone before them to heaven."

- **Sincerity is essential**: True religion requires "integrity of heart and uprightness" - "no religion without sincerity."

- **Generosity befits greatness**: Solomon's gifts to Hiram demonstrate that "it becomes those that are great and good to be generous."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 3** (God's presence in the temple): Henry applies this personally to "living temples" - those sanctified by God have "his eye, his heart, his love and care, and this perpetually."

Regarding **verses 6-9** (consequences of apostasy): Henry notes the irony that the temple's very height "will make its fall the more amazing," and that Israel's punishment will clearly reveal their sin: "Their sin will be read in their punishment."

On **verses 11-13** (Hiram's cities): Henry charitably interprets the dispute, suggesting the cities didn't suit Tyrian commercial culture rather than indicating meanness or fickleness from either party.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple scriptures:
- **2 Chronicles 7:1** (fire from heaven confirming acceptance)
- **Jeremiah 7:4** (warning against false temple confidence)  
- **Psalm 132:12** (conditional nature of Davidic covenant)
- **Deuteronomy 29:24-25** (nations asking why God judged Israel)

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic integration of Old and New Testament themes, applying temple imagery to Christian believers as living temples and emphasizing that God's covenantal principles remain consistent across Scripture.

Henry concludes with the reminder that God "deals plainly with us, sets before us good and evil, the blessing and the curse, and lets us know what we must trust to" - a sobering call to faithful obedience.

Chapter 10
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Kings 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Solomon's magnificence as a shadow of Christ's greater glory, emphasizing that "where Christ is, by his word and Spirit, 'Behold, a greater than Solomon is there.'" The commentary reveals divine providence in Solomon's prosperity, showing how God blessed him both for his own sake ("He delighted in thee") and for Israel's benefit ("because the LORD loved Israel for ever"). Henry portrays the Queen of Sheba's visit as a spiritual quest, noting she came not merely for political reasons but "concerning the name of the Lord," seeking instruction in divine matters.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers and leaders. He emphasizes that magistrates and ministers "must be more solicitous to do the duty of their places than to secure the honours and profits of them." For those in public service, he advocates continuous self-improvement, stating they "should, by all means possible, be still improving themselves in that knowledge which will more and more qualify them for it."

The commentary highlights the value of godly association: "It is a great advantage to be in good families, and to have opportunity of frequent converse with those that are wise, and good, and communicative." Henry notes that many "have this happiness who know not how to value it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry observes that the Queen of Sheba particularly admired Solomon's wisdom and piety, especially "the ascent by which he went up to the house of the Lord"—his devotional demeanor showing "as much humility then as majesty at other times." When she declared "the half was not told me" (v. 7), Henry connects this to spiritual experience: "Those who, through grace, are brought to experience the delights of communion with God will say that the one-half was not told them of the pleasures of Wisdom's ways."

Her blessing of Solomon's servants (v. 8) prompts Henry's reflection that "With much more reason may we say this of Christ's servants, Blessed are those that dwell in his house."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout. He references Matthew 12:42 regarding the Queen of the South, connects David's prophecy about "gold of Sheba" (Psalm 72:15) to her gifts, and links the wise men's gifts to Christ (Matthew 2:11) as fulfillment of this type. He cites Acts 8:27 regarding Candace, 1 Corinthians 2:9 about heavenly glories exceeding earthly reports, and Jeremiah 22:15 connecting justice with prosperity.

The commentary consistently points readers to Christ as the greater Solomon, making this Old Testament account a christological meditation on divine wisdom and glory that finds ultimate fulfillment in the Gospel.

----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Kings Chapter 11

## Main Theological Themes

Henry opens with striking gravity, noting this chapter begins with "as melancholy a 'but' as almost any we find in all the Bible." The commentary explores the profound fall of Solomon, whose "sun sets under a cloud." Key theological themes include:

**Divine Sovereignty in Judgment**: God's anger against Solomon demonstrates that even the most blessed can fall under divine displeasure when they abandon their covenant obligations. Henry emphasizes that "there is nothing perfect under the sun, but all is so above the sun."

**The Progressive Nature of Sin**: Henry warns that "the way of sin is down-hill; those that have got into it cannot easily stop themselves." Solomon's apostasy began with marrying foreign wives and culminated in building high places for pagan gods.

**Covenant Faithfulness**: God's judgment reflects Solomon's violation of explicit divine commands, particularly regarding foreign marriages that would "turn away your heart after their gods."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering applications:

**Guard Against Presumption**: "Let him that thinks he stands take heed lest he fall." Even the wisest and most blessed must maintain constant dependence on God's grace.

**Beware Prosperity's Dangers**: "Solomon, like Jeshurun, waxed fat and then kicked." Henry notes that abundant blessings can become spiritual snares, making "the food convenient" safer than "the food abundant."

**The Influence of Bad Examples**: "One bad act of a good man may be of more pernicious consequence to others than twenty of a wicked man," particularly affecting one's children and those who look up to them.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Solomon's 700 wives and 300 concubines, Henry observes pointedly: "1000 in all, and not one good one among them," noting that "no woman of established virtue would be one of such a set."

Regarding **verse 14** (Micaiah's oath of faithfulness): Henry praises the prophet's resolution as "nobly resolved, and as became one who had his eye to a greater King than either of these, arrayed with brighter robes, and sitting on a higher throne."

On the **false prophets' unanimity** (verses 6, 12): Henry warns that their agreement demonstrated how "they stole the word from the true prophets" and "spoke their language" to deceive both kings.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this narrative to broader scriptural themes, referencing **Jeremiah 23:30** regarding false prophets who "steal the word" from true prophets. He links Jehoshaphat's alliance to his father's earlier mistake of "joining with Syria against Israel" (1 Kings 15:19-20).

The commentary draws parallels to **Galatians 2:6** when describing how the false prophets "added nothing" to Jehoshaphat's spiritual discernment, and connects the theme of hating truth-tellers to the broader biblical pattern of persecution of God's messengers.

Henry concludes by noting that "Equity is not to be judged of by prosperity," emphasizing that righteousness doesn't guarantee earthly success—a theme consistent throughout Scripture's wisdom literature.
