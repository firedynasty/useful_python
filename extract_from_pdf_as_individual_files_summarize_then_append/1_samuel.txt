# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel: Introduction Summary

## Book Structure and Authorship

Matthew Henry explains that First Samuel bears Samuel's name not because he authored the entire book, but because it begins with his detailed biography and contains the history of those he anointed. Henry notes that Samuel likely wrote only the portions covering his own lifetime (through chapter 25, where his death is recorded), with later prophets—possibly Nathan—continuing the narrative through David's reign.

## Historical Context and Organization

Henry positions First Samuel as a transitional work that serves both "as an appendix to the book of Judges" and "an entrance upon the history of the kings." The book chronicles the final two judges (Eli and Samuel) who were notably "not, as the rest, men of war, but priests," and the first two kings (Saul and David).

## Three-Part Structure

Henry divides the book into three major sections:
- **Chapters 1-8**: Eli's downfall and Samuel's rise to leadership under divine governance
- **Chapters 9-15**: Samuel's transition from leadership and Saul's advancement followed by his "mal-administration"  
- **Chapters 16-31**: David's selection, his conflicts with Saul, Saul's ultimate destruction, and the preparation for David's ascension to the throne

## Theological Themes

The commentary emphasizes the book's focus on divine sovereignty in leadership transitions. Henry highlights the contrast between human and divine governance, showing how God orchestrates the rise and fall of leaders according to His purposes. The narrative demonstrates God's faithfulness in raising up faithful servants (Samuel, David) while bringing judgment upon those who fail in their calling (Eli, Saul).

## Practical Applications

Henry concludes with the essential practical note that "these things are written for our learning," emphasizing that the historical accounts serve as instruction for contemporary believers. The book provides lessons about faithful service, the consequences of disobedience, and God's sovereign care for His people through changing leadership.

## Scripture Connections

Henry notes the book's significance extends beyond its immediate narrative, as it is "sometimes referred to in the New Testament" and frequently referenced "in the titles of David's Psalms," making it essential for understanding both Old Testament history and New Testament references.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel Chapter 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes Samuel as a "child of prayer" in contrast to Samson as a "child of promise," highlighting how "wonders are produced by the word and prayer." He introduces God's title "Lord of hosts" (Jehovah Sabaoth) as particularly significant for Israel's comfort when their armies were weak, reminding them that God commands "all the hosts both of heaven and earth."

The commentary stresses God's sovereignty in bringing forth great leaders, noting that while some "drop out of the clouds...in their full growth and lustre," others like Samuel are "accounted for from the birth" according to God's declaration: "Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee."

## Key Practical Applications

**Family Devotion Despite Division**: Henry emphasizes that Elkanah maintained worship "notwithstanding this unhappy difference in his family," teaching that "if the devotions of a family prevail not to put an end to its divisions, yet let not the divisions put a stop to the devotions."

**Love in Affliction**: Elkanah's continued love for barren Hannah demonstrates that we shouldn't "abate our just love to any relation for the sake of any infirmity which they cannot help." Henry notes this mirrors Christ's love for his church despite her infirmities.

**Bearing Others' Burdens**: Condemning Peninnah's cruelty, Henry warns against delighting "in grieving those that are melancholy and of a sorrowful spirit," emphasizing "we ought to bear one another's burdens, not add to them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Samuel's Levitical heritage from the "most honourable house" of Kohathites, with ancestral connections to both Bethlehem-Judah and Mount Ephraim, showing potential "alliance to David."

**Verse 3**: Henry notes Elkanah's faithful worship despite corrupt priests Hophni and Phinehas, demonstrating that "if the priests did not do their duty, he would do his."

**Verse 5**: The "worthy portion" given to Hannah represented the "choicest piece...given to those that were most valued," showing deliberate public affection to counter her affliction.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Samuel's pre-birth significance to other biblical figures including Jeremiah (Jer. 1:5), John the Baptist, and Jesus. He references the original marriage institution (Matt. 19:5,8), parallels Hannah to Rachel and Peninnah to Leah, and cites Ephesians 4:11-12 regarding gospel ministry. He also draws from Job 1:6 about Satan's presence "when the sons of God come to present themselves before the Lord," and Malachi 2:13 regarding how marital discord affects worship.

The commentary presents this opening chapter as establishing themes of divine sovereignty, faithful devotion amid imperfection, and the power of persistent prayer that will characterize Samuel's prophetic ministry.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents several key theological themes through Hannah's song and the chapter's narrative. **God's sovereignty** dominates the commentary - Henry emphasizes that "the Lord maketh some poor and maketh others rich" and controls all life circumstances. **Divine holiness** receives special attention, with Henry noting that "There is none holy as the Lord" reflects God's "unspotted purity" and "infinite agreement with himself."

The **contrast between human weakness and divine strength** runs throughout, as Henry observes how God "raises up the poor out of the dust" while "the bows of the mighty men are broken." He also emphasizes **prophetic typology**, suggesting Hannah's salvation joy points beyond her personal circumstances to "that, above all, by Christ, which those are but the types of."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers. He stresses the importance of **gratitude in worship**: "What we win by prayer we may wear with comfort, and must wear with praise." He warns against **pride and self-reliance**, noting that "Let not the rich be proud and secure, for God can soon make them poor; let not the poor despond and despair."

The commentary emphasizes **contentment in all circumstances**: "Are we poor? God made us poor, which is a good reason why we should be content." Henry also highlights the need to **focus on the Giver rather than gifts**: "she overlooks the gift, and praises the giver; whereas most forget the giver and fasten only on the gift."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Hannah's opening (v.1), Henry notes her joy was "not so much in her son as in her God." For verse 3's warning against pride, he applies it both to personal enemies like Peninnah and national enemies like the Philistines. 

Regarding the reversals described in verses 4-8, Henry observes that God "has not only set the one over against the other, but the one very near the other," emphasizing how quickly circumstances can change. On verse 6 about God killing and making alive, he interprets this both as God's "sovereign dominion" over all life and death, and as describing spiritual resurrection.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes extensive scriptural connections throughout. He links Hannah's song to **Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46)**, noting "great affinity" between them. He references **Moses' song (Exodus 15:11)** regarding God's holiness, and connects the "rock" imagery to **Deuteronomy 32:31**.

Other significant references include **Psalm 18:31** on God's uniqueness, **Isaiah 6:3** and **Revelation 4:8** on divine holiness, and **Ecclesiastes 7:14** on providential reversals. Henry also cites **Job's experience** as an example of God making someone "poor and rich again," and references **Psalm 127:5** regarding children as divine blessing.

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic integration of systematic theology with practical piety, showing how Hannah's personal testimony reveals universal truths about God's character and governance of human affairs.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as marking a pivotal transition where "God in an extraordinary manner" revives prophecy in Israel through young Samuel. The central theological theme is **divine calling and faithful response**. Henry emphasizes that "the word of the Lord was precious in those days; there was no open vision," suggesting God had withdrawn prophetic revelation due to the nation's corruption under Eli's unfaithful sons.

The commentary highlights **God's sovereignty in choosing vessels for His service**, noting how God bypassed the established priest Eli to speak directly to a child. This represented both judgment on Eli's house and hope for Israel's spiritual renewal. Henry sees this as demonstrating that "those are fittest to rule who have learnt to obey."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**For young people**: Samuel's example shows that faithful service in small things prepares one for greater responsibilities. "Let those that are young be humble and diligent, which they will find the surest way to preferment."

**For spiritual readiness**: Henry emphasizes the proper posture for receiving God's word: "When we come to read the word of God, and to attend on the preaching of it, we should come thus disposed... Speak, Lord, for thy servant heareth."

**For mentorship**: Eli's gracious instruction to Samuel, despite being passed over himself, exemplifies how "the elder should, without grudging, do their utmost to assist and improve the younger that are rising up."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich detail on Samuel's repeated misunderstanding of God's call, noting this illustrates how "we make such mistakes oftener than we think of. God calls to us by his word, and we take it to be only the call of the minister... His voice cries, and it is but here and there a man of wisdom that understands it to be his voice."

On verse 7 ("Samuel did not yet know the Lord"), Henry observes that even those with great biblical knowledge must remember when "they were as babes, unskilful in the word of righteousness," encouraging patience with spiritual immaturity.

The final call where God "stood and called" suggests a visible manifestation, showing divine condescension in making Himself known to His servant.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to broader biblical themes through multiple references: Job 33:14 on God speaking though man perceives it not, Psalm 85:8 and Habakkuk 2:1 on listening for God's voice, Genesis 22:1 on God calling Abraham by name, and Revelation 1:12 regarding seeing the voice that speaks. These connections demonstrate the consistency of God's methods in calling His servants throughout Scripture history.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel Chapter 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as divine judgment fulfilling earlier prophecies against Eli's house. The central theme is **the danger of presuming upon God's favor without genuine repentance**. He emphasizes that "sin, the accursed thing, was in the camp" which gave Israel's enemies decisive advantage.

Henry critiques Israel's **idolatrous relationship with religious symbols**, noting they "made an idol of the ark" while ignoring "the God of the ark." He observes that "carnal people triumph much in the external privileges and performances of religion" while their hearts remain unchanged. The commentary warns against having "a fiery concern for the name of Christianity" while showing "profane contempt of the thing."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering applications:

**On spiritual presumption**: Israel's defeat despite having the ark demonstrates that "external privileges will secure none that abuse them and do not live up to them." True victory requires addressing sin first - they should have begun "their work at the right end" through repentance.

**On false spiritual confidence**: Henry warns against trusting in religious forms without substance, noting it's common for those "estranged from the vitals of religion to discover a great fondness for the rituals." 

**On human pride**: When defeated, Israel "quarrelled with God" rather than examining their own sin. Henry notes how "the foolishness of man perverts his way, and then his heart frets against the Lord."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry suggests Israel didn't consult Samuel despite his prophetic calling, perhaps because "the princes of Israel despised his youth."

**Verse 3**: Israel's question "Wherefore hath the LORD smitten us?" reveals their spiritual blindness - they "expostulate boldly with God" rather than acknowledge obvious sins.

**Verses 4-5**: The people's "profound veneration for the ark" while ignoring moral reformation shows how religious externals can mask spiritual emptiness.

**Verses 6-9**: The Philistines' fear was misplaced since they had "confused traditions" and "gross notions" of divine presence, yet their resolve actually strengthened.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical themes:

- References **Judges 13:1** regarding the forty years of Philistine oppression
- Cites **Proverbs 19:3** on how human foolishness leads to blaming God
- Alludes to **Deuteronomy 12:5,11** about the ark's proper place of rest
- Connects to Moses' prayer when the ark moved forward (Numbers 10:35)
- Implicitly references the Achan incident (Joshua 7) regarding sin in the camp

Henry concludes that these events fulfilled the earlier prophecy against Eli's house, demonstrating how "such sinners God often makes quick work with." The chapter serves as a sobering reminder that religious privileges without spiritual reality provide no protection from divine judgment.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Samuel 5

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty and the ultimate triumph of His glory over false gods. The central theme is God defending His own honor when His people fail to do so. Henry notes that while Israel showed shocking apathy toward the ark's capture—"It is gone, and let it go"—God "would therefore take the work into his own hands and plead his own cause, since men would not appear for him."

The commentary demonstrates that God uses even adverse circumstances for His glory. Henry observes that "even the wrath of man shall praise him" and that God is "bringing about his own glory even when he seems to neglect it." The chapter illustrates the futility of idolatry and the certainty of Christ's kingdom prevailing over Satan's realm.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons. He criticizes those who "have softness enough to lament the loss of the ark that have not hardiness enough to take one step towards the recovery of it." This challenges believers to move beyond mere sentiment to active faith.

Regarding Dagon's repeated falls, Henry applies this prophetically: "The kingdom of Satan will certainly fall before the kingdom of Christ, error before truth, profaneness before godliness, and corruption before grace in the hearts of the faithful." He encourages believers that even when "the interests of religion seem to be run down and ready to sink," they can be confident "that the day of their triumph will come."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry explains the Philistines placed the ark in Dagon's temple either as religious syncretism or as a victory trophy, but notes God "is not worshipped at all if he is not worshipped alone."

**Verses 3-5**: The repeated destruction of Dagon demonstrates divine power over idols. Henry observes the irony: "A sorry silly thing it was to make a god of, which, when it was down, wanted help to get up again." When Dagon's head and hands were severed, it showed him "utterly destitute both of wisdom and power."

**Verses 6-12**: The plague progression through Philistine cities shows God's escalating judgment. Henry notes that proper repentance after Dagon's fall "might have prevented the vengeance," but their obstinate idolatry brought divine wrath.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes. He references **Psalm 76:10** regarding God's sovereignty over human wrath, **Deuteronomy 32:26-27** about God preventing total destruction to protect His reputation, and **Isaiah 41:7** and **46:7** about the futility of securing idols.

He connects Dagon's threshold superstition to **Zephaniah 1:9** and links the Philistines' hardened hearts to **2 Timothy 3:13**. The commentary also references **Isaiah 8:9** regarding opposition to God and **Exodus 18:11** about God's supremacy over all gods.

Henry's exposition effectively demonstrates how this historical narrative reveals timeless truths about God's character, the certainty of His triumph, and the call for believers to actively participate in His kingdom purposes rather than remaining passive observers.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 6: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **God's sovereignty even in captivity**—though the ark was held by pagan Philistines for seven months, it remained "a curse to them" wherever they carried it, demonstrating that God's power cannot be contained or controlled by enemies. The commentary highlights the theme of **divine judgment and mercy**, showing how "sinners lengthen out their own miseries by obstinately refusing to part with their sins," drawing parallels to Pharaoh's hardened heart.

A central theological point concerns **atonement and sacrifice**. Henry notes that while the Philistines correctly understood that "injured justice demands satisfaction" and sought to offer a trespass offering, their golden images revealed them as "grossly ignorant of the methods of reconciliation appointed by the law of Moses; for there it appears all along that it is blood, and not gold, that makes atonement for the soul."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry warns against **hardening our hearts** like the Egyptians, noting "It is much cheaper to learn by other people's experience than by our own." He encourages believers during times when they're "deprived of the comfort of public ordinances" to remember that "we may have God nigh unto us when the ark is at a distance" through "faith and prayer."

The commentary emphasizes **proper reverence for holy things**, condemning the "over-bold curiosity" of the Israelites who looked into the ark and were struck dead, demonstrating that even God's people must approach Him with appropriate fear and respect.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: The seven-month captivity was punishment for both sides—Israel lost God's presence while the Philistines suffered under it.

**Verses 6-9**: Henry praises the Philistines' knowledge of Mosaic history, showing how God's past judgments should warn against obstinate rebellion.

**Verses 12-16**: The miraculous guidance of the untrained cattle "contrary to their natural instinct" proved divine intervention, yet Henry notes how "bad men are willing to shift off their convictions of the hand of God upon them."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively references **Deuteronomy 28:16** ("Cursed shalt thou be in the city, and cursed in the field"), **Romans 1:18** (imprisoning truth in unrighteousness), **Psalm 105:38** (Egypt's gladness at Israel's departure), and **Malachi 3:10** regarding testing God through reformation.

The commentary connects this narrative to broader biblical themes of God's faithfulness in delivering His people and His judgment on those who oppose Him, while emphasizing that true atonement comes through blood sacrifice, not material offerings.

# Summary: Matthew Henry's Commentary on 1 Samuel Chapter 7

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the contrast between the ark's obscurity and Samuel's rise as Israel's deliverer. He sees the ark's 40-year stay in Kirjath-jearim as divine judgment for Israel's neglect and lack of "holy zeal." Theologically, Henry argues this shows "the great stress which the institution laid upon the ark was but typical of Christ, and those good things to come which cannot be moved."

The commentary highlights true repentance as beginning with "lamenting after the Lord" - being "sensible that by sin we have provoked him to withdraw." Henry notes that "True repentance and conversion begin in lamenting after the Lord; we must be sensible that by sin we have provoked him to withdraw and are undone if we continue in a state of distance from him."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

- **On God's ordinances**: "The judgments of God on those who profane his ordinances should not make us afraid of the ordinances, but of profaning them and making an ill use of them."

- **On idolatry**: True repentance requires complete abandonment of idols: "True repentance strikes at the darling sin, and will with a peculiar zeal and resolution put away that, the sin which most easily besets us."

- **On pastoral ministry**: "Ministers would profit their people more if they did but pray more for them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry observes that common Israelites at Kirjath-jearim showed more devotion than the priests at Beth-shemesh, noting "Sometimes priests are shamed and out-done in religion by common Israelites."

**Verses 3-4**: Samuel's preaching demanded total commitment - "serve him only, else you do not serve him at all so as to please him."

**Verse 6**: The water-pouring ceremony symbolized multiple aspects: humiliation ("as water spilt upon the ground"), earnest prayer ("the soul is, in prayer, poured out before God"), and complete reformation.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter to numerous passages: Jeremiah 25:6 on not provoking God, Zechariah 12:10 on mourning, Hebrews 9:23 and 12:27 on Christ as the fulfillment of Old Testament types, Hosea 14:8 on abandoning idols, and Acts 2-3 as the only comparable revival in Scripture history.

The commentary presents Samuel as a model reformer who combined preaching against idolatry with intercessory prayer, ultimately leading Israel to both spiritual renewal and military victory over the Philistines.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Israel's request for a king as fundamentally a **rejection of God's sovereignty**. The central theological issue is not Samuel's aging or his sons' corruption, but Israel's desire to "be like all the nations" rather than remaining God's unique people. God tells Samuel, "They have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them" (v. 7).

Henry emphasizes this represents a pattern of **spiritual rebellion** dating back to the Exodus: "According to all the works which they have done since the day that I brought them up out of Egypt...wherewith they have forsaken me, and served other gods, so do they also unto thee" (v. 8). The commentary views this as Israel forsaking their "own mercies" and "thrusting themselves down from their own excellency" by abandoning their unique position as God's directly-governed people.

## Key Practical Applications

**On leadership transition and family legacy**: Henry notes the sobering reality that "those that have the most grace themselves cannot give grace to their children." He warns that many who "have begun well" and "set out in the right path" later "turn aside to by-paths," becoming grief rather than joy to their parents. Samuel's sons exemplify how "Honours change men's minds, and too often for the worse."

**On contentment and discernment**: Henry observes how "hard it is for people to know when they are well off." Israel had "a prophet to judge them, that had immediate correspondence with heaven," making them "great and happy above any nation," yet they preferred external pomp over spiritual privilege.

**On corruption in leadership**: He emphasizes that while Samuel's sons weren't as "profane and vicious as Eli's sons," their bribery was still devastating: "The love of money is the root of all evil. It is pernicious in any, but especially in judges."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry notes Samuel wasn't even sixty but had aged rapidly due to early responsibilities and "public business." He coined the memorable phrase: "The fruits that are the first ripe keep the worst."

**Verses 11-18**: God's warning about kingship is interpreted not as describing ideal monarchy, but as showing how human kings inevitably become oppressive, taking citizens' children, property, and freedom until "ye shall be his servants."

**Verses 19-20**: Henry sees Israel's insistence despite God's warnings as revealing their true motivation—not better governance but conformity: "Give us a king...any body that will but make a figure."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical themes:
- **Deuteronomy 4:7** - Israel's unique privilege of having "God so nigh unto them"
- **Judges 9:22** - Abimelech's failed kingship as precedent
- **Chapter 12:3** - Samuel's integrity contrasted with his sons' corruption
- **Philippians 4:6** - Samuel's response of prayer as a model for believers
- **Psalm 69:7** and **Colossians 1:24** - Bearing reproach for God's sake

The commentary presents this chapter as a pivotal moment where Israel chose worldly conformity over divine distinction, establishing a pattern of spiritual compromise that would plague their monarchy throughout its history.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 9 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty in leadership selection**, noting how remarkably no one in Israel sought the crown despite its availability. This demonstrated that "Most governments began in the ambition of the prince to rule, but Israel's in the ambition of the people to be ruled." God's choice of Saul reflects His accommodation to human desires rather than His perfect will - "when God chose a king after his own heart he pitched upon one that was not at all remarkable for the height of his stature... But when he chose a king after the people's heart, who aimed at nothing so much as stateliness and grandeur, he pitched upon this huge tall man."

The chapter illustrates **God's providence working through ordinary circumstances**. Henry observes how "a great event arising from small occurrences" - lost donkeys lead to finding a kingdom. This demonstrates God's ability to orchestrate seemingly mundane events for His purposes.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Filial obedience and diligence**: "Seest thou a man diligent in his business, and dutiful to his superiors, willing to stoop and willing to take pains? he does as Saul stand fair for preferment." Saul's willingness to seek his father's donkeys demonstrated commendable character.

**Consideration for parents**: Children should be "tender of their tenderness," as Saul showed concern that his father would worry about their extended absence.

**Spiritual priorities**: Henry critiques their shallow approach to consulting Samuel, noting "Most people would rather be told their fortune than told their duty, how to be rich than how to be saved."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Saul's physical appearance (v.2), Henry notes God chose someone impressive externally to satisfy the people's desires for a king "like the nations." 

Regarding the servant's proposal to consult Samuel (v.6), Henry observes the servant had "more religion in him than his master" and emphasizes that "Men of God are honourable men, and should be so in our eyes."

The discussion about bringing a gift (v.7-8) reveals character flaws: "Most people love a cheap religion, and like it best when they can devolve the expense of it on others."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple scriptures: Romans 11:1 and Philippians 3:5 regarding Benjamin's honor; 1 Corinthians 12:24 about God giving honor to lesser parts; Deuteronomy 17:15 about God's prerogative to choose Israel's king; 1 Samuel 2:8 about God raising the poor; and 1 Samuel 16:7,12 contrasting David's selection with Saul's.

The commentary demonstrates how God's providence operates through ordinary circumstances while accommodating human desires that fall short of His perfect will.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes that Saul's anointing represents **divine sovereignty in establishing monarchy in Israel**. He notes "much of God is seen" in this great work of setting up both a monarch and monarchy itself. The anointing ceremony, though conducted privately "under a hedge," carries full divine authority, demonstrating that "God's institutions are great and honourable, though the circumstances of their administration be ever so mean."

Henry presents the **symbolic significance of the anointing oil**, noting it was "only a vial of oil" with a "brittle vessel" because Saul's kingdom would be "cracked and broken," contrasting this with David's later anointing with a horn of oil. The anointing points to **Christ as the ultimate Messiah** - "the great anointed one, the king of the church, and high priest of our profession."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Humility in Leadership**: Saul must remember receiving alms (the two loaves) and "therefore be humble and charitable to the poor." Leaders should maintain simple lifestyles, as "Bread is the staff of life."

**Submission to Divine Authority**: Even as king, Saul "must act under the direction of Samuel." Henry emphasizes that "the greatest of men must own themselves in subjection to God and his word."

**The Transforming Power of Worship**: Saul's joining the prophets demonstrates how "the Spirit of God, by his ordinances, changes men, wonderfully transforms them." Henry notes we should "come from holy ordinances with our hearts greatly enlarged in holy joy and praise."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Samuel's kiss represents both "approbation of the choice" and "homage and allegiance," serving as a model for how "we must acknowledge him and do homage" to Christ.

**Verses 2-4**: The signs serve not just as confirmation but as lessons - meeting messengers near Rachel's tomb reminds Saul of mortality, while receiving bread from worshippers teaches humility.

**Verses 5-6**: The encounter with prophets at "the hill of God" demonstrates God's power to transform character. Henry observes that Saul became "another man, but whether a new man or no may be questioned."

**Verse 9**: "God gave him another heart" - Henry explains this as receiving "a new fire" in his breast, shifting his focus from mundane concerns to royal responsibilities.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the anointing to **Psalm 2:12** ("Kiss the Son"), showing how Samuel's kiss prefigures homage to Christ. He references **2 Kings 3:15** regarding music's role in spiritual preparation, and **Psalm 138:5** about coming from worship with enlarged hearts.

The commentary links Saul's prophesying to the broader work of **schools of prophets** that Samuel established, suggesting these institutions helped give rise to later synagogues and kept religion alive in Israel.

Henry concludes by noting Saul's discretion in not revealing "the matter of the kingdom" to his uncle, showing appropriate reserve about divine callings until the proper time for public revelation.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 11: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **God's sovereignty in leadership preparation**, noting that while Israel erred in demanding a king, God "did not reject them when they rejected him" and demonstrated His wisdom by making Saul "fit for the great trust" through enabling him to "merit the crown by his public services." The commentary highlights how **the Spirit of God came upon Saul** (v.6), showing divine empowerment for leadership.

A key theme is **God's grace despite human rebellion**. Henry warns against Israel inferring "they did well to ask a king" simply because God blessed Saul's victory, since "God could and would have saved them without one." This reflects God's faithfulness even when His people make faithless choices.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for both leaders and citizens:

**For Leaders:** Saul exemplifies proper humility, continuing to tend his flocks despite being anointed king, showing he "was not puffed up with his advancement." Leaders should maintain concern for subjects, as "good magistrates are in pain if their subjects are in tears."

**For Citizens:** Henry emphasizes that "religion and the fear of God will make men good subjects, good soldiers, and good friends to the public interests." The people responded to Saul's call because "the fear of the Lord fell upon them," demonstrating how spiritual devotion produces civic responsibility.

**For Crisis Response:** Saul's measured approach—numbering forces, strategic planning, and confident faith—provides a model for leadership under pressure.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3:** Henry sees the Gileadites' quick surrender as spiritual failure: "Had they not broken their covenant with God...they needed not thus to have courted a covenant with a Gentile nation." Their "abject concessions" only made the Ammonites "more insolent."

**Verse 7:** Saul's oxen-cutting summons demonstrates both "modesty" (joining Samuel in commission) and "mildness" (threatening only property, not lives). Henry notes this was "not nearly so severe" as previous judgments, showing Saul's gentler governance approach.

**Verse 11:** The complete victory reflects divine blessing on righteous leadership responding to genuine need.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter to broader biblical themes through multiple references:
- **Deuteronomy 2:19** regarding Israel's relationship with Ammonites
- **Judges 21:10** on Jabesh-Gilead's previous destruction
- **Proverbs 27:23-24** supporting Saul's continued attention to flocks
- **Deuteronomy 28:31** on threatened livestock destruction
- **Job 25:3** contrasting human and divine armies

The commentary particularly emphasizes how this victory foreshadows Israel's need for the ultimate King, while warning against misreading God's blessing as validation of their original faithless demand for earthly monarchy.

## Matthew Henry Commentary Summary - 1 Samuel 12

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as Samuel's "farewell sermon" and "Saul's coronation sermon," emphasizing God's sovereignty in Israel's history. The central theological theme is God's faithfulness despite human unfaithfulness. Henry highlights the tension between divine theocracy ("The Lord is your king") and human desire for earthly monarchy. He interprets the people's rejection of Samuel as ultimately a rejection of God's direct rule, showing how "they desired a bigger man" rather than better governance.

The commentary emphasizes God's "righteous acts" - both His merciful deliverances and just punishments - as demonstrations of His covenant faithfulness throughout Israel's cyclical pattern of sin, oppression, repentance, and deliverance.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**For Leaders:** Samuel's integrity provides "a good example" for successors. Henry notes the "just debt which every man owes to his own good name, especially men in public stations" - leaders must guard their reputation against unjust suspicions to "finish our course with honour as well as joy."

**For All Believers:** The commentary emphasizes that "Religion has reason on its side" and that ministers should "reason with people...to convince men's judgments, and so to gain their wills and affections." Henry advocates remembering God's past faithfulness: "Let their children then reckon that good at all times which they found good in bad times."

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5:** Henry praises Samuel's humble transition, noting he "had spent his days in their service" as "a guide to direct you, as a shepherd that leads his flock." Samuel's appeal for witnesses demonstrated his government "had no fault but that it was too cheap, too easy, too gentle."

**Verses 6-13:** Henry emphasizes the historical recitation as essential for spiritual formation, showing God's pattern of deliverance through unlikely leaders like Moses and Aaron "from mean beginnings."

**Verses 14-15:** The blessing and curse are presented as conditional on obedience, with Henry stressing that even under monarchy, God's favor depends on faithfulness.

### Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout: Psalm 80:1 (shepherd imagery), Nehemiah 5:18 (righteous governance), Acts 20:33 (Paul's integrity), 3 John 12 (good testimony), Isaiah 1:18 (reason in religion), and Judges 5:11 (God's righteous acts). These references demonstrate how Samuel's example prefigures New Testament leadership principles and connects to broader biblical themes of godly governance and personal integrity.

The commentary concludes that while Israel now has their desired king, their prosperity still depends entirely on covenant faithfulness to their ultimate King - the Lord.

Henry presents this chapter as marking David's entrance as "one of the worthies of the Old Testament" and "as illustrious a type of Christ." # Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Samuel 17

Henry presents this chapter as pivotal in God's preparation of David for kingship, emphasizing divine providence over human strength. # Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 23: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Public Service Despite Personal Cost**: David's willingness to serve his country while being persecuted demonstrates that believers should not "sullenly decline to do good because they have not been so well considered as they deserved for the services they have done."

**Patience in Trials**: Henry praises David's restraint in not taking revenge against Saul, showing "eminent virtues, his humility, modesty, fidelity to his prince, and patient attendance on the providence of his God."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights David's double inquiry of God (verses 2-4) as showing both "generosity and public-spiritedness" and "piety and regard to God." He notes the irony that Abiathar brought the ephod to David after the victory, providing "a great comfort to David, in his banishment, that when he could not go to the house of God he had some of the choicest treasures of that house brought to him."

Regarding the men of Keilah's potential betrayal, Henry observes how God's foreknowledge reveals human frailty - they would abandon their deliverer when pressured, demonstrating that gratitude often fails under trial.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects David's experience to Psalm 35:12 and 109:4 regarding enemies rewarding "evil for good," and parallels it with Christ's treatment in John 10:32. He references Psalm 140:8 in relation to Saul's presumptuous claims of divine favor. The overarching principle is supported by his opening quote: "Many are the troubles of the righteous, but the Lord delivereth them out of them all."

The commentary emphasizes that God's providential care for His anointed, even in apparent defeat, demonstrates His sovereignty over human schemes and His faithfulness to His covenant promises.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 24: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Providence and Restraint**: Henry emphasizes how God's providence orchestrates events—bringing Saul defenseless into David's cave while demonstrating divine grace through David's restraint. This illustrates that "David's sparing Saul's life was as great an instance of God's grace in him as the preserving of his own life was of God's providence over him."

**Respect for God's Anointed**: The central theological principle centers on respecting divinely appointed authority. David declares, "The LORD forbid that I should do this thing unto my master, the LORD's anointed." Henry presents this as fundamental to understanding legitimate authority and submission, even under persecution.

**Good Overcoming Evil**: David exemplifies Christ-like behavior by "rendering good for evil," serving as "both a type of Christ, who saved his persecutors, and an example to all Christians not to be overcome of evil, but to overcome evil with good."

## Key Practical Applications

**Tender Conscience**: When David's "heart smote him" for merely cutting Saul's robe, Henry notes: "It is a good thing to have a heart within us smiting us for sins that seem little; it is a sign that conscience is awake and tender, and will be the means of preventing greater sins."

**Misinterpreting Providence**: Henry warns against misreading God's promises and providences. David's men wrongly interpreted God's protection as license for revenge, showing how "apt we are to misunderstand the promises of God" and "the providences of God."

**Loyal Submission**: Even under unjust treatment, believers should maintain respect for authority. Henry references 1 Peter 2:18, teaching "servants and subjects to be dutiful and loyal, whatever hardships are put upon them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Saul's relentless pursuit reveals how "it is not enough for Saul that David is thus cooped up; he cannot be easy while he is alive," demonstrating the destructive nature of unchecked jealousy.

**Verse 13**: The ancient proverb "Wickedness proceedeth from the wicked" teaches that "bad men will do bad things" and that "men's own iniquity will ruin them at last."

**Verse 14**: David's self-description as "a dead dog, after a flea" shows proper humility before authority while highlighting the disproportionate nature of Saul's persecution.

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry connects this passage to **Jeremiah 7:10** regarding those "delivered to do all these abominations," **Psalm 7:3-5** as David's appeal to God concerning false accusations, **1 Peter 2:18** on dutiful submission under hardship, and **Song of Songs 1:7** regarding sheep seeking shelter. He also suggests this incident may have inspired **Psalm 7**, where David appeals to God's justice while maintaining his innocence.

The commentary presents David as a Christ-type, emphasizing how proper understanding of divine authority, tender conscience, and patient endurance under persecution reflect godly character and trust in divine justice rather than human revenge.

- **Psalm 37:25** regarding David's begging, showing God's ultimate faithfulness
- **1 Timothy 5:6** contrasting worldly prosperity with true spiritual life
- **Psalm 120:5** connecting David's wilderness experience to dwelling "in the tents of Kedar"

The commentary consistently demonstrates how this historical narrative illuminates timeless principles about divine providence, human character, and the contrast between worldly and spiritual wisdom.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Samuel Chapter 26

Henry connects this passage to **Romans 13:2** regarding resistance to God's ordained authority and **Romans 11:8** about God binding people "with a spirit of slumber." He references **Psalm 76:5-6** describing how "the stout-hearted" are spoiled through divine rebuke.

The commentary draws parallels to **Nabal's case**, showing God's pattern of avenging His servants in due time, encouraging David (and readers) to trust divine justice rather than self-vindication.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 27: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a study in human weakness despite divine calling. He emphasizes that "David was a man after God's own heart, and yet he had his faults, which are recorded, not for our imitation, but for our admonition." The primary theological theme centers on **the weakness of faith under prolonged trial**. Henry identifies David's core sin as unbelief, asking "O thou of little faith! wherefore dost thou doubt? Was he not anointed to be king? Did not that imply an assurance that he should be preserved to the kingdom?"

The commentary highlights how **unbelief affects even good men**: "unbelief is a sin that easily besets even good men. When without are fightings, within are fears, and it is a hard matter to get over them." Henry warns that "long trials are in danger of tiring the faith and patience even of very good men."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On Decision-Making**: David erred by "consulting his own heart only, and not the ephod or the prophet." This illustrates the danger of making major decisions without seeking God's guidance.

**On Humility and Contentment**: David's modest request for a country town rather than dwelling in the royal city demonstrates that "those that would stand fast must not covet to stand high; and humble souls aim not to dwell in royal cities." Henry notes "there is nothing lost by humility and modesty."

**On Family Leadership**: Henry praises David for bringing his household, noting that "masters of families ought to take care of those that are committed to them, to protect and provide for those of their own house."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry interprets David's fear as stemming from observable circumstances rather than trusting God's promises. Despite past divine protection, David concluded he would "perish one day by the hand of Saul."

**Verses 5-7**: David's request for Ziklag is seen as providential, allowing him to "keep up a correspondence with his own countrymen" while avoiding the idolatries of Philistine court life.

**Verses 8-12**: Henry presents David's military actions against the Amalekites as justified since "those people whom he cut off were such as heaven had long since doomed to destruction." However, he strongly condemns David's deception of Achish as "an equivocation not at all becoming his character."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical themes:

- **David's anointing** and God's promises of kingship
- **1 Chronicles 12:1-22** regarding supporters who came to David at Ziklag
- **Joshua 15:31 and 19:5** concerning Ziklag's original tribal assignment
- **Chapter 22:5** where God commanded David to remain in Judah
- **Psalm references** to David's later penitential reflections on deception

Henry concludes by suggesting David later reflected on this period when he prayed, "Remove from me the way of lying," indicating the episode's lasting impact on David's conscience and spiritual development.

The commentary balances understanding David's human frailty while maintaining that his actions—particularly his dishonesty—were inexcusable despite the circumstances.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 1 Samuel 28

Henry presents this chapter as illustrating the tragic consequences of departing from God. The central theological theme is divine judgment manifested through God's silence toward Saul. On **verse 6** (David's distress and the threat of stoning), Henry provides rich analysis of David's response versus his men's reaction. Regarding **verses 7-8** (consulting the ephod), Henry suggests David may have neglected this practice while with the Philistines, possibly being "ashamed to own his religion so far among the uncircumcised," and now recognizes this oversight.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 1 Samuel 31: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes **divine justice** as the central theme, describing this as "the day of recompence" when Saul must "account for the blood of the Amalekites which he had sinfully spared, and that of the priests which he had more sinfully spilt." The commentary stresses that **God's sovereignty** governs even tragic circumstances. Henry explains Jonathan's death as serving God's purposes: completing Saul's judgment, clearing David's path to the throne, and demonstrating that "the difference between good and bad is to be made in the other world, not in this."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws sobering lessons about **trusting versus forsaking God**: "One is set over against the other, that men may see what comes of trusting in God and what comes of forsaking him." He warns against following ungodly leadership, noting how Saul's followers "had followed him and served him in his sin went before him in his fall."

Regarding Saul's suicide, Henry provides stern condemnation while offering spiritual counsel: "Those are in a deplorable condition indeed who, being bitter in soul, long for death" and "despair of the mercy of God." He contrasts proper death preparation - being "solicitous to resign his soul into the hands of God" - with Saul's worldly concerns about bodily shame.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 2** (Jonathan's death): Henry wrestles with this tragedy, acknowledging that "it grieves us to find Jonathan among them" since he was innocent yet perished. This challenges simplistic theodicy while affirming God's mysterious purposes.

**Verse 4** (Saul's suicide): Henry condemns this as the act of "a man that had neither the fear of God nor hope in God," calling it the death of "a proud fool, a sneaking coward." He notes the armor-bearer's wisdom in refusing to participate.

**Verses 11-13** (Rescue by Jabesh-Gilead): Henry commends their grateful courage, noting they "remembered the good service he had done them in the beginning of his reign."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes:
- **Job 4:7** and **Job 18:5** regarding divine judgment
- **1 Samuel 12:25** fulfilling Samuel's prophecy about king and people being consumed
- **Hosea 13:10-11** on God giving and taking kings in anger
- **Ecclesiastes 12:7** on properly committing one's spirit to God
- **2 Samuel 1:20** referencing David's later lament

Henry concludes that this chapter demonstrates how rebellion against God leads to comprehensive destruction, while God's purposes ultimately prevail through his chosen servant David.
