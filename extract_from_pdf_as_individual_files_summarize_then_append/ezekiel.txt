Chapter 1
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Ezekiel 1 as a foundational vision revealing God's glory in three dimensions: **the heavenly realm** (living creatures representing angels), **divine providence** (the wheels symbolizing God's governance of earthly affairs), and **the person of Christ** (the figure on the throne). Henry emphasizes that this magnificent vision serves to establish divine authority: "When such a God of glory speaks, it concerns us to hear with attention and reverence; it is at our peril if we do not."

The commentary stresses that God's prophetic word transcends geographical boundaries, noting that "the spirit of prophecy was not confined to the land of Israel" but extended even to Babylon, foreshadowing the Gospel's eventual spread to the Gentiles.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Ezekiel's circumstances:

**Fellowship in Suffering**: "Words of conviction, counsel, and comfort, come best to those who are in affliction from their fellow sufferers." Ezekiel's credibility came partly from sharing the captives' experience.

**God's Accessibility**: "Wherever we are we may keep up our communion with God. Undique ad cœlos tantundem est viæ—From the remotest corners of the earth we may find a way open heavenward."

**Ministerial Preparation**: Henry emphasizes that those called to bring others to God must be "well acquainted with God themselves, and much affected with what they know of him."

**Timing of Divine Mercy**: God waited five years before raising up Ezekiel, teaching the people to value prophetic ministry after experiencing its absence.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1 (the "thirtieth year")**: Henry offers multiple interpretations—Ezekiel's age when priests normally began full service, the Chaldean chronological system, or thirty years since Hilkiah found the law book under Josiah.

**Verse 3 ("the word came expressly")**: Henry provides an emphatic reading: "Essendo fuit verbum Dei—The word of the Lord was as really it was to him...it came close to him, nay, it came into him, took possession of him."

**"The hand of the Lord was upon him"**: Interpreted as divine protection and enablement, like God's covering of Moses, ensuring Ezekiel could withstand the overwhelming divine revelations.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:

- **Genesis 13:4** - returning to places of divine encounter
- **Jeremiah 24:5** - the exiles as "good figs" 
- **Psalm 94:12** - being taught while chastened
- **Job 33:23** - the value of divine messengers during affliction
- **Revelation 1:10,17** - John's parallel experience of divine vision
- **2 Timothy 2:9** - God's word not being bound even when ministers are
- **2 Corinthians 1:5** - consolations abounding with afflictions

Henry particularly connects Ezekiel's experience to other inaugural prophetic visions (Isaiah 6, Jeremiah 1) and emphasizes parallels with New Testament revelatory experiences, demonstrating the consistency of God's communication patterns across biblical history.

The commentary concludes with Henry's memorable observation that divine grace provides both protection and empowerment: "God's grace is sufficient for him, and, in token of that, his hand is upon him."

Chapter 2
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 2: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Ezekiel's commission as a divine ordination to prophetic ministry, drawing parallels to Christ's calling of Paul. The central theme is **God's gracious persistence in sending messengers to rebellious people**. Even in captivity, God continues to offer the means of grace, demonstrating that "Christ gives the means of grace to many who he knows will not make a good use of those means."

The **"Son of man" title** receives significant attention as both humbling and honoring. Henry sees it as reminding Ezekiel of his mortality ("a mean, weak, mortal creature") while also connecting him typologically to Christ, since this title becomes central to Jesus' self-identification.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes several practical points for ministry:

**Divine empowerment is essential**: "God is graciously pleased to work that in us which he requires of us and raises those whom he bids rise." Ministers cannot accomplish their calling through human strength alone.

**Courage in the face of opposition**: Ministers must "be not afraid of them, neither be afraid of their words, though briers and thorns be with thee." Henry notes that prophetic ministry inevitably encounters hostility from hardened hearts.

**Faithful proclamation regardless of reception**: Whether people "hear or forbear," God's word must be declared. The minister's responsibility is faithful delivery, not successful results.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1-2**: The Spirit's role is crucial - "the Spirit entered into him and set him upon his feet." Henry observes that "Christ conveys his Spirit by his word as the ordinary means and makes the word effectual by the Spirit."

**Verse 3-5**: Israel is described as "a rebellious nation" with generational patterns of sin. Henry notes they "retain the name of their pious ancestors, but they have wretchedly degenerated."

**Verse 6**: The imagery of "briers and thorns" and "scorpions" represents the hostile environment ministers face. Henry warns against being "dismayed at their looks."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Ezekiel's calling to **Acts 26:16** (Paul's commission), **Daniel 7:13** (Son of man prophecy), and **Jeremiah 1:10** (prophetic authority). He references **1 Corinthians 9:2** regarding the validation of true ministry through transformed lives.

The commentary links the rebellious nature of Israel to **Amos 9:7**, noting how "the children of Israel have become as the children of the Ethiopian." References to **Zechariah 1:6** emphasize how God's word will ultimately "take hold of men" regardless of their initial response.

Henry concludes that whether God's word brings life or judgment, "either way it will appear to be of divine original," demonstrating the inevitable vindication of faithful prophetic ministry.

Chapter 3
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Revelation and Prophetic Calling**: Henry emphasizes the proper reception of God's word through the symbolism of eating the scroll. The prophet must "admit this revelation into thy understanding, take it, take the meaning of it, understand it aright, admit it into thy heart." This represents complete internalization of divine truth before ministry.

**God's Sovereignty in Judgment**: A central theme is Israel's stubborn rebellion despite their privileged position. Henry notes the divine irony that "Had God sent him to any other people, though of a strange speech, surely they would have hearkened to him" while God's own people remain hardened. He resolves this tension by stating, "We must resolve this into the divine sovereignty, and say, Lord, thy judgments are a great deep."

**Divine Enablement for Ministry**: God promises to make Ezekiel's "face strong against their faces" and his "forehead as an adamant harder than flint." Henry interprets this as God's provision of courage and resilience for difficult ministry.

## Key Practical Applications

**For Ministers**: Henry emphasizes that "ministers should in their studies and meditations take in that word of God which they are to preach to others." They must be "both well acquainted and much affected with the things of God, that they may speak of them both clearly and warmly."

**Faithful Proclamation**: Ministers must speak "all that, and that only, which God spoke to him" using "Scripture truths in scripture language, their native dress." Henry advocates for biblical fidelity in preaching.

**Courage in Opposition**: "When vice is daring let not virtue be sneaking." Believers should respond to bold wickedness with equally bold righteousness, trusting God's enablement.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3 (The Sweet Scroll)**: Though filled with "lamentations, and mourning, and woe," the scroll was "as honey for sweetness" to Ezekiel. Henry observes that "gracious souls can receive those truths of God with great delight which speak most terror to wicked people."

**Verse 14 (Bitterness of Spirit)**: Henry connects this to Revelation 10:9-10, noting that while God's word is initially sweet, ministry often leads to bitterness due to people's hardened response.

**Verses 16-21 (Watchman's Role)**: Though truncated in the text, Henry introduces the significant metaphor of the prophet as watchman, highlighting ministerial responsibility for souls.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry draws numerous biblical connections:
- **Jeremiah 15:16**: "Thy words were found, and I did eat them" - emphasizing personal appropriation of God's word
- **1 Timothy 4:15**: "Meditate on these things and give thyself wholly to them" - regarding complete devotion to ministry
- **1 Corinthians 2:13**: Paul speaks "in the words which the Holy Ghost teaches" - supporting biblical language in preaching
- **Revelation 10:9-10**: John's similar experience of eating the scroll
- **Isaiah 50:7**: "Therefore have I set my face like a flint" - regarding ministerial courage

Henry masterfully weaves these references to show the consistency of God's dealings with His messengers throughout redemptive history, emphasizing both the privilege and responsibility of receiving and proclaiming divine revelation.

Chapter 4
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes that this chapter serves as divine judgment against presumptuous hope. The Babylonian captives still looked toward Jerusalem with pride, expecting to return soon. God commanded Ezekiel to demonstrate through dramatic symbolic acts that Jerusalem would indeed fall, shattering their false expectations.

The **central theological principle** is that sin inevitably leads to judgment. Henry notes the proportional nature of divine justice: "It is a day of punishment for a year of sin... justly therefore they acknowledge that God had punished them less than their iniquity deserved" (referencing Ezra 9:13).

The symbolic timeline carries deep meaning - 390 days representing Israel's 390 years of apostasy since Jeroboam, and 40 days for Judah's final rebellious period since Josiah's reforms, showing how "Judah, that had such helps and advantages for reformation, fills the measure of its iniquity in less time than Israel does."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for ministers and believers:

**For Ministers:** Ezekiel's obedience despite personal cost exemplifies faithful service. Though the symbolic acts seemed "childish and ludicrous" and were "toilsome and tiresome," and went against his natural affections for Jerusalem, "he is a prophet, and must follow his instructions, not his affections."

**For Believers:** Henry emphasizes that "the power of imagination, if it be rightly used... may be of good use to kindle and excite pious and devout affections." Visual representations of spiritual truths can powerfully impact the heart when properly directed.

The commentary warns against presumption while encouraging repentance, showing that God's dramatic measures were designed "to bring them to repentance, by showing them sin, the provoking cause of this destruction."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3 (The Tile and Iron Pan):** Henry poignantly observes that while faithful Jerusalem was once "graven upon the palms of his hands" (Isaiah 49:16), now "a worthless brittle tile or brick is thought good enough to portray it upon" due to her harlotry. The iron pan represents "inflexible resolution of both sides" - the Chaldeans determined to conquer, the Jews resolved never to surrender.

**Verses 4-8 (Lying on His Side):** The 390 days represented both the actual siege duration (minus the five-month interruption when Pharaoh's army approached) and symbolically the years of Israel's apostasy. Henry notes this wasn't constant lying but daily demonstrations "when he received visits" at appointed hours.

**Verses 9-17 (The Famine):** The mixed grains and measured portions dramatically illustrated the desperation of siege conditions. Henry emphasizes how Ezekiel's personal revulsion at using human dung for fuel (leading to God's merciful allowance of cow dung instead) demonstrated the prophet's maintained holiness while bearing others' symbolic punishment.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout:

- **Daniel 6:10** - contrasting faithful captives who looked toward Jerusalem with faith versus those who looked with pride
- **Isaiah 49:16** - God's former care for Jerusalem when faithful
- **Jeremiah 52:4-6 and 37:5-8** - historical details of Jerusalem's siege timeline
- **Isaiah 52:10** - God making bare His arm for great works
- **Matthew 13:14-15** - Christ's use of parables paralleling Ezekiel's symbolic teaching
- **Lamentations 1:14** - being bound by cords of transgression
- **1 Corinthians 9:16** - ministerial necessity and duty

Henry masterfully demonstrates how Ezekiel's dramatic prophecy connects God's justice, human responsibility, ministerial faithfulness, and the ultimate purpose of bringing people to repentance through vivid demonstration of sin's consequences.

Chapter 5
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry interprets Ezekiel 5 as "a further, and no less terrible, denunciation of the judgments of God" upon Jerusalem. The central theme is divine justice executed through symbolic action and prophetic declaration. 

**Key theological concepts include:**
- **Divine abandonment**: The hair-shaving symbolizes "God's utter rejecting and abandoning that people, as a useless worthless generation"
- **Proportional justice**: The weighing and dividing of hair demonstrates "the very exact directing of God's judgments according to equity"
- **Complete destruction**: Unlike mere trimming, total shaving indicates "the full end that God would make of Jerusalem"
- **Covenant privilege and responsibility**: Jerusalem's position "in the midst of the nations" was designed for influence and example, making their failure more grievous

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes that **"Those will be ruined that will not be reformed."** He warns that contempt for God's word "opens a door to all manner of iniquity" and that those with good education who live wickedly "put the highest affront imaginable upon God."

The commentary stresses the principle that **"When places and persons are made great, it is with design that they may do good"** - privilege comes with responsibility for positive influence.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-4 (The Hair Symbolism)**: Henry notes the prophet becomes the sign himself, showing "how much he affected himself with...the case of Jerusalem." The sharp razor represents God's instruments of judgment that "would do execution."

**Verse 5 (Jerusalem's Position)**: The city was placed among nations not just geographically, but "as a candle upon a candlestick, to spread the light of divine revelation" and serve "as the heart in the body, to invigorate this dead world."

**Verse 6 (Changing Judgments)**: This represents the ultimate perversion - not just breaking God's laws, but making them "the excuse and colour of their wickedness."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple scriptures:
- **Isaiah 1:24** - "Ah! I will ease me of my adversaries" 
- **Isaiah 7:20** - Jerusalem "shaved with a razor that is hired"
- **Deuteronomy 4:6** - Nations should see Israel as "a wise and understanding people"
- **Romans 1:25** - "changing the truth of God into a lie"
- **Psalm 68:16** - "This is the hill which God desires to dwell in"

The commentary anticipates the New Testament fulfillment when "out of Zion went forth the gospel law and the word of the Lord Jesus from Jerusalem," after which "Jerusalem was leveled with the ground."

Chapter 6
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 6: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

Matthew Henry identifies three central themes in Ezekiel 6: **divine judgment against idolatry, gracious preservation of a remnant, and the call to lamentation**. The chapter demonstrates God as both righteous judge and merciful preserver, emphasizing that "he is a jealous God and will not bear a rival."

The overarching theological message centers on spiritual adultery—Henry describes idolatry as "spiritual whoredom" that breaks the marriage covenant with God. The commentary stresses God's sovereignty in judgment, noting that "the sword of the Chaldeans is at God's command" and that divine justice will ultimately triumph over false worship.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Divine Justice**: "It is just with God to make that a desolation which we make an idol of." This warns against elevating anything above God in our affections or priorities.

**On God's Patience**: "God's patience both leaves room for repentance and is an encouragement to sinners to repent." Henry emphasizes that preserved remnants receive both space and grace to repent.

**On True Repentance**: Genuine repentance begins with remembering God—"Sin takes rise in forgetting God...Repentance takes rise from the remembrance of him." Henry notes that sanctified afflictions often become means of conversion.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry observes that Ezekiel must "set his face" boldly toward the mountains like a judge pronouncing sentence. The mountains are addressed because "the prophets might as soon gain attention from the mountains as from that rebellious and gainsaying people."

**Verses 4-6**: The destruction of idols alongside their worshippers demonstrates that "war makes woeful desolations" and that God sometimes uses idolaters (the Chaldeans) to destroy idolatry, showing divine irony in judgment.

**Verses 8-10**: Henry emphasizes the "preserved remnant" theme, noting that true penitents "shall remember me among the nations"—highlighting how exile often brings spiritual awakening.

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects this passage extensively to other scriptures. He references **Micah 6:1-2** regarding God's controversy with creation, **Isaiah 1:9** on the preserved remnant, and **1 Corinthians 3:17** warning that those who defile God's temple will themselves be destroyed.

The commentary links to **Jeremiah 3:21** on sin arising from forgetting God, and **Psalm 95:10** describing God's grief over persistent rebellion. Henry also connects the theme to the prodigal son narrative, showing how affliction brings remembrance of the Father's house.

The theological framework anticipates the New Testament understanding of spiritual adultery and God's jealous love, while the remnant theme prefigures the church as those "reserved out of the body of mankind to be monuments of mercy."

Chapter 7
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 7: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Judgment and Finality**: Henry emphasizes that God's judgment, though long delayed, is absolutely certain. He interprets "the end" as both immediate (Babylonian destruction) and eschatological, noting it "looks further to the last destruction of that nation by the Romans" and "still further to the final destruction of the world of the ungodly." The repeated phrase "An end, the end is come" demonstrates divine certainty.

**God's Sovereignty Over Wrath**: Henry stresses that "God is Lord of his anger; it does not break out but when he pleases." Divine wrath flows from justice, not caprice, and when mercy is rejected, judgment becomes inevitable. He notes the escalation: "My eye shall not spare thee, neither will I have pity" (v. 4, 9).

**Retributive Justice**: A central theme is that punishment perfectly matches sin. Henry repeatedly emphasizes: "God never sends his anger but in wisdom and justice" and "In the heaviest judgments God inflicts upon sinners he does but recompense their own ways upon them; they are beaten with their own rod."

## Key Practical Applications

**Urgency of Repentance**: Henry draws the practical lesson that "Oh that the wickedness of the wicked might come to an end, before it bring them to an end!" He emphasizes that while judgment may be delayed by God's patience, "nothing but man's sincere repentance and reformation will put them by."

**Present Implications**: Henry connects the passage to personal mortality: "Oh that we could all see that end of time and days very near, and the end of our own time and days much nearer, that we may secure a happy lot at the end of the days!"

**Response to Affliction**: He notes that trials can be either "a half evil or an only evil according as we conduct ourselves under it and make use of it," showing how the same circumstances can lead to blessing or curse.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-6 (The Coming End)**: Henry interprets this as the final period of Israel's national existence, comparing it to "the deluge was the end of all flesh" (Gen. 6:13).

**Verse 5 ("An only evil")**: Henry provides a profound distinction: "Sin is an evil, an only evil, an evil that has no good in it" while noting that for the godly, even severe judgment contains "mixtures of mercy."

**Verses 10-11 (Pride and Violence)**: Henry identifies the specific sins: "What buds in sin will blossom in some judgment or other. The pride of Judah and Jerusalem appeared among all orders and degrees of men" and "Violence has risen up into a rod of wickedness."

**Verse 15 (Universal Destruction)**: Henry notes the comprehensive nature of judgment: "Men shall be safe nowhere; for he that is in the field shall die by the sword...and he that is in the city, famine and pestilence shall devour him."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes:
- **Genesis 6:13** (the flood as precedent for final judgment)
- **Jeremiah 29:11** (contrasting false hope with God's true plans)
- **Deuteronomy 32:29** (Moses' warning about considering their latter end)
- **Matthew 24:3, 15** (connecting to Christ's prophecy of Jerusalem's destruction)
- **Daniel 12:13** (securing "a happy lot at the end of the days")
- **Ecclesiastes 3:16** and **Isaiah 5:7** (corruption in places of justice)

Henry's commentary transforms Ezekiel's ancient warning into both historical explanation and timeless spiritual warning about divine justice, human responsibility, and the urgent need for repentance before judgment becomes irreversible.

Chapter 8
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents God's revelation to Ezekiel as both **diagnostic and justifying** - showing the prophet the depth of Israel's wickedness to justify the coming judgment. The central theme is **divine jealousy** over idolatry, which Henry describes as spiritual adultery that provokes God "as a husband would resent the whoredoms of his wife." 

The **progressive revelation of sin** is emphasized - God shows increasingly worse abominations, demonstrating that "where there is one abomination it will be found that there are many more. Sins do not go alone." Henry interprets this as God building His case for judgment, asking "whether such a provoking people should have any pity shown them."

The **glory of God** appears prominently to contrast Israel's sin - "The more glorious we see God to be the more odious we shall see sin to be, especially idolatry, which turns his truth into a lie, his glory into shame."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Regarding spiritual discipline:** "The more we retreat from the world, and retire into our own hearts, the better frame we are in for communion with God." He notes that the elders who once despised God's word now sought it eagerly in captivity - "When God brings men into the cords of affliction, then he opens their ears to discipline."

**About faithful service:** "A faithful ready servant of God will be drawn by a hair, by the least intimation of the divine will." 

**Concerning hidden sins:** The secret chambers of idolatry teach that "there is more wickedness in the world, in the church, than good men think there is."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry carefully dates this vision (sixth year, sixth month, fifth day) as occurring "fourteen months" after Ezekiel's first vision, noting "God keeps a particular account of the messages he sends to us, because he will shortly call us to account about them."

The **"image of jealousy"** (v.5) is interpreted as deliberately placed "at the door of the inner gate" both to "affront God" and "debauch the people" - like "the adulteress...that sits at the door of her house, to call passengers."

The **secret chambers** (vv.7-12) reveal systematic concealment of idolatry, with Henry noting the irony that "He that doth evil hates the light" - they built walls for privacy while claiming "The LORD seeth us not."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout: **Job 36:8,10** and **Psalm 141:6** regarding affliction opening ears to discipline; **2 Kings 21:7** and **2 Chronicles 33:3** identifying possible origins of the jealousy image; **Proverbs 9:14-16** comparing idolatrous enticement to the adulteress; **Nahum 1:2** on God's jealous vengeance; and **Acts 22:9** comparing Ezekiel's vision to Paul's Damascus road experience.

The commentary maintains that this vision serves both to "justify God in all his judgments" and to prepare for the ultimate departure of God's glory from the temple, making the coming destruction both inevitable and righteous.

Chapter 9
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as God's divine judgment following revealed sin, emphasizing the principle that "when sin goes before judgments come next." The central theological framework shows **God's graduated departure from His people** - the Shekinah glory moves from the cherubim to the threshold, demonstrating that "God's departures from a people are gradual" and that "he goes from none till they first drive him from them."

The commentary highlights **divine discrimination in judgment** - God distinguishes between the righteous and wicked even in times of general calamity. Henry identifies the man in linen with the writer's inkhorn as representing **Christ as Mediator**, noting this shows "mercy mixed with judgment" and providing "great comfort to all good Christians that, in the midst of the destroyers and the destructions that are abroad, there is a Mediator."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Personal Response to Sin**: The marked remnant are those who "sigh and cry" over societal abominations. "It is not enough that we do not delight in the sins of others...but we must mourn for them, and lay them to heart." Believers must "hate sin for its own sake" and maintain "tender concern for the souls of others."

**Comfort in Trials**: Those marked for salvation receive assurance that "the comfort of knowing it will be the most powerful support and cordial in calamitous times." Henry asks, "Why should we perplex ourselves about this temporal life if we know by the mark that we have eternal life?"

**Divine Protection**: "Those who keep themselves pure in times of common iniquity God will keep safe in times of common calamity. Those that distinguish themselves shall be distinguished."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: The six destroying angels represent comprehensive judgment - "one for each of the principal gates of Jerusalem." Henry notes that while "two destroying angels were sent against Sodom, but six against Jerusalem; for Jerusalem's doom in the judgment will be thrice as heavy."

**Verse 3**: The glory's movement to the threshold shows God's reluctance to depart: "he might show how loth he was to depart, and might give them both time and encouragement to invite his return."

**Verse 4**: The marking represents God's ownership, personal assurance, and protection for His people - serving as direction "to the destroyers whom to pass by."

**Verses 5-6**: The command to "begin at my sanctuary" emphasizes that judgment starts with God's house, showing no favoritism based on religious position alone.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple biblical texts:
- **Revelation 7:3** - the sealing of God's servants parallels the marking here
- **Revelation 16:1** - the seven angels with vials of wrath 
- **Jeremiah 1:14** - evil coming from the north
- **Psalm 119:136** - David's grief over others' sins
- **Isaiah 8:11** - God teaching "with a strong hand"

The commentary draws particularly strong parallels between the marked remnant and the **Passover protection** in Egypt, where blood on doorposts preserved the firstborn from judgment.

Henry concludes that this "shows a usual method of Providence in the government of the world" - demonstrating God's consistent pattern of preserving the righteous while executing judgment on the wicked.

Chapter 10
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Ezekiel Chapter 10

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents two primary divine judgments in this chapter: **the scattering of coals of fire upon Jerusalem** (verses 1-7) and **the departure of God's glory from the temple** (verses 8-22). He emphasizes that "When God goes out from a people all judgments break in upon them."

The vision reveals God's **absolute sovereignty and majesty**. Henry notes that God appears "in the firmament above the head of the cherubim" to demonstrate that "the divine nature infinitely transcends the angelic nature." The sapphire throne represents divine authority that "far exceeding the thrones of any earthly potentates."

A key theological insight is the **dual nature of divine revelation**: "His righteousness is conspicuous as the great mountains...but his judgments are a great deep, which we cannot fathom." Henry observes the paradox that "Nothing is more clear than that God is, nothing more dark than what he is."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Reverence in worship**: The vision should "inspire us with a holy awe and dread of God" and "oblige us all to the utmost reverence in our thoughts of God and approaches to him."

**Understanding divine justice**: The fire of judgment is "just and holy, for it is fire fetched from between the cherubim," teaching that all calamities ultimately trace back to divine sovereignty - "there is not any evil of that kind in the city, but the Lord has done it."

**Recognition of spiritual loss**: Those who provoke God's departure "might know what they had lost and might lament after the Lord, groaning out their Ichabod, Where is the glory?"

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: The man in linen (representing Christ) receives fire from between the cherubim, showing that "the same Jesus that is the protector and Saviour of those that believe...will come in a flaming fire to take vengeance."

**Verse 5**: The sound of cherubim wings "as the voice of the Almighty God" was heard even in the outer court, because "the Lord's voice, in his judgments, cries in the city."

**Verses 18-19**: The glory's departure from the threshold represents God's final withdrawal from those who reject Him, opening "the door for ruin to break in."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this vision to **Habakkuk 3:4** regarding God's hidden power, **Psalm 29:3** for the voice of the Lord in thunder, **Luke 12:49** where Christ came to send fire on earth, and **Psalm 103:19** about God's heavenly throne ruling over all.

He references the **original vision by the Chebar River** (Ezekiel 1) and draws parallels to divine manifestations at **Mount Sinai** and in the **tabernacle and temple** through clouds and glory.

The commentary concludes with eschatological hope: "In the temple above there will be no cloud, but we shall see face to face," pointing toward ultimate divine revelation in glory.

Chapter 11
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: Ezekiel Chapter 11

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry frames this chapter around two contrasting divine messages: **judgment for the presumptuous and mercy for the despairing**. The core theological theme centers on God's sovereignty over false security versus genuine hope. 

The chapter demonstrates God's omniscience - "I know the things that come into your mind, every one of them" - emphasizing that human schemes cannot hide from divine judgment. Henry presents this as a warning against the deadly sin of presumption, where leaders mock God's warnings while maintaining false confidence in earthly securities.

The theological tension between God's justice and mercy runs throughout, as Henry shows how the same God who pronounces judgment on Jerusalem's arrogant leaders promises restoration to the humble exiles in Babylon.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for his readers:

**Against False Security**: He warns that "those hearts are indeed hardened which are made more secure by those words of God which were designed for warning to them." The Jerusalem princes' mocking attitude toward prophetic warnings serves as a cautionary tale against spiritual complacency.

**For Ministers**: "The greatest kindness ministers can do to secure sinners is to preach against them, and to show them their misery and danger." Henry emphasizes that faithful preaching sometimes requires confronting comfortable assumptions.

**On Divine Knowledge**: The reality that God "knows us better than we know ourselves" should motivate careful heart-keeping: "The consideration of this should oblige us to keep our hearts with all diligence."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich exposition of the "caldron" metaphor (verses 3, 7, 11). The princes boasted "this city is the caldron, and we be the flesh" - claiming Jerusalem's walls would protect them like a pot protects boiling meat. Henry notes this was likely "a banter upon Jeremiah," mocking his earlier vision of a seething pot. God's ironic response: the only "flesh" remaining would be corpses of the slain.

Regarding Pelatiah's sudden death (verse 13), Henry observes this as immediate divine authentication of Ezekiel's prophecy, demonstrating that God's judgments can strike even during prophetic pronouncement.

On verses 19-20's promise of a "new heart" and "new spirit," Henry emphasizes this represents complete spiritual transformation - not mere reformation but regeneration that enables true obedience.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage extensively to other biblical texts. He references **Jeremiah 1:13** for the seething pot imagery, **1 Samuel 2:13-14** for the caldron metaphor's possible priestly connections, and **Jeremiah 26:10** regarding princes sitting in temple gates for judgment.

The promise of restoration echoes themes Henry traces through **Deuteronomy 30** and anticipates **New Testament** fulfillment in Christian conversion. He sees the "new heart" promise as foundational to gospel transformation.

**Memorable phrases** include Henry's warning that "there is no peace to those that have left their God" and his observation that "those who are most secure" often prove "least safe."

This commentary masterfully balances theological precision with pastoral application, making ancient prophecy relevant for contemporary spiritual life.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 12: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Judgment and Sovereignty**: Henry emphasizes God's absolute control over human affairs, particularly in orchestrating Judah's captivity. The commentary highlights that God uses even rebellious nations (Babylon) as instruments of His judgment while maintaining sovereign authority over the outcome.

## Key Practical Applications

**Ministerial Adaptation**: Henry draws lessons for pastors, noting that "ministers must accommodate themselves not only to the weakness, but to the wilfulness of those they deal with." He emphasizes the need for creative communication methods when facing resistant audiences.

**Verses 2-3**: Henry explains that symbolic actions become necessary precisely because the people "have eyes and see not, they hear not; for they are a rebellious house." The visual demonstrations serve as a last resort for communication.

**Verses 4-6**: The elaborate escape simulation (digging through walls, covering faces, carrying belongings) represents Zedekiah's desperate but futile flight attempts. Henry notes this shows "what a change sin makes with men."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical themes:
- **Psalm 115:5-8**: Comparing rebellious Israel to deaf and blind idols
- **Hebrews 1:1**: God's diverse communication methods throughout history
- **Isaiah 58:2**: People who seek God daily yet remain disobedient
- **Luke 21:12**: Christ's warning that troubles come to leaders first
- **Jeremiah 34:3**: Confirming Zedekiah's inevitable captivity

The commentary emphasizes that "faith comes by hearing that word of prophecy which is more sure than vision," suggesting that ordinary Scripture study surpasses even dramatic prophetic experiences in building genuine faith. Henry concludes that God's judgment serves ultimately to vindicate His sovereignty: "they shall know that I am the LORD."

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 13 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Ezekiel 13 as a divine condemnation of false prophecy, emphasizing that **"God's faithful prophets are nowhere so sharp upon any sort of sinners as upon the false prophets"** because they "put the highest affront upon God and did the greatest mischief to his people." The chapter reveals God's sovereignty over both truth and deception, noting that **"the deceived and the deceiver are his"** (Job 12:16).

A key theological insight is that false prophets emerge as divine judgment: **"Israel was never imposed upon by pretenders to prophecy till after they had rejected and abused the true prophets."** Henry draws a parallel to how Israel later rejected the true Messiah before being deceived by false messiahs.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry emphasizes the danger of religious leaders who prioritize popularity over truth. False prophets **"contrived how to please people, not how to profit them."** They failed in their fundamental duty to "make intercession" and bring people "to repentance and reformation." 

The commentary warns against leaders who act **"like foxes in the deserts"** - appearing busy but actually serving only themselves, abandoning their flocks when danger approaches. True spiritual leaders must **"stand in the battle against vice and immorality"** rather than offering false comfort.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: False prophets are **"prophets out of their own hearts"** who **"follow their own spirit, and have seen nothing."** They lack divine commission and deliver messages from their own imagination rather than God's revelation.

**Verse 5**: The metaphor of failing to **"go up into the gaps, neither made up the hedge"** illustrates their negligence in protecting God's people through intercession and moral reform.

**Verses 8-9**: The threefold punishment excludes false prophets from: (1) the assembly of God's people, (2) the register of Israel, and (3) the promised land - representing complete spiritual disinheritance.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple scriptures: Jeremiah 23:14-16 (false prophets in Jerusalem), 2 Timothy 3:9 (their folly being manifest), 1 John 1:1 (authentic apostolic witness), Psalm 94:16 (standing for the Lord), and Matthew 7:22-23 (false prophets in Christ's name being rejected).

The commentary emphasizes that authentic prophecy has consistent divine origin, while false prophecy stems from **"one and the same spirit of error,"** demonstrating the cosmic spiritual battle between truth and deception.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 14: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry identifies the central theological tension in this chapter: the ineffectiveness of religious forms when hearts remain corrupt. The chapter demonstrates that "hearing the word and prayer are two great ordinances of God," yet even these sacred acts become meaningless when performed by those with **idols set up in their hearts**. 

Henry emphasizes God's omniscience in detecting hypocrisy, noting that "ministers know not how people stand affected when they come to hear the word, but God does." **On Spiritual Idolatry**: He warns against both literal and metaphorical idols, stating "Many who have no idols in their sanctuary have idols in their hearts." He identifies worldly affections as modern idolatry: "those whose affections are placed upon the wealth of the world and the pleasures of sense, whose god is their money, whose god is their belly."

**On Self-Deception**: Henry emphasizes personal responsibility in spiritual failure: "Sinners are their own tempters...and so they are their own destroyers." The commentary warns against approaching God while maintaining secret reservations for sin.

**Verses 7-8**: Henry notes that even "strangers" and proselytes receive no exemption from this standard, emphasizing that "a dissembled conversion is no conversion."

**Verses 12-21**: Though not fully detailed in this excerpt, Henry indicates that even the intercession of righteous figures like Noah, Daniel, and Job cannot avert determined judgment, highlighting the severity of corporate sin.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

- **Judges 10:13-14** - God sending people to the gods they've chosen

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 15: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Jerusalem through the metaphor of an unfruitful vine, emphasizing **conditional blessing and divine judgment**. The central theme is that God's chosen people, when they abandon their calling, become "the most unprofitable creatures in the world." Henry explains that while a fruitful vine is supremely valuable—"it makes glad the heart" and "cheers God and man"—an unfruitful vine is utterly worthless, "good for nothing" except fuel for fire.

The commentary develops the doctrine of **divine sovereignty in judgment**: "Those who are not fruitful to the glory of God's grace will be fuel to the fire of his wrath." Henry emphasizes that God "will not be a loser at last by any of his creatures," demonstrating His glory either through their obedience or their judgment.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws stark warnings for **religious professors who fail to live up to their calling**. He compares apostate believers to "salt that has lost its savour and is thenceforth good for nothing." The commentary warns that those who "degenerate and depart from" their profession become spiritually useless.

He applies this historically to the Jewish nation: once "famous as a holy people," when they "lost their holiness, and became wicked, were thenceforth good for nothing" and became "the most base and despicable people under the sun." The principle extends beyond temporal judgment: "though they go out from the fire of temporal judgments...there is an everlasting fire that will devour them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: Henry emphasizes the vine's singular purpose—without fruit, it lacks even basic utility as timber. He notes the "various gifts of nature" where "some are strong and not fruitful, as the oaks and cedars; others are weak but very fruitful, as the vine."

**Verse 4**: The imagery of both ends and middle burning illustrates total consumption—no one bothers to save a burning vine branch because "even when whole it was meet for no work."

**Verse 7**: Henry interprets "they shall go out from one fire, and another fire shall devour them" as successive judgments, where "escape from one" is merely "reprieve till another comes."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout: Judges 9:12-13 and Psalm 104:15 for the vine's value; Jeremiah 2:21 describing Jerusalem as originally "a choice and noble vine"; Hosea 10:1's "empty vine"; Hebrews 6:8 on rejected plants "whose end is to be burnt"; Isaiah 5:4-7 on God's vineyard producing wild grapes; Mark 9:50 on salt losing its savor; John 15:6 on branches cast into fire; and Matthew 3:10 on trees bearing no good fruit being "hewn down and cast into the fire."

The commentary concludes with the sobering principle that "when God judges, first or last he will overcome, and he will be known by the judgments which he executes."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 16: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry frames this chapter as God's justification for Jerusalem's coming desolation, using an extended allegory comparing Jerusalem to an unfaithful wife. The primary theological themes include:

**Divine Grace and Election**: God's unmerited favor toward Israel is emphasized through the metaphor of rescuing an abandoned infant. Henry notes that God chose Israel "not because he saw any thing in them inviting or promising, but so it seemed good in his sight."

**Covenant Relationship**: The progression from rescue to marriage covenant represents God's relationship with Israel, culminating at Mount Sinai where "thou becamest mine."

**Universal Depravity**: Henry applies the infant metaphor to all humanity, stating that "in the day that we were born we were shapen in iniquity and conceived in sin," explaining why "you must be born again."

Henry draws several practical lessons:

1. **Ministers' Duty**: "It is the work of ministers to cause sinners, sinners in Jerusalem, to know their abominations, to set before them the glass of the law, that in it they may see their own deformities."

2. **Self-Knowledge**: "It is requisite that we should know our sins, that we may confess them, and may justify God in what he brings upon us for them."

**Verse 8** (Time of Love): Henry identifies this as the moment of divine election, calling it "distinguishing love, when God manifested his love to us, and courted our love to him."

**Verses 9-14** (Divine Blessings): The washing, clothing, and adorning represent God's progressive work in establishing and blessing Israel, with Henry noting that their beauty was "perfect through my comeliness, which I had put upon thee."

- **Job 3:11-12** - referenced for the helplessness of newborn infants

Henry notes the complementary ministries of Jeremiah (writing consolation to captives) and Ezekiel (writing conviction to Jerusalem), showing how God uses different approaches for different needs.

The commentary emphasizes that this passage serves as both historical reflection on Israel's relationship with God and spiritual illustration of God's gracious work in converting sinners from spiritual death to life.

## Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 17

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry identifies this chapter as addressing dual covenantal betrayals: Judah's spiritual treachery against God and King Zedekiah's political treachery against Babylon. The parable of two eagles and a vine illustrates God's sovereignty over earthly kingdoms and the consequences of covenant-breaking. Henry emphasizes that "when God came to contend with them he found many grounds of his controversy," showing how multiple sins compound divine judgment.

The chapter demonstrates God's patient progression in judgment - making kingdoms "base" before destroying them entirely, "to try if that would humble them" and provide "space to repent." Henry sees this as evidence of divine mercy even amid discipline.

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons: First, that "nations by sin sell their liberty, and princes their dignity." Political troubles often stem from spiritual unfaithfulness. Second, he advocates contentment with reduced circumstances, noting "A man may live very comfortably and contentedly, though he cannot bear a part, and make a figure, as formerly."

For ministers, Henry emphasizes adapting communication methods: "Ministers should study to find out acceptable words, and try various methods to do good," using parables and familiar illustrations to reach careless audiences.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry interprets the great eagle (v. 3) as Nebuchadnezzar, whose "dominion extends itself far and wide, like the great and long wings of an eagle." Jerusalem represents Lebanon, the royal family the cedar, and Jehoiachin the "top branch" that was cropped off.

Regarding Zedekiah's appointment (vv. 5-6), Henry notes the name change from Mattaniah ("gift of the Lord") to Zedekiah ("justice of the Lord") as a reminder "to be just like the God he called his, for fear of his justice."

The vine metaphor (v. 6) shows how Zedekiah "became a spreading vine of low stature" - Henry comments it's "better to be a spreading vine of low stature than a lofty cedar of no use."

### Cross-References and Messianic Hope

Henry connects Nebuchadnezzar to Daniel's vision (Dan. 7:4) and references Job 39:30 regarding eagles. He cites 1 Corinthians 14:13 on interpretation and Isaiah 14:14 on prideful ambition.

Significantly, Henry concludes with hope: despite judgment on Zedekiah, God promises "hereafter to raise the royal family of Judah again, the house of David, in the Messiah and his kingdom" (vv. 22-24), pointing to ultimate restoration through Christ.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 18: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Individual Responsibility**: Henry emphasizes that this chapter establishes "the rule of judgment according to which God will deal with the children of men in determining them to their everlasting state." The central principle is that "the soul that sinneth, it shall die" - each person bears responsibility for their own spiritual destiny.

**Divine Justice and Sovereignty**: Henry addresses the Jews' complaint against God's fairness through their proverb "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge." He argues that God's assertion "all souls are mine" establishes His absolute sovereignty while demonstrating His equitable judgment.

**Repentance and Transformation**: The commentary highlights God's desire for repentance over punishment, noting that "it shall be well with penitents, though they began ever so ill" while "it shall be ill with apostates, though they began ever so well."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Personal Accountability**: Rather than blaming circumstances or ancestry for spiritual failure, individuals must take responsibility for their own choices and relationship with God.

**Hope for the Repentant**: The chapter offers encouragement that past sins need not determine future destiny - genuine repentance opens the door to divine favor regardless of one's starting point.

**Warning Against Presumption**: Those who begin well but turn away face judgment, emphasizing the need for perseverance in righteousness.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3** - Henry notes that the Jews' proverb was both "witty" and "sly," showing how "the malice of hell against God and religion is insinuated and propagated" through clever but blasphemous sayings.

**Verse 4** - The declaration "all souls are mine" establishes both God's ownership and care: "He that is the Maker of all things is in a particular manner the Father of spirits."

**Verses 5-9** - Henry provides detailed exposition of righteousness, emphasizing both avoiding evil ("keeping himself clean from the pollutions of sin") and active goodness in worship, relationships, and justice.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to broader biblical themes:

- **Genesis 4:7** - "If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted?" as an ancient parallel to individual responsibility
- **Job 13:26** - Comparing national judgment to an individual possessing "the iniquities of his youth"
- **Matthew 23:32** - Children filling up "the measure of their iniquity" when following parental sins
- **Romans 2:9** - Sin's punishment as "tribulation and anguish of the soul"

Henry concludes that this chapter serves two vital purposes: "to justify God and clear the equity of all his proceedings" and "to engage and encourage us to repent of our sins and turn to God." These principles, he emphasizes, "belong to our everlasting peace" and demand urgent attention before they become "hidden from our eyes."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 19: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a prophetic lament over the royal house of David, focusing on God's judgment upon the Davidic covenant due to the corruption of Judah's kings. The central theological theme is divine justice—when rulers abandon their God-given responsibility to protect and serve, they face inevitable judgment. Henry emphasizes that "when they had left their divinity they soon lost their humanity too; and, when they feared not God, neither did they regard man."

The commentary highlights the irony of the Davidic line's corruption: though Jacob had blessed Judah as a noble lion (Genesis 49:9), these kings became predatory beasts against their own subjects rather than protectors against external enemies.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for rulers and subjects alike. He warns that tyrannical leaders who "make themselves as wild beasts" will ultimately face retribution, noting that "bloody tyrants seldom die in peace." For ministers, he emphasizes the importance of genuine emotion in preaching: "how can we expect that others should be affected with that which we ourselves are not affected with?"

He underscores that abuse of divine authority leads to inevitable consequences, stating: "The righteousness of God is to be acknowledged when those who have terrified and enslaved others are themselves terrified and enslaved."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry identifies the "lioness" (v. 2) as the kingdom of Judah that "lay down among lions"—mingling with pagan nations instead of dwelling alone as God intended. The "young lions" represent specific kings: Jehoahaz, who "learned to catch the prey and devoured men" but was quickly captured and taken to Egypt; and Jehoiakim, who "knew their desolate palaces" and oppressed his subjects until foreign nations "spread their net over him."

In the vine metaphor (vv. 10-14), Henry notes that Jerusalem was initially "like a vine in thy blood, planted by the waters"—prospering despite violence—but was ultimately "plucked up in fury" when God's patience ended.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to other biblical texts. He references Psalm 89's covenant with David and its subsequent lament over the crown's profanation. He links the lion imagery to Genesis 49:9 (Judah as a lion's whelp) and Revelation's "Lion of the tribe of Judah." 

He cites Proverbs 28:15 about wicked rulers being "as roaring lions and ranging bears" over poor people, and references 2 Kings 24:2 and 2 Chronicles 36:6 for historical context about these kings' actual fates. The vine metaphor connects to his earlier commentary on Ezekiel 15:1.

Henry concludes that this lamentation serves as both historical record and prophetic warning about the consequences of abandoning covenant faithfulness.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 20: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Ezekiel 20 as a divine history lesson demonstrating God's persistent grace despite Israel's chronic rebellion. The central theological theme is **God's faithfulness to His covenant for His name's sake**, even when His people prove utterly faithless. Henry emphasizes that "when nothing in us will furnish him with a reason for his favours he furnishes himself with one."

The commentary traces a recurring pattern: God's gracious provisions followed by Israel's rebellion, divine judgment threatened, then mercy extended "for my name's sake." Henry notes this pattern began immediately with Israel's formation as a people, paralleling humanity's fall after creation, making Israel "a people designed to represent the body of mankind both in their dealings with God and in his with them."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for contemporary believers:

**On Hypocritical Worship**: The elders' consultation while planning compromise with pagan worship illustrates hearts "wretchedly hardened which ask God leave to go on in sin." **On God's Sovereign Grace**: The commentary emphasizes that Israel's deliverance came purely from divine grace, not merit. Henry notes it should "magnify the riches of God's goodness, that he was pleased to work so great a salvation for them even when he saw them ripe for ruin."

**Verse 3**: God's refusal to be consulted by hypocrites demonstrates that "their shows of devotion shall be neither acceptable to God nor advantageous to themselves."

**Verse 9**: The phrase "I wrought for my name's sake" becomes Henry's theological anchor - God acts to preserve His reputation and covenant faithfulness regardless of human merit.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout:

- **Deuteronomy 7:7-8** and **9:4-5** on God's election not being based on Israel's righteousness

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Justice and Judgment**: Henry emphasizes God's sovereign role in orchestrating judgment through the "sword" (representing Babylonian conquest). **Ministerial Responsibility**: Henry draws significant lessons for preachers from Ezekiel's commanded emotional displays. Ministers must "show that they are themselves in the greatest sincerity affected" with their message, even if it creates "uneasiness to themselves" or ridicule from others.

**The Importance of Plain Speaking**: When people complained about Ezekiel's parables, God provided interpretation. Henry applies this: "When we speak to people about their souls we should study plainness, and express ourselves as we may be the best understood."

**Verses 1-5**: Henry explains this as God's interpretation of the previous chapter's forest fire parable, demonstrating divine concern that "all men shall be rendered inexcusable at God's bar."

**Verses 8-17**: Henry interprets the sharpened, furbished sword as representing both divine justice's restored brightness after apparent eclipse and Christ's rod of iron that "contemns every tree and will bear it down."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive biblical connections throughout:

- **1 Corinthians 14:13** on interpretation of tongues
- **Mark 4:34** on Christ expounding parables
- **Genesis 18:25** on divine justice
- **Jeremiah 24:5-6** on the good figs sent to Babylon
- **Revelation 19:15** on the sharp sword from Christ's mouth
- **Psalm 2:7-9** on the Son's rod of iron

## Overall Assessment

Henry presents Ezekiel 21 as a comprehensive revelation of divine judgment's inevitability and justice. The commentary balances theological sophistication with pastoral concern, showing how God's judgments serve both justice and mercy. The chapter demonstrates that even covenant privileges cannot shield the unfaithful from consequences, while maintaining hope that divine judgment ultimately serves redemptive purposes. Henry's emphasis on ministerial authenticity and clear communication makes this ancient prophecy practically relevant for Christian leaders and believers facing difficult spiritual truths.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 22: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Ezekiel 22 as three interconnected divine messages exposing Jerusalem's comprehensive moral collapse. The **central theological theme** is divine judgment as the inevitable consequence of covenant unfaithfulness. Henry emphasizes that Jerusalem, once the "holy city," had become a "bloody city" deserving God's wrath.

**Key theological concepts include:**
- **Divine justice**: God's judgment is "according to truth" and perfectly just
- **Corporate guilt**: The entire community bears responsibility for systemic sin
- **Measure-filling sin**: Jerusalem had reached "such a pitch of impudence in sin that God could not in honour give them a further day"
- **Covenant violation**: The people had fundamentally "forgotten me [God]," which Henry identifies as "at the bottom of all this wickedness"

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several **practical warnings** for believers:

**On murder and violence**: Those who "sow discord among brethren" through gossip and tale-bearing "will be accountable for all the mischief that follows upon it; as he that kindles a fire will be accountable for all the hurt it does."

**On idolatry**: "Those who make idols for themselves will be found to have made them against themselves, for idolaters put a cheat upon themselves and prepare destruction for themselves."

**On family relationships**: "Those that set light by their parents are in the highway to all wickedness," emphasizing parental authority as foundational to social order.

**On Sabbath-breaking**: Henry warns that "Sabbath-breaking is an iniquity that is an inlet to all iniquity. Many have owned it to contribute as much to their ruin as any thing."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry structures his analysis around **Jerusalem as defendant** in a divine courtroom, with Ezekiel as judge presenting "a true bill" of indictment.

**The "bloody city" (v.2)**: Jerusalem earned this title not just for literal bloodshed, but because "her crimes in general were bloody crimes" that polluted the entire community.

**The dross metaphor (vv.17-22)**: Henry explains that just as refiners separate precious metal from worthless dross through fire, God would purge Jerusalem through judgment.

**Corporate responsibility (vv.23-31)**: Henry emphasizes that "all orders and degrees of men" contributed to national guilt, with **no intercessors** standing in the gap—a particularly sobering observation about spiritual leadership failure.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects Ezekiel's message to broader biblical themes:
- **Genesis 9:6** and **Deuteronomy 21:1** regarding laws about murder and bloodguilt
- **2 Kings 24:4** identifying bloodshed as Jerusalem's "measure-filling sin more than any"
- **Jeremiah 1:10** on prophetic authority over nations

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 23: Summary

Matthew Henry interprets this chapter as an allegorical condemnation of Israel and Judah's spiritual adultery through idolatry and political alliances. # Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 24: Summary

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 35: Summary

**Verses 12-13**: Henry emphasizes God's omniscience regarding human speech: "In the multitude of words, not one escapes God's cognizance."

## Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 37: Summary

### Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets Ezekiel 37 as addressing two major obstacles to Israel's restoration from Babylonian captivity. # Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel 40: Summary

**For Church Leadership:** Henry stresses that "gospel ministers should reside upon their charge; where their service lies there must they live," drawing from the priests' proximity to the sanctuary. He advocates for dedicated church lands, noting "The appropriating of lands for the support of religion and the ministry is an act of piety that bids as fair for perpetuity."

**For Civil Authorities:** Princes must reform their ways: "Let it suffice you, O princes of Israel! that you have been oppressive to the people." Henry emphasizes that Christian magistrates should be "nursing fathers" to the church, with "holy religion" restraining them from oppression.

**For Commerce and Justice:** Henry demands absolute honesty: "It concerns God's Israel to be very honest and just in all their dealings, very punctual and exact in rendering to all their due." He notes that "godliness without honesty is but a form of godliness."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides detailed measurements and calculations, working with scholars like Dr. Cumberland to establish precise values for biblical weights and measures. He explains the distinction between the maneh as weight (100 shekels) versus coinage (60 shekels).

The land allocation demonstrates divine priorities: sanctuary lands first as "first-fruits that sanctify the lump," then city lands "for the whole house of Israel" (representing tribal unity), followed by crown lands positioned protectively around both.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the unified city to Nehemiah 11:1-2, showing post-exilic tribal integration. He references Nehemiah 5:15,18 regarding righteous governance and Exodus 16:36 for measurement standards. The commentary links 1 Kings 10:17 with 2 Chronicles 9:16 to establish shekel calculations.

## Conclusion

Henry concludes that true religion encompasses both "righteousness toward men" and "religion towards God," viewing them as interconnected branches of universal righteousness. The chapter establishes the foundation for a holy society where justice, worship, and proper governance create the ideal community of faith.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 46: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry treats Ezekiel 46 as describing the restored temple worship, though he notes uncertainty about whether these rules were literally implemented in the second temple period or should be understood mystically. The chapter emphasizes **divine prerogative in worship**, as Henry states: "it is his prerogative to institute the rites and ceremonies of religious worship" - even princes cannot arbitrarily determine sacrificial requirements.

A central theme is **Christ as the ultimate sacrifice**. Henry observes that while morning sacrifices are specified, no evening sacrifice is mentioned, interpreting this prophetically: "Christ having come, and having offered himself now in the end of the world, we are to look upon him as the evening sacrifice."

## Key Practical Applications

**Leadership and Worship**: Henry emphasizes that "Great men should, by their constant and reverent attendance on God in public worship, give a good example to their inferiors." He praises princes who worship alongside "their servants, and tenants, and poor neighbours," noting that "those who thus honour God with their honour he will delight to honour."

**Humble Worship**: Even the prince must worship at the threshold, leading Henry to observe: "The greatest of men are less than the least of the ordinances of God. Even princes themselves, when they draw near to God, must worship with reverence and godly fear."

**Giving According to Ability**: The commentary notes that princes "must spend as they can afford," and "God expects and requires but that we should do according to our ability." This applies the principle that "God has not made us to serve with an offering but considers our frame and state."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3 (Gate Regulations)**: Henry explains the east gate's special status - opened only on sabbaths and new moons. The prince's position "at the threshold" demonstrates that even rulers should be like David, "a door-keeper in the house of his God."

**Verses 9-10 (Traffic Flow)**: The requirement to exit through opposite gates serves multiple purposes: preventing confusion, providing meditation time for worshippers to "consider the palaces of it," and symbolically ensuring people don't "go back as we came, but more holy, and heavenly, and spiritual."

**Verses 13-15 (Daily Sacrifices)**: The increased sabbath offerings (six lambs vs. the Mosaic four) intimate "how much we should abound in sabbath work, now in gospel-time."

**Verses 16-18 (Inheritance Laws)**: These laws protect subjects from royal oppression. Henry warns that a prince gains nothing by "forcing" subjects from their land, as "he will be but a poor prince when the people are scattered."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the prince's humble worship to **Psalm 84:10** (David preferring to be a doorkeeper), and to **Jeremiah 30:21** (Christ as the prince who draws near to God). 

The blemishless sacrifices point to **1 Peter 1:19** (Christ as unblemished sacrifice) and the call for Christians to present themselves as "living sacrifices." 

The inheritance laws recall **John 8:35** (difference between sons and servants) and **Genesis 48:22** (Jacob's special gift to Joseph).

Henry references **Philippians 3:13** regarding pressing forward in spiritual growth, and **1 Corinthians 16:2** on giving according to ability.

The commentary presents these regulations as both historical prescriptions and spiritual principles pointing toward Christ's superior priesthood and the Christian's worship responsibilities.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Ezekiel 47: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets the vision of waters flowing from the temple as fundamentally **spiritual and symbolic**, representing "the gospel of Christ, which went forth from Jerusalem" and "the gifts and powers of the Holy Ghost which accompanied it." He emphasizes that Christ himself is the temple and source, noting that "from him those living waters flow, out of his pierced side."

The commentary establishes a **progressive revelation theme**, observing that "God makes known his mind and will to his people, not all at once, but by degrees." The increasing depth of the waters (ankles to knees to loins to unpassable depths) symbolizes both the gospel's expanding reach and the believer's deepening understanding of divine mysteries.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Spiritual Growth**: The waters represent grace as "an active principle" that is "still pressing forward" until perfection. He encourages believers to "follow these waters, and go along with them" and "walk after" the Spirit's motions.

**Scripture Study**: Using the water depths as metaphor, Henry famously observes that "in the scripture...there are some places so shallow that a lamb may wade through them, and others so deep that an elephant may swim in them." He advises beginning with easier passages before tackling deeper mysteries.

**Evangelistic Mandate**: The waters flowing from Jerusalem eastward fulfill the principle that "from Zion should go forth the law and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem," emphasizing the gospel's universal scope.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry sees the threshold origin as significant—believers "lie at the fountainhead of comfort and grace" when they attend at God's house. The waters flowing from "the right side" indicate "gospel-blessings are right-hand blessings."

**Verses 8-11**: The healing of even the Dead Sea represents the gospel's transformative power, making "dead sinners alive and living saints lively." However, the marshes that remain unhealed warn that some will reject grace and "be given to salt"—becoming "monuments of divine justice."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to:
- **Zechariah 14:8** (living waters from Jerusalem)  
- **Revelation 22:1** (river of water of life)
- **John 4:14 and 7:38-39** (Christ as source of living water)
- **2 Kings 2:20-21** (Elisha's healing of Jericho's waters)
- **Isaiah 2:3** (law going forth from Zion)

Henry concludes that this vision represents "grace and joy which are glory begun," pointing toward the ultimate fulfillment in Revelation where streams "proceed from the throne of God and of the Lamb."

The commentary masterfully weaves together themes of divine grace, progressive sanctification, and evangelical mission, presenting the temple waters as a comprehensive picture of God's redemptive work through the gospel.

# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Ezekiel Chapter 48

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry views Ezekiel 48 primarily through a spiritual lens, emphasizing that this vision has its ultimate fulfillment in "the establishment and enlargement of the gospel church." He notes that the distribution differs radically from Joshua's original allocation, indicating this is "not so much to be understood literally as spiritually." The passage represents "the happy settlement of all those who are Israelites indeed in the sure and sweet enjoyment of the privileges of the new covenant."

A central theme is divine sovereignty in distribution. Henry observes that "in the distribution of spiritual and heavenly blessings there is not that danger of murmuring and quarrelling that there is in the participation of the temporal blessings," referencing Christ's parable about laborers receiving equal wages.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**Unity and Fellowship**: The contiguous tribal arrangement demonstrates that "like stones in an arch, they fixed, and strengthened, and wedged in one another," showing "how good and how pleasant a thing it is for brethren thus to dwell together."

**Ministerial Responsibility**: Since priests' maintenance "is reckoned a holy oblation to the Lord," ministers must "attend continually to this very thing, to reside on their cures." He warns: "Those that live upon the altar must serve at the altar, not take the wages to themselves and devolve the work upon others."

**Faithful Service**: Citizens are called to "serve the city" rather than merely seeking personal ease, emphasizing that "wherever we are, we must study to be serviceable to the place."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7**: Dan's priority placement (previously last in Joshua's distribution) illustrates the gospel principle that "the last shall be first" (Matt. 19:30).

**Verse 11**: The sons of Zadok receive special honor because they "went not astray when the children of Israel went astray." Henry notes: "Those are swimming upwards, and so they will find at last, that are swimming against the stream."

**Verse 14**: The prohibition against selling sacred land demonstrates that "it is sacrilege to convert that to other uses which is dedicated to God."

**Verses 16-17**: The city's perfect square design, "never being literally fulfilled in any city, intimates that it is to be understood spiritually of the beauty and stability of the gospel church."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to multiple biblical texts:
- **Matthew 19:30** - "the last shall be first" principle with Dan's placement
- **Acts 2:44** - early Christian community having "all things common"  
- **Psalm 47:4** - God choosing "our inheritance for us"
- **Jeremiah 23:15** - warning about profaneness spreading from Jerusalem

The commentary emphasizes that this vision represents the New Testament reality where "old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new," with the sanctuary at the center symbolizing how worship should be "the centre of their unity" for God's people.
