**David Smith: Collected Writings, Lectures, and Interviews**

This collection, edited by Susan J. Cooke and published by University of California Press in 2018, presents a comprehensive view of David Smith's artistic philosophy and approach to sculpture. The 312-page volume compiles Smith's lectures, interviews, and personal reflections, offering insight into one of America's most influential sculptors.

**Core Artistic Philosophy**

Smith's approach to art centered on what he called "telling it like it is" - an uncompromising commitment to authenticity in artistic expression. His philosophy emphasized materials, passion for creation, and sculpture above all else, representing what many consider the archetypal traditional artist mindset.

**Artist Identity and Creative Process**

Smith defined artistic identity as stemming from "inner confidence and certainty strengthened by constant struggle and a 'defensive belligerence' to all externally imposed rules." He believed this identity originated in the artist's visual-immediate world and expressed itself through "eclectic" images. This perspective reveals Smith's belief that authentic artistic expression requires resistance to external constraints and unwavering commitment to personal vision.

**The Artist's Life**

Throughout his lectures and personal musings, Smith acknowledged the inherent difficulties of the artistic life - a existence characterized by perpetual financial constraints, insufficient funds for materials or rent, and the constant struggle to balance creative demands with practical necessities. He seemed to view these challenges as almost genetically predetermined aspects of the artist's path, suggesting that such difficulties were inseparable from authentic creative practice.

**Authenticity and Materials**

Smith's writings demonstrate his profound commitment to materials and the physical process of creation. His approach emphasized direct engagement with sculptural materials and processes, viewing this hands-on relationship as fundamental to authentic artistic expression. This material-focused philosophy positioned him as an artist who prioritized the tangible, physical aspects of creation over conceptual or theoretical concerns.

**Existential Artistic Authenticity**

The collection reveals Smith's existential approach to art-making, where the act of creation becomes a form of authentic self-expression and struggle against imposed limitations. His "defensive belligerence" toward external rules reflects a broader philosophical stance that authentic art must emerge from the artist's immediate, visceral experience rather than academic or institutional frameworks.

**Creative Struggle as Identity**

Smith's writings suggest that the constant struggle inherent in artistic life wasn't merely an obstacle to overcome but a fundamental component of artistic identity. The perpetual tension between creative vision and practical limitations, the ongoing battle for time and resources, and the need to maintain artistic integrity despite external pressures all contributed to what Smith saw as the authentic artist's existence.

**Legacy and Influence**

The compilation demonstrates Smith's role in articulating a particular vision of the artist's relationship to materials, process, and society. His writings provide insight into mid-20th century American sculpture and the mindset that drove some of its most significant innovations. The collection serves as both historical document and continuing source of inspiration for contemporary artists grappling with similar challenges of authenticity, materials, and creative integrity.

**Practical Wisdom**

Beyond philosophical insights, Smith's collected works offer practical perspectives on maintaining artistic practice under difficult circumstances. His reflections on time management, resource allocation, and sustaining creative momentum while facing financial constraints provide valuable guidance for working artists navigating similar challenges.

# David Smith: Collected Writings, Lectures, and Interviews

**Editor:** Susan J. Cooke  
**Publisher:** University of California Press, 2018  
**Pages:** 312 pp. 28 color photographs, 11 b/w illustrations

## Smith's Philosophy on Art Criticism and History

David Smith held a deeply skeptical view of art historians and critics. In a 1960 speech, he declared: "There is no true art history, nor true appreciation." He expanded on this complaint, stating:

"We have all the anthropologists, philosophers, historians, connoisseurs and mercenaries, and everybody else telling us what art is or what it should be. But I think we ought to very simply let it be what art says it is. And what art says it is, you can see by their working. I would like to leave it just like that."

For Smith, only art could speak meaningfully about art—the work speaks for itself. He viewed critics, historians, and curators as distracting the public from the essence of art through "rhetorical tricks and gold-plated erudition," piling up what he considered irrelevancies like influence, biography, context, meaning, and stylistic analysis.

This stance had ethical dimensions for Smith. He believed art flowed from individuals asserting or expressing themselves, and that art historical discourse interfered with artists' fundamental right and obligation to express their identity. As he wrote in a 1955 essay: "The theory-laden historians' truth-based calculations of past ages have no connection with us."

## The Paradox of Smith's Anti-Intellectual Stance

Despite his harsh criticisms, Smith's actual behavior revealed significant contradictions. While teaching at Sarah Lawrence College in 1950, he created extensive reading lists for students, directing them to sympathetic bookstores and providing annotated bibliographies of books by and about artists including Hieronymus Bosch, Paul Klee, Pablo Picasso, and André Masson. Among his recommended readings were Giorgio Vasari's "Lives of the Artists" and Robert Goldwater and Marco Treves's "Artists on Art."

In a 1964 interview, Smith admitted: "I love to read art books. I want to know everything that has ever been known by any man." This interview, notably the book's longest and most generous conversation, was conducted with Thomas B. Hess, one of America's most prominent art critics at the time.

The collection reveals Smith engaged regularly with art world intellectuals, including conversations with dancer-turned-dance critic Marian Horosko and Frank O'Hara (working in his capacity as the Museum of Modern Art's assistant curator of painting and sculpture). Question-and-answer sessions following his talks demonstrate his willingness to discuss art historical and critical concerns, despite his publicly prickly stance.

Smith also maintained relationships with literary-minded painters like Robert Motherwell (founding editor of the "Documents in Modern Art" series) and Barnett Newman (regular contributor to mid-twentieth century magazines like The Tiger's Eye). Most tellingly, he appointed Clement Greenberg as an executor of his estate.

## Political and Philosophical Underpinnings

Smith's writings reveal his engagement with Marxist thought and left-wing politics. He assured interviewers he had read his Marx, and references to Marx, socialism, and unions appear throughout his writings, supporting what can be characterized as "left-wing formalism." This position, influenced by commentators like Greenberg in "Avant-garde and Kitsch" (1939) and Theodor Adorno in "Commitment" (1962), positioned art's autonomy as protection against capitalism's attempts to manage imagination.

Smith's philosophy linked him to high Romanticism's commitment to art for the sake of a way of life. "Liberty—or freedom—of our position is the greatest thing we've got," he told David Sylvester in 1960, echoing mid-twentieth century equations of radical abstraction with autonomy.

## Artistic Practice and Masculinity

The collection explicitly displays the masculinity underpinning mid-twentieth century formalism. Smith took pride in welding his own work, expressing greater appreciation for Julio González (who did much of Picasso's welding) than for Picasso himself. His sculptures became progressively bigger and heavier throughout his career.

Smith discussed scaling up his practice by building works on train cars. To Hess, he revealed his ultimate aspiration: "I've sat on those goddamn 4-8-4s welding them up, hoping that I could someday make sculptures as big as that." The "4-8-4" designation refers to massive steam locomotives weighing close to 60 tons that powered passenger trains across North America at speeds approaching or exceeding 100 miles per hour—representing the ultimate expression of industrial power and masculine authority.

## Teaching Philosophy and Student Guidance

During his tenure at Sarah Lawrence College, Smith developed comprehensive educational materials. His typescript for students included:
- Directions to sympathetic bookstores
- Annotated bibliographies of essential art books
- Lists of "untold numbers of books you should have or should read"
- Focus on artists who wrote about their own work

This pedagogical approach reflected his belief that students should learn directly from artists' own accounts rather than through secondary interpretation.

## Theoretical Connections and Influences

Smith's thought anticipated aspects of Donald Judd's 1960s writing. Both artists developed sculptural practices from painting backgrounds and viewed painting and sculpture as unified disciplines. However, Smith's insistence on overcoming the paradox by which art never escapes instrumentalization but should, connected him more to art-for-art's-sake traditions than to late Modernism's tautological approaches.

His position embodied what Jacques Derrida termed the "pharmakon"—writing as "both remedy and poison." Smith's dismissals of attempts to write about art exemplified this contradiction: protecting art from discourse by participating in the very discourse he criticized.

## Editorial Approach and Historical Context

Editor Susan J. Cooke, as Associate Director of the Estate of David Smith, utilized superior archival access to improve upon earlier collections by Cleve Gray and Garnett McCoy from forty-five and fifty years prior. Rather than attempting exhaustive compilation, Cooke selected representative samples across Smith's three decades as a mature writer.

The chronological arrangement demonstrates Smith's efforts to make his writing engaging, avoiding the tendency of artists' notes to drift into ineffective poetry. His interests in sincerity and monumentality continue to resonate, as evidenced by his show at Yorkshire Sculpture Park in late 2019.

## Contemporary Relevance

Smith's insistence on art as the site of liberty, while potentially appearing as ideological capture, reflects his era's convictions as much as our current belief that spectacle inevitably subsumes culture. His assertion that art should speak through work rather than reducing to passive echoing of historical spirit remains relevant to contemporary debates about artistic autonomy and critical discourse.

The collection contributes significantly to understanding the 1950s and 1960s art world, particularly the persistent tensions beneath seemingly calm periods. Smith's writings illuminate the complex relationship between artistic practice and intellectual discourse that continues to shape contemporary art world dynamics.

**David Smith: Collected Writings, Lectures, and Interviews**
*Edited by Susan J. Cooke*

This comprehensive volume presents the brilliant, iconoclastic American sculptor David Smith through his own words, offering intimate access to an artist's life and creative philosophy through collected writings, lectures, and interviews spanning from the 1930s to 1960s.

**Key Insights on Artistic Identity**

Smith defined artistic identity as "an inner confidence and certitude strengthened by constant struggle and a 'defensive belligerence' to all externally imposed rules." This identity originated in the artist's visual-perceptual responses to their immediate world and expressed itself through "eidetic" images.

**The Artist's Social Role and Struggles**

Smith embodied the archetypal traditional artist, living with perpetual difficulties: insufficient funds for materials or rent, never enough time for creative work, and constant conflict with conservative society attempting to suppress artistic expression. For sculptors specifically, the use of dangerous materials, chemicals, and industrial processes added additional hazards.

Smith articulated the paradox of artistic necessity: "There is a need for art. The artist has a social obligation, as well as his own ego satisfaction to produce to the fullest extent of his ability. It is society's duty to make the effort to understand before it takes an active prejudice... To the majority there is no need for art on the basis that creative art is produced. We live in a hypocritical world."

**Art, Science, and Technology Integration**

Notably prescient for the 1940s, Smith championed the symbiotic relationship between art, science, and technology: "Art cannot be divorced from its time, place or science. It has never been dependent on but has always been related to science... In the creative sense art has been related to science, but the inverse is likewise true." He supported this philosophy with concrete examples throughout his work.

**Book Structure and Content**

The volume features an informative introduction by editor Susan Cooke, followed by three chronological sections of Smith's writings: Thirties & Forties, Fifties, and Sixties. Additional materials include a comprehensive chronology, excellent index, and illustrations featuring scans from Smith's notebooks and sketchbooks alongside photographs of finished sculptures.

From his 1947 Skidmore College lectures, Smith challenged aspiring artists: "So, you the artist—if you are an inspired mind, if you feel that you can express something that has not been expressed before, if you are willing to lay yourself open to opprobrium and tough sledding in a wealthy country with a narrow..."

Smith's commitment to materials, passion for sculpture above all else, and existential artistic authenticity defined his practice. His words reveal an artist willing to endure hardship for creative expression, embodying the struggles and dedication required for authentic artistic life.

This collection serves as an invaluable resource for understanding one of America's most important sculptors, whose life ended tragically in a car crash, possibly before receiving full recognition for his contributions to modern sculpture and artistic philosophy.
